How a property claim moves through appraisal in the Mountain State

Appraisal is a valuation tool. It is built into most homeowners, farm, landlord and commercial property policies, and it exists for one situation: the insurer agrees there is a covered loss, but the two sides cannot agree on what that loss is worth. This guide walks through the insurance appraisal process West Virginia policyholders usually encounter, from the first disagreement to the check. Your policy’s wording controls, so keep a copy beside you as you read and check each step against it.

Nothing here is legal advice. If your claim involves a denial, a coverage fight or questions about how the insurer handled it, talk to a West Virginia attorney before you rely on appraisal alone.

Measure the gap

Before anyone demands anything, figure out how far apart you really are. Lay the insurer’s estimate next to your contractor’s bid and look for the reasons, not just the totals. Gaps usually come from a short list of sources:

  • Scope: rooms, roof slopes or siding elevations left off entirely.
  • Quantity: measurements that are too small, or waste factors that are too thin.
  • Pricing: unit costs that do not reflect what crews charge in your county.
  • Method: repair where replacement is needed, or patching where matching fails.

Write the gap down by category. A short, specific list often produces a supplement without appraisal, and if it does not, that list becomes the backbone of everything that follows. A claim estimate review can help you sort genuine shortfalls from items that are simply priced differently.

Serve the written demand

Most appraisal clauses let either side start the process with a written demand. Read the clause first: some require a sworn proof of loss, a specific notice method or a statement of the amount you claim. A clear demand usually includes:

  • Your name, the property address, the policy number and the claim number.
  • A plain statement that you disagree with the insurer on the amount of loss.
  • A reference to the appraisal provision by its policy heading.
  • The name of your appraiser, or a commitment to name one within the policy’s window.

Send it in a way you can prove later, such as certified mail plus email, and keep copies. The claim timeline planner can help you track the dates your own policy sets.

Select your appraiser

Each party picks its own appraiser. Policies commonly call for someone “competent” and “disinterested” (or “independent”), and some insurers will challenge an appraiser who has a financial stake in the repair. That is one reason many owners avoid naming the contractor who will do the work.

What to ask a prospective appraiser

  • How is the fee calculated, and will you put it in writing?
  • Have you worked claims involving this type of damage and building?
  • Can you get to the property, including a cabin up a gravel road or a house in a narrow hollow?

The insurer names its appraiser too. Do not assume that person will be hostile; many appraisals settle because two practical people sat down with the same measurements.

Agree on an umpire

The two appraisers then choose an umpire, a neutral third person who only steps in if they deadlock. Picking one early is wise: an umpire who is already in place keeps the process moving if talks stall. If the appraisers cannot agree on a name within the time the policy allows, the clause typically lets either side ask a court in the county where the property sits to appoint one. Local familiarity matters here. An umpire who understands how a Southern Coalfields roof job is priced compared with one in the Eastern Panhandle will read the evidence more accurately.

Joint inspection and estimates

Now the appraisers go to work. Many walk the property together so they measure the same walls and count the same shingles. Each builds or refines an estimate, usually line by line in software such as Xactimate, and they exchange them.

Your job at this stage is to make the evidence easy to find. Gather photos from the day of the loss, contractor bids, invoices for emergency work, receipts for temporary housing and anything showing the condition of the property before the storm. Owners in older river-town houses should keep any records of plaster, trim or slate that would be costly to match.

Line-by-line reconciliation

This is where most of the money moves. The appraisers go down both estimates and agree on as many items as they can: a quantity here, a unit price there, whether a wall needs paint or a full replacement of drywall. Items that remain in dispute are listed, and each appraiser sends the umpire a position with supporting documents.

The umpire may reinspect, ask for more documentation or hold an informal meeting. The umpire then decides only the items that are still open. Plenty of West Virginia appraisals finish here with an agreed figure and never need the umpire to rule at all.

Who paysMost policies have each side pay its own appraiser and split the umpire’s fee. Check your clause, and get every fee arrangement in writing before work starts.

Award, payment and follow-through

The award is a written document stating the amount of loss. It usually becomes effective once any two of the three panel members sign it. A useful award spells out replacement cost, depreciation and actual cash value, and it separates the dwelling, other structures, contents and loss of use where those are in dispute.

The insurer then pays according to the policy: the award, less the deductible and earlier payments, with any recoverable depreciation typically released once repairs are done and documented. Keep track of what is still owed, and use the RCV, ACV and holdback calculator to check the math. If payment stalls after an award, that is a question for a West Virginia attorney.

Common missteps to avoid

  • Demanding appraisal on a denial. If the insurer says the loss is not covered at all, the panel usually cannot fix that.
  • Letting the suit deadline slide. Appraisal may not pause the time limit to sue in your policy. Ask an attorney how it applies to you.
  • Arriving without documentation. An appraiser can only argue for what can be shown.
  • Ignoring flood or earth movement exclusions. Water from a rising creek or a slipping hillside raises coverage questions that appraisal does not answer.
  • Cashing checks without reading the cover letter. Make sure a payment is not described as final before you deposit it, and ask counsel if it is.
