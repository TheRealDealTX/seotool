How to Document Storm Damage for Insurance in WV

Good records are the cheapest way to strengthen a claim. This checklist covers what to capture in the first hours, days and weeks after a West Virginia storm.

Why records decide disputes

An estimate is only as strong as the evidence behind it. When an adjuster, an appraiser or an umpire looks at your claim, they are asking the same thing: can the damage be shown? Owners who document storm damage for insurance in WV carefully, from the first hour onward, usually have an easier time getting a fair number, whether the claim settles normally or ends up in appraisal.

West Virginia storms bring their own complications. Downed trees block roads for days, power can be out long enough to spoil food and freeze pipes, and many homes sit on slopes where the roof is hard to see from the ground. The checklist below is built around those realities.

The first hours: safety, then pictures

Stay away from downed lines and unstable trees. Once it is safe, start recording before anything is moved.

  • Wide shots first. Photograph each side of the house, outbuildings and the yard so the overall picture is clear.
  • Then close-ups. Capture each area of damage with something for scale, like a tape measure or a coin.
  • Video walk-through. Narrate as you walk room by room, naming each space and pointing out water stains, broken glass or ceiling sags.
  • Time stamps. Keep phone settings that record date and location, and do not edit or filter the originals.

If a tree is on the house, photograph it in place before removal. Note the species if you know it and whether it was healthy, since insurers sometimes ask whether a tree was already dead. If the tree service cuts it up, ask them to leave the stump and a section of trunk until the adjuster has seen it.

The first days: mitigation and receipts

Most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. That might mean tarping a roof, boarding a window, running fans or pulling soaked carpet.

Keep a paper trail

  • Save every receipt for tarps, lumber, fuel for a generator and rented equipment.
  • Get written invoices from tree services and water mitigation companies.
  • Keep a log of your own hours spent on emergency work.
  • Photograph damaged items before you throw them away, and keep a sample of flooring or carpet if you can.

Track extra living costs

If you cannot stay in the house, keep hotel bills, restaurant receipts and mileage records. Additional living expense coverage generally pays costs above what you would normally spend, so a simple comparison with your usual budget helps.

The first weeks: measurements and condition records

Once the immediate crisis passes, build the detailed record that an estimate depends on.

  • Measure rooms. Length, width and ceiling height for every affected room.
  • Roof details. Note the material, number of layers if known, and whether it is shingle, metal or slate. The roof square and pitch estimator can give you a rough size to compare with the insurer’s figures.
  • Pre-loss condition. Gather older photos, listing photos, inspection reports or receipts for a recent roof, siding or kitchen.
  • Contents list. Write down damaged belongings with brand, approximate age and where you bought them.
A habit worth starting nowWalk around your home once a year with your phone recording video. A dated baseline makes it much easier to show what changed after a derecho, ice storm or heavy snow.

Storm types and what to watch for

Wind and derechos

The June 2012 derecho showed how much tree damage straight-line winds can cause in a heavily forested state. Look for lifted shingle tabs, loose metal panels, damaged gutters and siding scuffs from flying debris. Our derecho, wind and tornado claim page has more on these losses.

Snow and ice

Heavy wet snow, like the October 2012 storm that dropped feet of it in the central mountains, can sag or collapse roofs. Ice storms bring down limbs and cause ice dams. Photograph ceiling stains, cracked rafters and bowed ridgelines.

Hail

Hail is less frequent here than on the plains, but it does hit the Ohio Valley and Eastern Panhandle. Look at soft metals such as vents and gutters, and at window screens and siding, where dents or splatter marks show up clearly.

Heavy rain and remnant storms

Remnant tropical systems can dump heavy rain. Keep in mind that rising floodwater is typically excluded from homeowners policies and handled under separate flood insurance, so note how water entered the building.

Organizing it all

Create one folder, physical or digital, with subfolders for photos, receipts, letters from the insurer, estimates and your notes. Keep a simple log of every call: date, name, what was said. Back up the digital files somewhere other than your phone, since phones get lost, dropped or replaced in the middle of long claims. When the insurer sends a new estimate or letter, add it to the folder the same day and note any change from the last version. If the claim stalls, that folder becomes the foundation for a supplement request or an appraisal. A claim estimate review can show where your documentation already supports a higher number and where gaps remain.
