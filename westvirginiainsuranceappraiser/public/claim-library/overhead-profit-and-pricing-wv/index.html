Overhead and Profit on an Insurance Claim: Rural WV Pricing

Contractor overhead and profit, plus the real cost of getting crews and materials up a West Virginia hollow, are frequent sources of estimate gaps.

Two pricing questions that come up again and again

When a contractor’s bid and an insurer’s estimate are far apart, the difference often traces back to two things. The first is whether the estimate includes general contractor overhead and profit. The second is whether the unit prices reflect what it actually costs to do the work where the house sits. In West Virginia, with its narrow roads, long drives and small local crews, both questions come up constantly on an overhead and profit insurance claim.

This guide explains common practice. Policy wording and the facts of each claim matter, so treat this as a starting point.

What overhead and profit means

Overhead covers a contractor’s business costs that are not tied to one line item: insurance, office staff, vehicles, licensing, project supervision and scheduling. Profit is the margin that keeps the business running. Together, they are often shown as a percentage added to the repair cost, sometimes described in the industry as “10 and 10,” although actual rates vary.

When it is usually included

  • The repair needs several trades, such as roofing, drywall, painting and electrical, that a general contractor would reasonably coordinate.
  • The project is complex enough that a reasonable owner would hire a general contractor.
  • Your policy pays replacement cost and does not specifically exclude these charges.

When insurers push back

  • Only one or two trades are involved.
  • The insurer argues a general contractor is not needed.
  • The claim is paid on an ACV basis and the insurer withholds O&P until work is done.

Whether O&P is owed on a particular claim can depend on the policy and how West Virginia courts treat the issue. If the dollar amount is large, it may be worth asking a West Virginia attorney.

Why rural West Virginia pricing looks different

Estimating software uses regional price lists, and those lists often reflect conditions in larger metro areas. That can miss the costs of doing work in much of the state.

Travel and mobilization

A roofer based in Charleston who takes a job in a Southern Coalfields hollow or on a ridge in the Potomac Highlands may spend hours each day driving. Those trip charges, lodging and fuel are real costs, and estimates sometimes leave them out.

Small crews and limited competition

Many rural areas have only a handful of contractors. Small crews take longer on large jobs, and after a widespread event they may be booked for months. Demand surges after storms like the 2012 derecho can push prices up across a region.

Material delivery

Supply trucks cannot always reach a house at the end of a one-lane gravel road. Materials may need to be off-loaded at the paved road and shuttled up in smaller loads, or carried by hand. Delivery fees, equipment rental and extra handling labor should be accounted for when they genuinely apply.

Steep lots and access

Hillside homes may need scaffolding, extra safety equipment or special lifts. Steep roof pitch, which is common on older homes and mountain cabins, adds labor and safety charges. Disposal can cost more too, since the nearest landfill or transfer station may be a long haul away.

An illustrative examplePicture an estimate of $24,000 for siding and roofing on a home in a narrow hollow. As an illustrative case only, adding reasonable travel, a material shuttle and 20 percent O&P might bring a supportable figure closer to $32,000. Real numbers depend on the job and need documentation.

The point is not to inflate a claim. It is to make sure the estimate reflects the real cost of putting a crew and materials on that particular site, which a panel can weigh against the insurer’s figures.

How to support your pricing

  • Get written bids from contractors who actually work in your area.
  • Ask them to list travel, delivery and access charges as separate lines.
  • Photograph the road, driveway and slope to show access problems.
  • Keep supplier quotes that show delivery fees or minimums.
  • Compare the insurer’s line items with your bids to find exactly where prices differ.

It also helps to ask the adjuster which price list and date the estimate used. Price lists are updated regularly, and an estimate written right after a storm may not reflect the surge in labor costs that followed. If the insurer’s figures rely on a contractor it recommends, ask whether that contractor will actually travel to your property at those prices and on what schedule.

The claim gap and fee estimator can help you decide whether a pricing gap is large enough to pursue.

When pricing disputes go to appraisal

Pricing and O&P disputes are about amount, which makes them a natural fit for appraisal once coverage is accepted. An appraiser familiar with local conditions can explain why a job in a Greenbrier Valley hollow does not cost the same as one in a Morgantown subdivision. If your estimate seems to ignore those realities, our claim estimate review is a sensible first step, and the Beckley service area page describes our coverage across southern counties.
