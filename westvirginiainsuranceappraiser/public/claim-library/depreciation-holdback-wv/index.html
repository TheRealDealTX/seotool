Recoverable Depreciation in West Virginia: Getting the Holdback Paid

The first insurance check is often smaller than the repair bid because of depreciation. Here is how the withheld amount usually works and how to collect it.

Why the first check comes in short

Many West Virginia owners open their first claim payment and find it well below the contractor’s quote. Often the reason is not a mistake but a policy design: the insurer has paid actual cash value now and is holding back depreciation until repairs are done. Understanding recoverable depreciation in West Virginia claims can turn that frustration into a plan.

Policies differ, so read your loss settlement section closely. What follows describes common patterns, not guarantees.

Three numbers that matter

  • Replacement cost value (RCV): the cost to repair or replace with like kind and quality today.
  • Depreciation: a reduction for age, wear and condition.
  • Actual cash value (ACV): RCV minus depreciation.

On a replacement cost policy, the insurer commonly pays ACV first, minus the deductible. The depreciation amount is the holdback. If it is labeled recoverable, you can usually collect it once the work is completed and documented.

An illustrative exampleSay a roof is valued at $20,000 RCV with $7,000 of depreciation and a $1,000 deductible. In this illustrative case, the first payment would be $12,000, and up to $7,000 more might be released after replacement. Your figures will differ. Try the RCV, ACV and holdback calculator with your own estimate.

Recoverable or not?

Not all depreciation comes back. Whether it does depends on the policy:

  • A replacement cost policy usually makes depreciation recoverable on completed repairs.
  • An actual cash value policy, sometimes found on older homes, rental property or manufactured homes, may never pay it back.
  • Some roof endorsements switch roofs past a certain age to ACV or to a payment schedule.

Check your declarations page and any roof endorsements. If the estimate labels depreciation as non-recoverable and you believe your policy says otherwise, raise it in writing.

Manufactured homes deserve a special mention. They are common across the state, and their policies are often written on different forms with their own settlement terms. Some pay replacement cost, some pay actual cash value and some use a schedule. Read the form itself rather than assuming it works like a standard homeowners policy, and ask the agent for a copy if you only have the declarations page.

How depreciation gets calculated, and argued

Insurers usually depreciate line by line based on an assumed life expectancy and the item’s age and condition. Disputes often come from:

  • Age assumptions. A roof replaced eight years ago treated as twenty years old.
  • Condition ratings. A well-kept metal roof on a Greenbrier Valley farmhouse rated as worn.
  • Labor depreciation. Some estimates depreciate labor as well as materials; how that is treated can depend on the policy and the law, so ask an attorney if it is a large factor.
  • Items that should not depreciate much. Debris removal or permits sometimes get depreciated without a clear reason.

Receipts, permits and dated photos showing the age and upkeep of the item are your best tools. If the gap remains, depreciation is a valuation question that an appraisal panel can often address.

Questions to put to the adjuster

  • What life expectancy did you assign to each major item, and where does that figure come from?
  • What age did you use, and what records support it?
  • Which line items carry depreciation on labor, and why?
  • Is any of the depreciation listed as non-recoverable, and under what policy language?

Asking these in writing often produces either a corrected estimate or a clear statement of the insurer’s reasoning, and either one helps you decide what to do next.

Collecting the holdback

Finish and document the work

Most insurers want proof that repairs are complete: a final invoice, photos of the finished work and sometimes a certificate of completion. Keep everything from the contractor, including change orders.

Watch the timing

Many policies set a period within which repairs must be completed to recover depreciation. Long waits for contractors after big events, such as the 2016 floods or the 2021 ice storm, can strain those windows. If you need more time, ask the insurer in writing and keep the response.

Ask for the release in writing

Send the documents with a short letter requesting the recoverable depreciation, and follow up if you do not hear back. Keep copies of everything.

If the repair cost more than the estimate

Hidden damage often turns up once work begins, such as soft roof decking or wet insulation behind a wall. Tell the insurer before or as soon as you find it, send photos and the contractor’s change order, and ask for a supplement. The final invoice may then support both a larger replacement cost and a larger depreciation release.

When the holdback is part of an appraisal

If you invoke appraisal, ask that the award separate RCV, depreciation and ACV for each disputed category. That makes it clear how much is payable now and how much is released when repairs are done.
