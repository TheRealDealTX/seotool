Where the money goes missing

The gap is usually in the line items, not the headline

Most short claims aren’t denials. They’re estimates with a slope left off, ice & water shield priced at the eaves only, a ceiling painted but not the room, or overhead and profit left out. A New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser finds those lines, measures them and prices them so they hold up in front of an umpire.

How it works

The appraisal clause, one step at a time

The appraisal clause swaps an open-ended argument for a defined process. Here’s how New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser moves a file through it.

  1. 1

    Read the file

    Policy, declarations, the insurer’s estimate and your photos — then a plain answer on whether appraisal fits.

  2. 2

    Re-scope the loss

    Our own inspection and measurements, priced line by line for your part of New Hampshire.

  3. 3

    Invoke the clause in writing

    Appraisal is invoked under your policy, and your appraiser is named within its time limits.

  4. 4

    Reconcile line by line

    Line-by-line work with the insurer’s appraiser. Most of the gap is usually settled here.

  5. 5

    Two signatures, one award

    Anything left goes to a neutral umpire. Two signatures set the amount of loss.

Regional knowledge

From the Seacoast to the North Country

Salt wind on the Seacoast, frozen camps on Winnipesaukee, snow-loaded condos below Mount Washington. A New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser has to know how each one shows up in an estimate.

Seacoast

Eighteen miles of coastline, the Piscataqua River and Great Bay. Older homes in Portsmouth’s South End and Exeter meet newer subdivisions inland, and nor’easters test both.

  • Nor’easter wind
  • Wind-driven rain
  • Salt-air wear disputes
  • Historic homes

Merrimack Valley

The state’s population center. Summer storms track across Hillsborough and Merrimack counties, and multi-unit buildings turn one frozen pipe into several claims.

  • Microbursts & hail
  • Burst pipes in multifamily
  • Tree fall
  • Mill-condo losses

Lakes Region

Lake Winnipesaukee’s shoreline is lined with seasonal camps that sit empty for months. Freeze-ups found in spring and ice damage to waterfront structures lead the claim list.

  • Vacant-camp freeze-ups
  • Ice dams
  • Docks & boathouses
  • Ice storms

Monadnock & Upper Valley

Brick downtowns, river valleys and college-town rentals. Heavy wet snow and river flooding have both left their mark on Cheshire and Grafton counties.

  • River flooding
  • Snow load
  • Rental & campus housing
  • Downed hardwoods

White Mountains & North Country

Elevation, deep snowpack and steep valleys. Carroll and Coös county claims often involve condominium associations, snow-load framing damage and storm runoff.

  • Roof snow load
  • Ski-condo HOA claims
  • Ice storms
  • Flash flooding

On the record

The storms behind today’s claim arguments

Older storms set the patterns adjusters and appraisers still argue about: what counts as wind versus flood, how ice damage is scoped, when a tree-on-roof claim includes the tree. A New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser reads every file with that history in mind.

  1. 1938

    The Great Hurricane of ’38

    Still the benchmark for wind damage in the state — a reminder that inland New Hampshire can see hurricane-force gusts.

  2. 1998

    North Country ice storm

    Ice-loaded limbs across the North Country and Lakes Region. Tree-fall and roof-impact claims dominated.

  3. 2006

    Mother’s Day floods

    Days of rain swelled rivers in the Lakes Region and Seacoast, separating flood-policy losses from homeowners claims.

  4. 2008

    Deerfield tornado & December ice storm

    A rare EF2 tornado in July, then an ice storm that left hundreds of thousands without power in December.

  5. 2011

    Tropical Storm Irene & “Snowtober”

    Irene’s flooding tore through White Mountain valleys; an October snowstorm then dropped heavy snow on trees still in leaf.

  6. 2015

    Record late-winter snowpack

    Weeks of accumulated snow led to ice dams, interior leaks and roof-load damage across southern New Hampshire.

  7. 2017

    Halloween-eve windstorm

    One of the largest power outages in state history and a wave of tree-on-house claims.

  8. 2023

    December 18 wind and rain

    Damaging gusts, heavy rain on frozen ground and river flooding, just before the holidays.

Right help, first time

Who should you call about a short claim?

Each professional solves a different problem. New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser will tell you when appraisal isn’t the right tool.

Hire an appraiser when…

Coverage accepted, amount disputed.

  • Values the amount of loss
  • Works inside the appraisal clause
  • Result: an award signed by two of three

Hire a public adjuster when…

You want the whole claim handled.

  • Prepares and negotiates the claim
  • Usually best early on
  • Result: a negotiated settlement

Call an attorney when…

Denials, coverage fights, deadlines.

  • Advises on legal rights
  • Handles litigation
  • Result: settlement or judgment

FAQ

Plain answers from a New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser

Short answers about how appraisal works, what it costs and when it’s the wrong move.

All questions Claim glossary
What does a New Hampshire Insurance Appraiser do?

We value property damage when you and your insurer agree the loss is covered but disagree on what it costs. As your appointed appraiser we inspect, build an independent line-item estimate, work through the differences with the insurer’s appraiser and, if needed, present open items to a neutral umpire.

Is an appraiser the same as a public adjuster?

No. A public adjuster handles and negotiates the whole claim. An appraiser works inside the policy’s appraisal clause on one question — the amount of loss. We don’t negotiate outside appraisal and we don’t give legal advice.

When does it make sense to call?

When coverage has been accepted, you have the insurer’s itemized estimate, and the gap to your contractor’s number is meaningful — often more than about 10% or several thousand dollars.

Is the award binding?

On the amount of loss, generally yes, once two of the three panel members sign. That’s why the file should be complete before appraisal is demanded.

What does it cost?

Each side usually pays its own appraiser and half of the umpire. We put our fee in writing before any work starts, and the first conversation is free.

Do you cover my town?

Yes. We work in all 10 New Hampshire counties, from Seabrook to Pittsburg.
