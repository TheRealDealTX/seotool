Roof Types for Insurance: How Texas Insurers See Your Roof

Insurers price your roof by its material, shape, age, and impact rating. Here is how common roof types for insurance compare in Texas, and what to document to earn a discount.

Updated October 7, 20268 min readBy Stone Coated Roofs

Why roof types for insurance matter so much in Texas

When you shop for homeowners coverage, the roof is one of the first things an underwriter looks at. Roof types for insurance are judged by material, impact rating, shape, and age, and in Texas, where hail and wind drive a large share of property claims, those factors can affect your premium, your deductible structure, and even whether a carrier will write the policy. This guide explains how insurers typically view common roof types, what documentation matters, and which upgrades tend to pay off. It is general information, not insurance advice; your carrier's underwriting guidelines are what actually apply.

Stone Coated Roofs installs Class 4 stone coated steel roofing across Texas, and homeowners regularly ask us how a new roof will change their insurance. The honest answer is that it varies by carrier, but the patterns below are consistent enough to plan around. Whether you call them insurance roof types or roof types for home insurance, the underlying questions are the same: how likely is this roof to produce a claim, and how expensive would that claim be?

What UL 2218 and a Class 4 rating mean

UL 2218 is the standard most insurers reference for impact resistance. In the test, steel balls of increasing size are dropped onto the same spot of a roof sample. Class 1 is the lowest rating and Class 4, which uses a 2-inch steel ball, is the highest. A product earns the class only if the covering shows no cracks or openings after the impacts.

The UL 2218 impact resistance Class 4 rating is the reason many Texas carriers offer premium credits, because it is a clear, third-party benchmark. Keep in mind that the rating applies to the specific product, not to a material category. Two metal roofs or two shingle lines can carry different ratings. Our page on Class 4 stone coated roofing explains how stone coated steel performs in that test and in real hail.

The Texas Class 4 roof certificate

To get a discount, you usually have to prove what is on your roof. A Texas Class 4 roof certificate is not one universal state form; it is the set of documents your carrier accepts to confirm a Class 4 roof was installed. Expect to provide some combination of:

  • Product documentation showing the exact manufacturer and product line and its UL 2218 Class 4 listing.
  • A certificate of installation signed by the roofing contractor, often listing the address, installation date, and product.
  • The final invoice with material details.
  • The manufacturer warranty or registration.
  • Your carrier's own form, if it uses one.

Ask your agent which documents they need before the job starts, so your installer can prepare them at completion. Stone Coated Roofs provides the product and installation documentation with every Class 4 roof we install.

Does a metal roof lower your insurance?

Often, yes, but not because it is metal. Discounts are usually tied to the Class 4 rating, so a Class 4 stone coated steel or standing seam roof and a Class 4 shingle may earn the same credit with some carriers. In Texas, Class 4 discounts commonly range from about 5% to 25% depending on the carrier. We do not quote any specific insurer's numbers, so confirm with your own.

The bigger long-term difference is what happens after several storms. An impact-resistant shingle discount applies to a roof that still ages like asphalt, and asphalt shingle roofs in Texas typically last 12 to 18 years. Stone coated steel carries a planning lifespan of 40 to 70 years with manufacturer warranties from 50 years to lifetime. Our stone coated vs impact-resistant shingles comparison walks through that tradeoff, and the insurance discount estimator lets you model potential savings on your own premium.

Roof shapes for insurance: hip vs gable and more

Insurers also consider roof geometry, particularly for wind. Roof shapes for insurance are usually grouped like this:

Roof shapeWind performanceTypical insurer view
HipStrong: slopes on all sides, no tall gable wallGenerally favorable; may earn wind credits in some programs
GableWeaker at gable ends and overhangs under high windStandard; bracing gable ends can help
Complex (multiple hips, valleys, dormers)Varies; more seams and valleysNeutral; more potential leak points
Flat or low-slopeWind uplift at edges; drainage concernsOften higher risk for water claims
Mansard or gambrelVaries; steep sides can catch windCase by case

Hip shapes tend to get the most credit in coastal wind programs. Inland, shape usually matters less than material and age. For coastal homes, see wind-resistant roofing in Texas.

Why flat roofs are more expensive to insure

Flat and low-slope roofs drain slowly, so water sits longer on seams and penetrations. Ponding, membrane shrinkage, and flashing failures lead to leaks that can go unnoticed until interior damage appears. Many membranes also have shorter service lives than steep-slope materials, and they are harder for an underwriter to evaluate from photos. The result is a higher expected rate of water claims, which can show up in pricing or eligibility.

Roof age, ACV vs RCV, and cosmetic damage exclusions

Material is only part of the picture. Three other factors often have a bigger effect on what a claim pays.

Roof age

Insurers weigh roof age heavily. Older roofs may face higher premiums, roof-specific deductibles, or a switch to actual cash value coverage for the roof. Some carriers decline to write homes with roofs past a certain age. This is why a new roof insurance discount, or simply a better rate, is common after replacement. Keep your installation paperwork, because the installation date is what underwriters use.

ACV vs RCV

  • Replacement cost value (RCV) pays to replace the damaged roof with like kind and quality, minus your deductible. Carriers often pay in two parts, with depreciation released after the work is done.
  • Actual cash value (ACV) pays replacement cost minus depreciation. On an older roof, depreciation can take a large share of the payout, leaving the homeowner to cover much of the new roof.

Many Texas policies apply ACV to roofs past a certain age, or use a roof payment schedule that reduces coverage each year. Check your declarations page and endorsements for the exact terms.

Cosmetic damage exclusions

Some policies exclude cosmetic damage to roofs, meaning dents or marks that do not affect the roof's ability to shed water. These endorsements are most often applied to metal roofs. With a cosmetic exclusion, a carrier may decline to pay for hail dents on a metal roof that still functions. Stone coated steel's textured granular surface tends to hide minor marks better than smooth metal, but large hail can still leave dents. Read any cosmetic exclusion closely, and ask your agent whether it applies to your roof type before you sign.

What underwriters ask about your roof

When you apply for a policy or renew, the insurer may pull aerial imagery, review prior claims, or send an inspector. The questions they are trying to answer tend to be the same:

  • What is the covering? Material and product line, and whether it carries a Class 4 rating.
  • How old is it? Usually taken from permit records, invoices, or your own disclosure.
  • What condition is it in? Missing shingles, granule loss, staining, moss, patched areas, or visible hail marks.
  • What is the shape and pitch? Hip or gable, steep or low slope, and how complex the layout is.
  • What is the claim history? Prior hail or wind claims on the property can affect pricing regardless of the current roof.
  • Is there more than one layer? Roofs installed over an existing layer are often viewed less favorably.

Having clear answers ready, backed by documents, makes underwriting smoother and reduces the chance of a surprise roof inspection notice after the policy is bound. If an inspection report flags your roof, ask for the specific items cited. Sometimes a targeted repair resolves the issue; other times, especially with an older asphalt roof that has seen several Texas hail seasons, replacement is the more economical path.

Weight can matter too when you switch materials. Moving from asphalt to concrete or clay tile adds a large load to the framing, while stone coated steel weighs about 2.4 pounds per square foot, compared with roughly 9 to 10 for concrete tile and 8 to 12 for clay. The roof weight calculator shows the difference for your roof size.

Types of roofs for insurance purposes: what to do next

  1. Pull your declarations page and look for roof age schedules, ACV terms, cosmetic exclusions, and your wind and hail deductible.
  2. Call your agent and ask what discount, if any, applies to a Class 4 roof, and what documentation they require.
  3. Compare options on lifetime cost, not just upfront price. Stone coated steel installed in Texas runs about $10–$18 per square foot as a planning range, not a quote.
  4. Plan the paperwork so your installer delivers the certificate and product documentation at completion.

    FAQ

    Frequently asked questions

    Does a metal roof lower your insurance?

    It can. Many Texas carriers offer a premium credit for roofs rated Class 4 under UL 2218, and many metal and stone coated steel systems carry that rating. The discount depends on the carrier and the documentation you provide, so ask your insurer before you buy.

    What is a Texas Class 4 roof certificate?

    It is documentation showing that your roof covering is rated Class 4 for impact resistance under UL 2218 and was installed on your home. Carriers typically want the product listing or manufacturer certificate plus an installer-signed certificate of installation, and some use their own forms.

    Why are flat roofs more expensive to insure?

    Flat and low-slope roofs drain more slowly, are prone to ponding and seam leaks, and are harder to inspect. Many membranes also have shorter service lives than steep-slope materials. Insurers see higher odds of water claims, which can show up in rates or eligibility.

    Is a hip roof or gable roof better for insurance?

    Hip roofs generally perform better in high winds because all four sides slope and there is no tall flat gable end to catch wind. Some insurers and wind mitigation programs give credit for hip shapes, particularly in coastal areas, but practices vary by carrier.

    Do impact-resistant shingles get the same discount as metal?

    Often the discount is tied to the Class 4 rating rather than the material, so a Class 4 shingle and a Class 4 stone coated steel roof may receive the same credit with some carriers. The difference is in durability over time and how each holds up after repeated hail.

    Does a new roof lower my insurance?

    A new roof can reduce premiums or improve coverage terms because insurers weigh roof age heavily. Some carriers offer a new roof insurance discount, and a newer roof may also keep you on replacement cost coverage rather than actual cash value.
