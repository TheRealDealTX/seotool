How to Distress Painted Furniture for a Timeworn Look

July 2, 20264 min readBy the Oh, Mother Mine DIY team
DifficultyEasy
Time1–3 hours (plus painting time)
Cost$10–$25

What you'll need

  • A painted piece of furniture (chalk paint works best)
  • Sanding sponges or sandpaper in 120 and 220 grit
  • Damp lint-free cloths or a kitchen sponge
  • Petroleum jelly or a wax candle stub (for resist methods)
  • Two paint colors for layering
  • Clear wax or water-based polycrylic topcoat
  • Dust mask and safety glasses
  • Tack cloth or microfiber cloth

A brand-new coat of paint can look a little too perfect on an old farmhouse table or a vintage dresser. Learning how to distress painted furniture lets you add those soft, worn edges that make a piece look like it has been in the family for generations. Best of all, it's one of the most forgiving techniques in DIY. A little extra wear just adds character.

Distressing works best on matte paints. If you haven't painted your piece yet, start with our guide on how to paint furniture with chalk paint, then come back here before you seal it.

Where to Add Wear So It Looks Natural

The secret to believable distressing is thinking about where hands, feet, and years would naturally rub away paint. Random scuffs in the middle of a drawer front look forced, while worn edges look authentic.

  • Outer edges and corners of tabletops and dressers
  • Around knobs, pulls, and keyholes
  • Raised trim, carved details, and moldings
  • The bottom of legs and chair rungs where feet rest
  • Drawer edges and cabinet door frames
Mom tip: Step back every few minutes and look at the piece from across the room. It's easy to overdo distressing when you're up close, and you can always sand more, but you'll have to repaint if you take off too much.

How to Distress Painted Furniture with Sandpaper

Dry sanding is the classic method and gives you the most control. Let your paint dry completely, ideally overnight, before you begin.

  1. Put on a dust mask and safety glasses, and work outside or in a ventilated space.
  2. Start with a 220-grit sanding sponge for subtle wear. Switch to 120-grit only where you want heavier distressing.
  3. Rub along edges and high points with light pressure, following the direction of the wood grain.
  4. Vary the amount of wear so it doesn't look uniform. Some spots should barely show wood, others a little more.
  5. Wipe away all dust with a tack cloth or damp microfiber cloth before sealing.

Remember that sanding old finishes can release lead dust on pieces made before 1978. If you aren't sure of the age, use a lead test kit before you sand.

Wet Distressing (Our Low-Dust Favorite)

Wet distressing works beautifully with chalk paint and creates almost no dust, so it's a great choice if you're working indoors or with kids nearby.

  1. Let the paint dry to the touch, but not for days. Within a few hours is ideal.
  2. Dampen a lint-free cloth or kitchen sponge with water and wring it out well.
  3. Rub the edges and details you want to distress. The paint will soften and lift away.
  4. Rinse your cloth often and keep it barely damp so water doesn't drip onto the piece.
  5. Let the piece dry fully before sealing.

Resist and Layering Methods

Petroleum jelly or wax resist

This method creates chippy, naturally worn spots. Before painting, dab a little petroleum jelly or rub a candle stub on the edges you want to show wear. Paint over the whole piece. Once dry, wipe or lightly sand those areas and the paint lifts away easily, revealing the wood or base color underneath. Wipe off any leftover jelly with a cloth and mild soap before sealing, or your topcoat won't stick.

Two-color layering

For a farmhouse look with depth, paint a base coat in one color, such as a soft blue or warm wood tone, and let it dry. Add your top color, then sand edges lightly so the base color peeks through. Pairing colors is easier with our color palette generator, which suggests harmonious combinations.

Choose a Distressing Style That Fits Your Home

Not every piece needs the same amount of wear. Think about the overall feel of the room before you start sanding.

  • Light distressing: just the edges and corners are softened. This looks clean and works well in modern farmhouse and transitional spaces.
  • Medium distressing: edges, trim, and areas around hardware show wood or a base color. This is the classic cottage look.
  • Heavy distressing: larger patches of paint are worn away for a chippy, rustic, antique-shop feel.

Color matters, too. White and cream over a darker wood creates strong contrast, while a soft gray over a natural wood tone is more subtle. If you want a gentle look, choose a top color and base that are close in value. For bold contrast, pair a light paint with dark stain underneath.

Seal Your Distressed Piece

Once you love the look, protect it. Clear wax gives a soft, aged sheen and deepens exposed wood slightly. A water-based polycrylic in a matte finish is more durable for tables and kids' furniture. Apply thin coats and let the topcoat cure fully before heavy use. For a warm, antique look, some makers also use a dark-tinted wax in the recesses after the clear wax.

Mom tip: Practice on a scrap board or the back of a drawer first. Five minutes of testing tells you exactly how much pressure to use on the real piece.

Pieces That Look Great Distressed

Distressing suits more than dressers. Try it on picture frames, wooden signs, step stools, crates, and side tables. It also pairs well with homemade paint, so try our DIY chalk paint recipe to keep the cost low. For more budget-friendly room ideas, browse our DIY home decor ideas on a budget.

Frequently asked questions

What grit sandpaper is best for distressing furniture?

Start with 220-grit for subtle wear, and use 120-grit only where you want heavier distressing that shows more wood.

Should I distress before or after sealing?

Distress before sealing. Once you're happy with the look, wipe off all dust and apply wax or a water-based topcoat to protect it.

Can I distress latex paint?

Yes, though it can peel in strips instead of sanding cleanly. Matte and chalk-style paints are easier to distress evenly.
