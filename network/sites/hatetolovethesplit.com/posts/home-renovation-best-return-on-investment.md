Home renovation best return on investment is a question we did not ask once in the first ten years of this house, and we should have. We renovated in the order that annoyed us most: the kitchen first because it was avocado green, the bathroom second because of the wallpaper, the hallway because a door fell off. Resale value never came up. Then a neighbor sold a nearly identical split-level, we saw the appraisal, and we learned that the two projects that moved their number most were the two we had been putting off: a new roof and a new front door. This is the ranking we wish we had read in 2013, built from the national resale data, adjusted for what a split-level actually is, and with our own costs where we have them.

One honest caveat before the list. Return on investment in renovation almost never means profit. The best projects return 80 to 100 percent of their cost at sale. Most return 50 to 70. You renovate to live better and to sell faster, and the ranking below is about not throwing money away, not about making it.

## Where the numbers come from

The most-cited source is the annual Cost vs. Value report, which surveys real estate professionals on what each project adds to resale in dozens of markets. The [Zonda Cost vs. Value report](https://www.remodeling.hw.net/cost-vs-value/) is the one we use; the Minneapolis figures sit close to the national ones. We also looked at the National Association of Realtors' remodeling impact surveys, which ask agents and homeowners the same questions and land in the same places. Where the two disagree, we say so.

## The ranking, 2026

### 1. Garage door replacement (roughly 190 to 200 percent)

The single highest-return project in the country for several years running, and the most boring. A new insulated steel garage door costs 4,000 to 5,500 installed and adds more than that to the appraised value, because it is the largest single object on the front of most houses and buyers see it before anything else. On a split-level with the garage under the bedrooms, it is half the facade.

### 2. Steel entry door (roughly 180 to 190 percent)

Same logic, smaller scale. 2,000 to 3,000 installed, and the first thing a buyer touches. We did a cheap version of this with the [front stoop refresh](/2014/10/12/front-stoop-refresh/) and it changed how the house felt from the street more than any interior project.

### 3. Manufactured stone veneer (roughly 150 to 160 percent)

Replacing the bottom third of vinyl siding with stone veneer on the front elevation. 11,000 to 13,000. Popular with buyers, sometimes overdone. On a split-level, the lower-level wall under the living room windows is the natural spot.

### 4. Minor kitchen remodel (roughly 95 to 100 percent)

This is the kitchen project that pays: keep the layout, reface or replace cabinet fronts, new counters, new appliances, new floor, new paint. 27,000 to 30,000 nationally. Our 2014 kitchen was a budget version of exactly this, and the [twelve-year review](/2026/11/17/kitchen-remodel-before-and-after/) explains why keeping the footprint was the decision that made it pay.

### 5. Siding replacement, fiber cement or vinyl (roughly 85 to 95 percent)

18,000 to 22,000. The return is high because old siding scares buyers and inspectors. A split-level with original 1970s hardboard siding is the classic case.

### 6. Window replacement, vinyl (roughly 70 to 75 percent)

20,000 to 25,000 for a typical house. Returns are lower than people expect because buyers assume windows work until they do not. The energy savings are real but slow. Replace them when they fail, not for resale.

### 7. Roof replacement, asphalt (roughly 60 to 70 percent)

This is the one that confused us. A new asphalt roof returns 60 to 70 cents on the dollar in the surveys, which sounds poor until you understand what the surveys do not capture: a roof at end of life does not reduce the sale price by its replacement cost, it kills the sale. Buyers' inspectors flag it, lenders flag it, insurers increasingly refuse to write policies on roofs over 15 to 20 years old, and the buyer walks or demands a credit larger than the roof costs. The neighbor's appraisal moved most on the new roof because the comparable houses had old ones. Our view: a roof is not a return project, it is a prerequisite for selling at all, and the right time to do it is before you list, or when a storm gives you an insurance claim that covers most of it.

### 8. Bathroom remodel, mid-range (roughly 65 to 75 percent)

25,000 for a full redo in the surveys. Our [bathroom reveal](/2015/02/22/main-bathroom-reveal/) cost a fraction of that because we kept every fixture in place, and the return logic is the same as the kitchen: the footprint is the expensive part. A second bathroom added where there was none can return more, especially in a split-level with only one full bath, but it costs 50,000 and up.

### 9. Deck addition, wood (roughly 50 to 65 percent)

17,000 to 20,000. Buyers like decks and discount them for maintenance. Composite decks cost more and return a smaller share. Our [paver patio](/2014/06/22/installing-cement-pavers/) was a DIY alternative that cost under 2,000 and photographs just as well.

### 10. Major kitchen remodel, upscale (roughly 35 to 45 percent)

Moving walls, custom cabinets, professional appliances, 80,000 and up. The worst return on the list, because you are buying taste that the next owner may not share. Do it if you are staying fifteen years and want it. Do not do it for resale.

## Where a split-level changes the ranking

The surveys are for the average house. A 1970s split-level has quirks that move a few of these:

- The front elevation is mostly garage door and lower-level wall. That makes the garage door and stone veneer projects even more visible than average, and worth more.
- Finished lower levels count. A finished basement or family room on the lower level (the surveys put basement finishing at 70 to 75 percent) returns well in split-levels because buyers expect that space to be livable and are disappointed when it is not.
- Low ceilings on the upper level punish anything that adds bulk. Crown molding, soffits, big pendants and tall upper cabinets all make a split-level feel smaller, which is why the minor kitchen remodel beats the major one here by more than the national gap.
- Roof planes are visible from the street. A split-level's lower roof sits at eye level from the sidewalk, so a tired roof is not hidden the way it is on a two-story. Curling shingles on that lower plane are the first thing a buyer sees after the garage door.

## How to choose if you are selling within five years

1. Fix what fails inspection first: roof, electrical, water intrusion, the furnace. These do not return money; they prevent losing the sale.
2. Spend on the front elevation next: garage door, entry door, paint, a clean stoop. Cheap, fast, highest return.
3. Do the minor kitchen and the cosmetic bath. Keep the plumbing where it is.
4. Finish the lower level if it is unfinished and dry.
5. Skip the upscale kitchen, the composite deck, the master suite addition and the pool. Enjoy them if you are staying; do not expect the next buyer to pay for them.

## How to choose if you are staying

Reverse the list. Spend where you live: the kitchen you cook in, the bathroom you use every morning, the lower level the kids will take over. The exterior jobs will still be waiting when you sell, and they are the ones that age predictably. Our [room-by-room cost guide](/2026/11/10/home-renovation-cost/) has the 2026 numbers for both lists.

The short version of home renovation best return on investment: the dull exterior projects pay back the most, the roof is a gate rather than a return, a kitchen pays when you leave the walls alone, and a split-level rewards anyone who makes the front of the house look cared for. We learned it from someone else's appraisal. Cheaper than learning it from our own.
