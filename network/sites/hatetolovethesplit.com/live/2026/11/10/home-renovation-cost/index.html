What a Home Renovation Really Costs in 2026, Room by Room

By the Split · November 10, 2026 · 7 min read

  1. Home
  2. Random Thoughts
  3. What a Home Renovation Really Costs in 2026, Room by Room

Home renovation cost is the number everyone wants before they start and nobody trusts after they finish. Ours came in at roughly double the first guess for the kitchen, about right for the bathroom, and embarrassingly over on the hallway, which was supposed to be the cheap one. This is the room-by-room version I wish someone had handed us in 2013: what each space tends to cost in 2026, which line items blow up, and where a split-level specifically bites you.

A note on the numbers. They are national ranges for a mid-range job on an ordinary house, adjusted to this year, with Twin Cities labor in mind. Coastal cities run higher, small towns lower. The point is not the exact figure. It is the shape of the spending, so you can see where your own estimate is thin.

How to read any home renovation cost figure

Every quote has three layers: materials, labor, and the stuff nobody puts on the first sheet. Permits, dumpster rental, the plumber who has to come twice because the first pipe was not where the drawing said, the extra sheet of drywall because you dropped one. In our experience that third layer runs 10 to 20 percent of the total, and it is the reason a 15 percent contingency is the minimum, not a cushion.

The other thing to know is that labor is now the larger half of most jobs. Ten years ago materials and labor were close to even on a kitchen. Today a licensed electrician in Minneapolis bills more than many of the cabinets he is wiring around. If you can do the demolition, painting and cleanup yourself, you are removing the cheapest labor, not the expensive kind, but it still adds up.

Kitchen

The kitchen is where the money goes. A full mid-range remodel with new semi-custom cabinets, quartz counters, new appliances and the layout left alone lands between 35,000 and 70,000 dollars in 2026. Move the sink or the range and add 3,000 to 8,000 for plumbing, venting and electrical. Open a wall and you are into engineering and a beam, usually another 5,000 to 15,000.

Our own kitchen was the budget version of that, with IKEA boxes, laminate counters and the same footprint, and even that cleared 14,000 once appliances were in. The Akurum cabinet install saved us the installer's fee, which at the time was quoted at about 4,000. The thing we did not save on was the countertop template and fabrication, because nobody should learn that on their own house.

Where kitchens run over: electrical. Older houses, split-levels included, rarely have enough circuits for a modern kitchen. Budget two new 20-amp circuits for counters, a dedicated circuit for the microwave and another for a dishwasher, and you are at 1,500 to 3,000 before a single cabinet goes up.

Bathroom

A main bathroom, kept in place, with new tile, vanity, toilet, tub or shower and fixtures, runs 15,000 to 30,000. A small half bath is 6,000 to 12,000. The spread is almost entirely tile labor and waterproofing. A tiled shower pan done by someone who knows what they are doing is 2,500 to 5,000 on its own, and that is the one place in the house where cheap work shows up as a ceiling stain two years later.

Our bathroom reveal used faux wood tile and a mocha grout that hid a lot of sins, and we kept the plumbing exactly where it was. That one choice is worth more than any other on a bathroom. The moment the toilet moves, the floor comes up, the drain gets rerouted and the plumber's number doubles.

Basement

Finishing a basement is the best cost per square foot in the house and the one most likely to be a mistake if the basement is damp. Framing, insulation, drywall, drop ceiling or drywall ceiling, flooring and electrical for a basic finished space is 30 to 60 dollars per square foot in 2026, so a 600-square-foot room is 18,000 to 36,000. Add a bathroom down there and you are adding 10,000 to 20,000, more if there is no rough-in and an ejector pump is needed.

Before any of that, fix water. Grading, gutters and downspout extensions are hundreds of dollars. Interior drain tile and a sump are 8,000 to 15,000. Finish a basement that still gets a puddle in April and you will spend the drain tile money anyway, plus the cost of redoing the drywall.

Flooring

Flooring is simple to price and easy to underestimate because of the prep. Installed costs in 2026: luxury vinyl plank 5 to 10 dollars per square foot, laminate 4 to 8, engineered hardwood 9 to 15, site-finished solid hardwood 12 to 20, carpet 4 to 8. Refinishing existing hardwood is 4 to 7 per square foot and is almost always the best money on this list.

The prep is the catch. Pulling old carpet and tack strip, leveling a floor, replacing subfloor around an old leak, and the transitions at every doorway can add 2 to 4 dollars a square foot. A split-level has a lot of doorways and a lot of half-flights of stairs, and stairs are priced per step, usually 75 to 150 dollars each for hardwood treads.

Walls, ceilings and paint

This is the DIY zone and the place a determined homeowner saves the most. Popcorn ceiling removal and skim coat by a pro is 2 to 4 dollars a square foot. We did ours ourselves and wrote up the tongue and groove ceiling that went over the worst of it, which cost about 900 dollars in material for the room and a weekend of misery.

Professional interior painting is 3 to 6 dollars per square foot of floor area for walls and ceilings, so a 1,600-square-foot house is 5,000 to 10,000. Paint and supplies to do it yourself are 400 to 900 for the same house. Wallpaper removal, which we learned about the hard way, is 1 to 3 dollars a square foot of wall if someone else does it, and a lot of patching either way.

Windows and doors

Replacement windows are 600 to 1,500 each installed for vinyl, 1,000 to 2,500 for fiberglass or wood-clad. A split-level with 14 to 18 windows is a 12,000 to 30,000 project. Exterior doors are 1,500 to 4,000 installed. Interior doors are 300 to 700 each installed, or the cost of a hinge template and a lot of patience if you hang them yourself, which is a story we have already told.

Roof, siding and the exterior

The exterior is where estimates are least negotiable because the labor is the labor. An asphalt shingle roof on a 1,800-square-foot split-level, with its two roof planes at different heights, runs 12,000 to 22,000 in 2026 for architectural shingles, tear-off included. Metal is roughly double. Gutters and downspouts are 1,500 to 3,500. Vinyl siding is 8 to 14 dollars a square foot installed, fiber cement 12 to 20.

A roof is also the one job on this list where storm damage changes the math. If a hail or wind event is the reason, a homeowner's insurance claim can cover most of it minus the deductible, and the real cost becomes the deductible plus whatever upgrades you add. Get the inspection before you get the quote.

What the national data says

The Joint Center for Housing Studies at Harvard tracks remodeling spending every year and its Improving America's Housing reports are the sanest source for how much Americans actually spend and on what. The pattern it shows every cycle: kitchens and baths get the headlines, but replacement projects (roofs, windows, siding, HVAC) take the biggest share of real spending because they cannot wait. Plan for the boring ones first.

A whole-house estimate for a split-level

Put it together for a typical 1970s split-level that needs everything, done mid-range with a mix of pro and DIY labor:

  • Kitchen, same footprint: 30,000 to 50,000
  • Main bathroom: 15,000 to 25,000
  • Half bath: 6,000 to 10,000
  • Basement finish, 600 square feet: 20,000 to 35,000
  • Flooring throughout, mixed LVP and refinished hardwood: 12,000 to 20,000
  • Paint, ceilings and trim, mostly DIY: 3,000 to 8,000
  • Windows, 16 units: 12,000 to 24,000
  • Roof and gutters: 14,000 to 25,000
  • Contingency at 15 percent: 17,000 to 30,000

That is 130,000 to 225,000 dollars, spread over however many years your patience and your savings allow. Ours has been spread over twelve so far. The order that worked for us was water first, roof and gutters second, then the kitchen, then one room at a time as money appeared. The order that does not work is the one where the kitchen goes first and the roof leaks on it.

The costs estimates leave out

  • Permits: 200 to 2,000 depending on scope. Required for electrical, plumbing, structural and most roofing in Minneapolis and the suburbs.
  • Dumpsters: 400 to 700 each, and a kitchen fills one.
  • Living somewhere else: a kitchen without a kitchen is six to ten weeks of takeout, which is real money.
  • Tools: if you are doing it yourself, the first project pays for a miter saw, a good drill and a tile saw, which is 600 to 1,200 you will not spend again.
  • The thing you find: knob and tube, a cracked drain stack, a rotten rim joist. Every house has one. The contingency is for that, not for upgrading the faucet.

If you want the budget framework that goes with these numbers, the whole-house renovation budget post walks through how to turn ranges like these into a plan you can actually track. The short version: price the house, not the room, and make the roof and the water the first lines on the sheet.
