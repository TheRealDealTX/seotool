Carpet, Countertops and Beer: Our Basement Bar Kegerator

By Emelia · March 17, 2014 · 2 min read

  1. Home
  2. Basement
  3. Carpet, Countertops and Beer: Our Basement Bar Kegerator

The lower level of a split-level is basically designed for one thing: hanging out. Ours had wall-to-wall 1970s carpet and a sad wet bar. Now it has new carpet, a butcher-block countertop, and a kegerator with a beer tap right on the counter.

Built-in kegerator vs. freestanding

This is the single most important thing we learned: a built-in kegerator needs front ventilation. Most freestanding kegerators vent heat out the back or sides. Shove one under a countertop and it will overheat, run constantly, and die young. If you want an under-cabinet kegerator, buy one rated for built-in use.

  • Built-in (front-venting): designed to slide under a counter, zero-clearance on the sides.
  • Freestanding: needs several inches of airflow around it. Fine at the end of a counter run.

Choosing a kegerator

We compared a few brands while shopping. Summit and Marvel both make built-in kegerators that fit standard 24" undercounter openings, and Beverage Factory sells a wide range of units, towers and conversion kits. We cared about three things: front venting, a stainless interior, and whether a full-size half-barrel would fit. Read the specs carefully; "fits a keg" sometimes means a sixtel.

The countertop beer tap

Most kegerators come with a tower that mounts on top of the unit. Under a counter, the tower has to go through the countertop instead. That means:

  1. Drilling a 3" hole through the countertop directly above the kegerator's tower opening.
  2. Running the beer line up through an insulated column so the beer stays cold all the way to the faucet.
  3. Using a tower cooling fan if the line is long, or your first pour will be all foam.
✦
IKEA cabinets + kegerator

We built the bar from IKEA base cabinets with one 24" bay left open for the kegerator. A matching filler panel above and a continuous toe-kick make it look built-in.

And the carpet?

Low-pile, stain-resistant, and a warm gray. Basements get cold in Minnesota, and bare floors down here would be miserable in January. Plus: it hides the occasional drip from the tap. Not that we'd know.
