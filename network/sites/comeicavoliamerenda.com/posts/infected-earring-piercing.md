What to do when an earring is infected depends on one question most guides skip: is it actually infected, or is it irritated? The two look alike in the first day, they are treated differently, and getting it wrong in either direction causes problems. Treat an irritation like an infection and you may remove the earring, close the hole and have to start over. Treat an infection like an irritation and it can spread, especially in cartilage. This guide gives you the test for which one you have, the routine that resolves most cases in a week, the things people put on piercings that make them worse, and the short list of signs that mean a doctor today, not tomorrow.

This is general information, not medical advice. A piercer can look at a piercing; only a doctor can prescribe antibiotics. When in doubt, see one.

## Irritation or infection: the one-minute check

Irritation is the piercing reacting to something: a snagged earring, sleeping on it, a too-tight back, a metal you are sensitive to, over-cleaning. Infection is bacteria inside the wound. Here is how they differ.

| Sign | Irritation | Infection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Redness | A ring close to the hole, fades over the day | Spreading, hot to the touch, getting bigger |
| Swelling | Mild, stable or improving | Increasing, may close around the earring |
| Discharge | Clear or pale yellow crust (lymph), dries clear | Thick yellow, green or gray, may smell |
| Pain | Tender when touched | Throbbing without touching, worse over 48 hours |
| Timeline | Improves within two to three days of removing the cause | Worsens over two to three days |
| Fever, chills, red streaks | Never | Possible; this is a doctor visit |

If your piercing matches the left column, it is almost certainly irritation, and the answer is to find the cause and stop it. If it matches the right column, keep reading.

## What to do when an earring is infected: the routine

### Step 1: leave the earring in

This surprises people. The instinct is to take it out. Do not, unless a doctor tells you to. The earring keeps the channel open so the infection can drain. Remove it and the surface can close over an infected pocket, which is how a sore lobe turns into an abscess. The exception is a cheap earring you suspect is the cause (nickel, a rough post, a butterfly back that is clamped too tight); swap it for an implant-grade titanium or 14-karat gold stud with a flat back, ideally at a piercing studio, and keep the hole open.

### Step 2: saline, twice a day, nothing else

Make or buy sterile saline: 0.9 percent sodium chloride, which is the same as the body. Pre-mixed wound wash saline in a spray can is easiest and stays sterile. If mixing at home, use a quarter teaspoon of non-iodized salt in one cup (250 ml) of boiled, cooled water, and make a fresh batch each time.

Soak a clean gauze pad or a folded paper towel, hold it against the piercing for five minutes so the crust softens, then gently rinse with fresh saline and pat dry with a disposable paper towel. Do not twist the earring. Twisting tears the healing tissue and pushes bacteria in. Do not pick off crust with fingernails. Twice a day is enough; more than that dries and irritates the skin and slows healing.

### Step 3: warm compress for drainage

If there is swelling and pus, a warm (not hot) compress for ten minutes before the saline soak helps it drain. A clean washcloth run under warm water, wrung out, held against the ear. Use a fresh cloth every time.

### Step 4: hands off and sleep on the other side

Wash your hands before touching the ear and do not touch it otherwise. Change your pillowcase daily or put a clean T-shirt over the pillow. Keep hair, hats, headphones, phones and hair products away from it. If it is a cartilage piercing, a travel pillow with a hole lets you sleep without pressing on it.

### Step 5: give it 48 to 72 hours

A minor lobe infection treated this way usually turns a corner within two to three days: less heat, less swelling, discharge thinning and lightening. Full resolution takes a week or two. If it is not improving by day three, or it is worse at any point, that is the doctor threshold below.

## What not to put on it

- Rubbing alcohol and hydrogen peroxide. Both kill bacteria and also kill the new cells trying to close the wound. They sting because they are damaging tissue. Skip them.
- Antibiotic ointment (Neosporin and similar). Ointments trap moisture and bacteria in the channel and are not designed for puncture wounds. A doctor may prescribe a specific topical; over-the-counter ointment on your own is usually counterproductive.
- Tea tree oil, essential oils, toothpaste, aspirin paste. All irritants. None are antiseptic enough to matter and all can cause contact dermatitis that looks exactly like a worsening infection.
- Ear care solutions with benzalkonium chloride, often sold at mall piercing kiosks. Harsh, and associated with prolonged irritation. Saline is better and cheaper.
- Makeup, perfume, hairspray near the ear. Obvious, often forgotten.

## Lobe versus cartilage: why it matters

An infected lobe is common, low-risk and responds to the routine above because the lobe has good blood supply. Cartilage (helix, tragus, conch, rook, industrial) is different. It has poor blood supply, infections there progress faster and deeper, and an untreated cartilage infection can destroy the cartilage and leave a permanently misshapen ear. The threshold for seeing a doctor with a cartilage piercing is much lower: if a cartilage piercing is hot, swollen and painful for more than a day, go. Our [helix earrings guide](/helix-earrings/) and [cartilage earrings guide](/cute-cartilage-earrings/) both cover jewelry choices that reduce the risk in the first place, mainly flat-back labret studs instead of hoops during healing.

## When to see a doctor

Go the same day if any of these apply:

- Fever, chills or feeling unwell.
- Red streaks spreading from the piercing toward the face or neck.
- Swelling that is closing over the earring or the earring is embedded.
- Pus that is green or gray, or a foul smell.
- A cartilage piercing that is hot, swollen and throbbing.
- Any infection in a child under 10, or in anyone with diabetes, an immune condition or a heart valve condition.
- No improvement after 72 hours of the routine, or worsening at any point.

A doctor will usually prescribe an oral antibiotic for a true infection, drain an abscess if one has formed, and tell you whether to leave the jewelry in. The American Academy of Dermatology's guidance on [caring for pierced ears](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/tattoos/caring-for-pierced-ears) is the mainstream medical reference, and it agrees on the basics: saline, leave the earring in, see a doctor if it spreads.

## Keloid or bump? Not the same as infection

A firm, painless or slightly tender bump beside a piercing that appears weeks or months later, with no heat or pus, is not an infection. It is usually an irritation bump (hypertrophic scar) or, in people prone to them, a keloid. Irritation bumps often resolve when the cause (a hoop, sleeping on it, a too-short post) is removed. Keloids need a dermatologist. We cover both in detail in the [earring guides hub](/earring-guides/), which is the next thing to read if your "infection" has no discharge and has been there for a month.

## Preventing the next one

Most infections come from the first six weeks, and most are preventable:

1. Get pierced with a needle at a studio, not a gun at a kiosk. Guns use blunt studs that crush tissue and cannot be sterilized.
2. Start with implant-grade titanium or 14-karat-plus gold. No "hypoallergenic" mystery metal, no sterling silver in a fresh piercing. The [earring types guide](/earring-types/) explains the metals.
3. Posts long enough for swelling, downsized after four to eight weeks.
4. Saline twice a day, hands off, nothing else, for the full healing time: six to eight weeks for lobes, six to twelve months for cartilage.
5. No hoops, no dangles and no sleeping on it until it is healed.

The short version of what to do when an earring is infected: leave it in, saline twice a day, warm compresses, hands off, and a doctor if it spreads, if it is cartilage, or if it is not better in three days. Most lobe infections are a week-long annoyance handled at home. The ones that become stories are the ones where someone pulled the earring, put peroxide on it and waited.
