How to get rid of an earring keloid is one of the most searched piercing questions and one of the most mis-answered, because most of the bumps people call keloids are not keloids. That is good news. The common bump beside a new piercing, the pink or skin-colored lump that appears a few weeks in and seems to have a mind of its own, is usually an irritation bump or a small hypertrophic scar, and those respond to simple changes at home. A true keloid is rarer, grows beyond the original wound, does not go away on its own, and needs a dermatologist. This guide tells you which one you have, what to do about each, what not to do, and how to keep it from coming back on the next piercing.

As always on this site: general information, not medical advice. A piercer can look at a bump; only a doctor can treat a keloid.

## Keloid or irritation bump: the differences that matter

| Sign | Irritation bump or hypertrophic scar | True keloid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When it appears | Weeks to a few months after piercing | Months to a year or more, sometimes later |
| Size | Small, usually under 5 mm, stays at the piercing | Grows larger than the original wound, can reach 1 cm or more |
| Shape | Soft or slightly firm dome right at the hole | Firm, rubbery, shiny, often irregular, spreading outward |
| Color | Pink, red or skin-colored; may have clear or white fluid | Pink to dark brown or purple; darker on deeper skin tones |
| Feel | Tender when bumped | Itchy, sometimes painful, can feel tight |
| Course | Shrinks when the irritation stops | Stays or keeps growing without treatment |
| Where | Any piercing, especially cartilage | Earlobes and cartilage; people with a family history or darker skin are more prone |

If your bump appeared within the first three months, sits right at the piercing, is small and changes with how you treat it, it is almost certainly an irritation bump. If it has outgrown the piercing, is hard and shiny, and has been there for six months or more, read the keloid section and book a dermatologist.

## Getting rid of an irritation bump

Irritation bumps form because the healing tissue is being disturbed. The treatment is to remove the disturbance, and the bump follows within two to eight weeks. In rough order of how often each one is the cause:

1. Change the jewelry. Hoops, rings and dangles in a healing piercing move constantly and are the number one cause. Switch to a flat-back labret stud in implant-grade titanium or 14-karat-plus gold, sized with a little room for swelling. A piercing studio will do this and will tell you if the post is too long or too short, which is the second most common cause. Our [helix earrings](/helix-earrings/) and [cartilage earrings](/cute-cartilage-earrings/) guides cover the jewelry that heals cleanly.
2. Stop sleeping on it. A travel pillow with a hole, or sleeping on the other side, is the fix nobody wants to hear and the one that works.
3. Stop touching, twisting and over-cleaning. Saline twice a day and nothing else. No alcohol, no peroxide, no tea tree oil, no aspirin paste; all of them irritate healing tissue and make bumps worse. The [infected earring guide](/infected-earring-piercing/) explains why.
4. Check for a metal reaction. Nickel is the usual culprit. A reaction looks like redness and a bump that will not settle no matter what you do, often with itching. Implant-grade titanium solves it.
5. Keep hair, hats, headphones and phones off it. Cartilage piercings on the phone side of the head are notorious.
6. Be patient. A bump that has shrunk by half in a month is a bump that is going away. Do not pull the jewelry; the hole closes over the bump and traps it.

Things that do not help and are often recommended: hot compresses with salt (saline soaks are fine, but the heat and the extra salt are not doing the work), chamomile, crushed aspirin, apple cider vinegar, and any ointment. If the bump is tender, warm and draining pus, it is not an irritation bump; it is an infection, and the infected earring guide is where to go.

## Getting rid of a true keloid

A keloid is an overgrowth of scar tissue that keeps producing collagen long after the wound has closed. It is a skin condition, not a hygiene problem, and nothing you buy at a pharmacy will shrink an established keloid. What a dermatologist can do, usually in combination:

- Corticosteroid injections. The first-line treatment. A steroid is injected into the keloid every four to six weeks for several sessions. Most keloids soften and flatten; some resolve. Side effects can include lightening of the skin at the site.
- Pressure earrings. Specially designed clip earrings worn for 12 or more hours a day for months after injection or surgery. Pressure reduces recurrence, which is the whole challenge with keloids.
- Cryotherapy. Freezing the keloid, often combined with injections, for smaller lobe keloids.
- Surgical removal plus something else. Cutting a keloid out on its own has a high recurrence rate, often worse than the original. Surgeons pair removal with injections, pressure, or low-dose radiation to the site.
- Silicone gel sheets. Modest evidence for softening and for prevention after treatment; not a cure for an established keloid, but low-risk.
- Laser. Can reduce redness and thickness in some keloids, usually alongside injections.

The American Academy of Dermatology's [keloid treatment overview](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/keloids-treatment) lists the options and their expected results and is the best place to go before an appointment, so you know what to ask.

## Preventing the next one

If you have had a true keloid, you are prone to them, and every future piercing is a gamble. Many dermatologists will advise against further ear piercings; if you go ahead, choose the lobe over cartilage, have the piercing done with a needle by a professional, wear a pressure earring on the site after healing, and see a dermatologist at the first sign of a firm bump, when injections work best.

If you have only had irritation bumps, prevention is the healing routine: needle piercing, implant-grade jewelry, flat-back studs, saline only, no sleeping on it, no hoops until healed. The [earring piercing guide](/earring-piercing-guide/) lists healing times by placement so you know how long "until healed" is for each one.

## When to see someone

- A piercer: for any bump in the first three months, to check jewelry fit and metal, and for a downsize.
- A doctor, soon: for a bump that is hot, painful, spreading or draining pus, or for a cartilage bump with fever.
- A dermatologist: for a firm, growing bump that has outlasted the piercing's healing time, for any bump in someone with a personal or family history of keloids, and for a lump on the lobe that keeps growing.

The short version of how to get rid of an earring keloid: find out whether it is one. Most are irritation bumps, and those go away when you change the jewelry, stop sleeping on the ear and leave it alone. The rare true keloid needs a dermatologist and usually injections, and the earlier you go, the better the result.
