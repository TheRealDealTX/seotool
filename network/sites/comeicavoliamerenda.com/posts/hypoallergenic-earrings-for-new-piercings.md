Hypoallergenic earrings for new piercings is the search every kiosk, pharmacy and online shop is ready for, and the word they are all leaning on is not regulated. "Hypoallergenic" has no legal definition for jewelry in the United States or the United Kingdom. A seller can print it on a card of nickel-plated brass. So this guide sets the word aside and talks about what a new piercing actually needs: a specific metal, a specific finish and a specific shape, for the six weeks to twelve months that the channel takes to heal. It covers the four materials professional piercers use, the ones that cause most of the reactions people blame on "sensitive ears," how to read a product listing to tell them apart, and what changes once the piercing is healed.

As always: general information, not medical advice. A piercer can look at your jewelry; a dermatologist can test for a metal allergy.

## What "hypoallergenic" is hiding

Most jewelry reactions are nickel. Nickel allergy affects roughly one in six people, more often women, and is often caused by ear piercing in the first place: a fresh wound in constant contact with nickel is how many people become sensitized. The reaction looks like itching, redness, weeping and a rash around the hole that does not improve with cleaning. Once you have it, you have it for life.

The problem is that nickel is cheap, hard and shiny, so it is in most inexpensive jewelry: as the base metal under plating, as a hardener in white gold, and in the stainless steel used for a lot of "surgical" studs. A gold-plated stud wears through to its nickel base in weeks. A "surgical steel" stud may release more nickel than the European limit for prolonged skin contact. Neither label tells you.

The European Union regulates nickel release from jewelry; the United States does not. The American Academy of Dermatology's [nickel allergy guidance](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/nickel-allergy) is the plain-language reference for what the reaction looks like and how it is confirmed.

## The four materials piercers trust for healing

Professional piercing associations recommend materials by standard, not by marketing word. These are the four you will find in any reputable studio, and the labels to look for.

### Implant-grade titanium (ASTM F-136 or ISO 5832-3)

The default for a new piercing. Nickel-free, light, does not corrode in body fluids, and can be anodized to colors without coatings that flake. The standard number matters: "titanium" alone can mean a lower grade or a titanium-plated base. Look for "ASTM F-136" or "implant grade" in the listing, from a seller who names the mill or the manufacturer.

### Implant-grade steel (ASTM F-138)

Steel made to a surgical implant standard releases very little nickel because of how it is alloyed and finished, and many people with mild nickel sensitivity wear it without trouble. It is heavier than titanium and a poor choice for anyone with a confirmed nickel allergy, who should go to titanium or niobium. "Surgical steel" or "316L" without the F-138 standard is not the same thing.

### Niobium

A pure element, nickel-free, slightly heavier than titanium, and anodizes beautifully. Less common and a little more expensive, with fewer jewelry styles available. An excellent choice for a confirmed nickel allergy.

### Solid gold, 14 karat or higher, nickel-free and alloyed for body jewelry

Gold is soft, so it is alloyed. Lower karats and most commercial white gold use nickel. For a healing piercing, 14 karat or 18 karat yellow or rose gold from a body-jewelry maker who states it is nickel-free, with a polished finish and no plating. 24 karat is too soft to hold a post. Gold-filled and gold-plated are not solid gold and do not belong in a fresh piercing.

Platinum is also safe and is simply expensive. Glass (borosilicate or fused quartz) is inert and used for some healed piercings and retainers, rarely for initial jewelry.

## What to avoid while healing

- Anything plated. Plating wears through.
- Sterling silver. It tarnishes, the tarnish can stain the channel, and many silver alloys contain copper and traces of nickel. Fine for healed lobes, wrong for a healing piercing.
- "Surgical steel," "stainless steel," "hypoallergenic metal," "nickel-free" with no standard quoted.
- Acrylic and most plastics in a fresh piercing. They scratch, harbor bacteria and can degrade. Medical-grade PTFE or bioplast retainers are the exception, for people who must hide a piercing at work.
- Wood, bone, horn and stone in a new piercing. Porous.
- Costume jewelry of unknown composition, however pretty.

## Shape matters as much as metal

The right metal in the wrong shape still irritates. For a new lobe or cartilage piercing, piercers fit a flat-back labret stud: a straight post with a flat disc on the inside and a threaded or push-fit top on the outside. It sits still, does not catch on hair or pillows, and is easy to clean behind. Butterfly backs trap debris and clamp swelling; hoops move with every turn of the head and are the most common cause of the irritation bumps covered in our [earring keloids guide](/how-to-get-rid-of-earring-keloid/). Posts should be sized with a little room for swelling and then downsized after four to eight weeks; a post that is too long swings, and one that is too short embeds.

Gauge matters too. Standard lobe piercings are 20 or 18 gauge; cartilage is usually 16. Our [gauge chart](/ear-gauge-size-chart/) converts the numbers, and the [earring types guide](/earring-types/) shows the shapes.

## How to read a listing

A trustworthy body-jewelry listing says, somewhere, all of the following: the exact material (implant-grade titanium, not "titanium"), the standard (ASTM F-136), the gauge (16g, 18g), the post length in millimeters, the threading type (internally threaded or threadless, never externally threaded for a fresh piercing because the threads scrape the channel), and the finish (mirror polished). If a listing gives a color name and a price and nothing else, it is not for a healing piercing.

Price is a rough signal, not a guarantee. An implant-grade titanium flat-back stud costs 15 to 40 dollars from a reputable maker; a card of six "hypoallergenic" studs for 8 dollars is plated base metal.

## Healing times, so you know how long the rules apply

Lobes: six to eight weeks to settle, three to six months to be robust. Cartilage: six to twelve months. For the whole of that time, the metal and shape rules above apply, and so does the cleaning routine in our [earring piercing guide](/earring-piercing-guide/): saline twice a day, hands off, no hoops, no sleeping on it.

## After it heals

A healed piercing is far more forgiving. Most people can wear sterling silver, good-quality plated pieces and a wider range of styles for a day without trouble. People with a nickel allergy stay with titanium, niobium, platinum and nickel-free gold permanently, and switch back to a titanium stud at the first sign of itching. Rotating back to implant-grade jewelry for a week calms most flare-ups.

If you want earrings that can be worn through showers, sleep and workouts for months at a time, the answer is the same as for healing: a titanium or solid gold flat-back stud, which is why the piercing world has quietly made them the everyday earring.
