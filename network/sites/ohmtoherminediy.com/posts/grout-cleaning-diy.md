Grout cleaning DIY recipes are everywhere, and they all promise the same thing: a before and after where gray lines turn white with a spray and a wipe. We wanted to know which ones are real. So we taped off three sections of the same tiled kitchen floor, the one by the back door that sees muddy boots, dog paws and whatever falls off the counter, and tested three homemade grout cleaners side by side on the same afternoon with the same brush and the same amount of scrubbing. One of them won clearly. One was fine. One is the recipe you see most often, and it did almost nothing.

This is the full test, the recipes, the safety rules (there is one combination you must never make), and the maintenance routine that has kept the floor clean since.

## First, what grout is and why it goes gray

Grout is sand, cement and water. It is porous, which means it drinks whatever touches it: mop water, cooking grease, the dirt off shoes. The gray you see is not usually mold or stain in the grout; it is dirt sitting in the pores of the surface. That matters because it tells you what works. You need something that lifts dirt out of a porous surface (a mild alkaline cleaner, oxygen bleach, or a surfactant) plus physical scrubbing. You do not need acid, which can etch cement grout, and you do not need chlorine bleach on a kitchen floor.

If your grout is actually black in the shower corners, that is mold, and the recipe below with oxygen bleach handles it, but the real fix is ventilation.

## The test setup

- Floor: 12-by-24-inch porcelain tile, sanded cement grout, originally light gray, installed about six years ago, sealed once at install and never since.
- Three test squares of about 2 by 2 feet, side by side, each with a different cleaner.
- Same stiff nylon grout brush, same two minutes of scrubbing per square, same rinse with clean water and a microfiber cloth.
- Before and after photos in the same light, plus the low-tech test: a white paper towel pressed on the wet grout after cleaning. Less gray on the towel means more dirt came out.

## Recipe 1: baking soda and hydrogen peroxide paste (the winner)

Mix half a cup of baking soda with a quarter cup of 3 percent hydrogen peroxide (the brown-bottle kind from the pharmacy) and a teaspoon of dish soap into a paste. Spread it on the grout lines with an old toothbrush or a putty knife, let it sit ten minutes, scrub, rinse.

What happened: the grout lines went from gray to the original light gray with two minutes of scrubbing. The paper towel test came back almost clean. The baking soda is a gentle abrasive, the peroxide lifts organic stains and brightens, and the dish soap carries grease away. It is also the safest recipe here for kids and pets in the house; the only caution is to keep peroxide off colored grout for more than ten minutes, as it can lighten it.

Cost: about 60 cents per batch, enough for 15 to 20 feet of grout line.

## Recipe 2: oxygen bleach solution (the fine one, and the one for mold)

Dissolve two tablespoons of oxygen bleach powder (sodium percarbonate, sold as OxiClean and store-brand equivalents) in two cups of warm water. Pour it along the grout lines so they stay wet, wait 15 minutes, scrub, rinse.

What happened: good, not quite as bright as recipe 1 on this floor, but it took less scrubbing, and in the shower test we ran afterward it was the clear winner on dark mold spots because the solution soaks in and keeps working. Oxygen bleach is color-safe on grout and tile, breaks down into soda ash and water, and is what most professional grout cleaners actually use.

Cost: about 30 cents per batch.

## Recipe 3: vinegar and baking soda (the famous one that fizzes)

Sprinkle baking soda on the grout, spray white vinegar over it, watch it fizz, scrub, rinse. This is the recipe in every viral video.

What happened: a lot of fizzing and the least improvement of the three. The chemistry explains it. The moment vinegar hits baking soda they neutralize each other into salt water and carbon dioxide. The fizz is the cleaning power leaving. What you are left with is a mild abrasive (the leftover baking soda) and a damp floor. On top of that, vinegar is an acid, and acid on cement grout, done often, slowly eats the surface and makes it more porous and harder to keep clean.

If you love vinegar, use it alone on the tile face for hard-water film, and keep it off the grout.

## The one thing you must never mix

Never combine chlorine bleach with vinegar, with ammonia, or with any other cleaner. Bleach plus vinegar releases chlorine gas; bleach plus ammonia releases chloramine. Both can send you to the emergency room in a small, closed bathroom. None of the recipes in this post use chlorine bleach, and if you ever do use it on grout, use it alone, with the window open, and rinse completely before anything else touches the floor. The Centers for Disease Control's page on [cleaning and disinfecting safely](https://www.cdc.gov/) is blunt about this, and so are we.

## The method that makes any recipe work better

The cleaner is half the result. The other half:

1. Vacuum first. Scrubbing loose grit into grout just grinds it in.
2. Work in small areas so the cleaner never dries on the grout.
3. Use a real grout brush. Stiff nylon, narrow head, long handle if it is a floor. A toothbrush is for the edges; an old one for the paste.
4. Scrub along the line, not across it, so you are pulling dirt out of the groove.
5. Rinse with clean water and dry with a cloth. Dirty rinse water settles right back into the pores.
6. Let it dry fully, overnight, before you judge the color. Wet grout always looks darker.

## Seal it, then keep it clean

Clean grout that is not sealed is gray again in a month. A penetrating grout sealer (a small bottle is 10 to 20 dollars and does a kitchen floor twice) brushed onto dry, clean grout lines and wiped off the tile after a few minutes closes the pores. Reapply once a year on a kitchen floor, every six months in a shower. Test it the way you test a sealed deck: drip water on the line. If it beads, you are sealed; if it soaks in and darkens, it is time.

After that, maintenance is boring on purpose:

- Mop with a pH-neutral cleaner, not vinegar, not bleach, not the "with bleach" multi-surface spray.
- Wipe spills on grout lines when they happen, especially coffee, red sauce and pet accidents.
- Once a month, a quick pass of recipe 1 on the high-traffic lines only.

## Results, side by side

The honest takeaway from our grout cleaning DIY test: the recipe that fizzes is the one that does the least, the pharmacy peroxide paste is the one to make, and the oxygen bleach soak is the one to keep around for the shower.
