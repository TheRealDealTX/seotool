DIY Termite Treatment: What Works, What Is a Waste, and When to Call Someone

November 12, 20267 min readBy the Oh, Mother Mine DIY team

Termite treatment DIY is one of those searches you make at 11 p.m. after finding something you wish you had not. For us it was a line of dried mud, about the width of a pencil, running up the wall behind the laundry room baseboard. If you are here for the same reason, take a breath. Termites are slow. You have weeks, not hours, to figure out what you are dealing with and whether this is a job for you or for someone with a license and a truck. This post walks through both, honestly, including the part where we decided which half to do ourselves.

A quick disclaimer, because this one matters more than our usual chalk paint warnings: these are pesticides. Read every label, keep kids and pets out of treated areas until the product says otherwise, and if anyone in the house has a respiratory condition, lean toward the bait options rather than sprays. The Environmental Protection Agency's page on termites and how to control them is the plain-language source we kept open the whole time.

First, make sure they are termites

Half of the "termite" panic online turns out to be carpenter ants or just old water damage. Three things tell you it is termites:

  • Mud tubes. Pencil-width tunnels of dried soil on foundation walls, piers, or behind baseboards. Subterranean termites build these to travel between the soil and your wood.
  • Discarded wings. Little piles of identical, clear wings on windowsills in spring. Swarmers drop them after they land.
  • Hollow wood with a honeycomb inside. Press a screwdriver into suspect trim. Termite damage has mud-lined galleries that run with the grain. Carpenter ant damage is clean and smooth.

If you have mud tubes, you have subterranean termites, which are the common kind in most of the country and the kind every product below is designed for. Drywood termites, which live inside the wood with no soil contact and leave tiny pellet-shaped droppings, are mostly a coastal and southern problem and are not a DIY job, full stop. Whole-structure fumigation is the usual fix and nobody tents their own house.

The DIY options that actually work

Bait stations around the foundation

This is the method we chose, and it is the one most professionals use too. Plastic stations go into the ground every 10 to 15 feet around the house, about a foot or two out from the foundation. Inside is a cellulose bait laced with a slow-acting growth regulator. Foraging termites find it, carry it back, and the colony declines over weeks to months.

What it costs: a 10- to 20-station consumer kit runs 80 to 200 dollars. Replacement bait cartridges are 20 to 60 a year.

What it takes: a post-hole digger or a sturdy trowel, a tape measure, and a free afternoon. Place stations where you found tubes first, then spread them around the whole perimeter. Check them monthly. When you see termites feeding, do not disturb them; that is the system working.

What it does not do: baits are slow. If you have active damage in a load-bearing area, you need a faster barrier treatment too, which is where a pro comes in.

Liquid termiticide trench along the foundation

The other proven method is a trench treatment. You dig a narrow trench 6 inches wide and 6 inches deep against the foundation, mix a non-repellent termiticide (the active ingredients to look for are fipronil or imidacloprid) with water, pour it in at the rate on the label, and backfill. Termites pass through it without detecting it, pick it up, and share it.

What it costs: a 20-ounce concentrate bottle is 40 to 80 dollars and treats 60 to 100 linear feet of foundation depending on dilution. A typical house needs two to three bottles.

What it takes: a shovel, a 5-gallon bucket, a watering can, gloves, eye protection, and more digging than you expect. Figure a full day for a small house. Where a slab or a patio meets the house, you cannot trench, and a pro would drill and inject. That is the limit of the DIY version.

Who should not do this: anyone with a well within 50 feet, a house with a sump that drains to daylight, or a yard where kids dig. The label will say so, and the label is the law.

Treating the wood you can see

For a small, exposed infestation in a non-structural spot (a porch post, a garage door frame), a borate wood treatment brushed or sprayed on bare wood will kill termites that eat it and protect the wood going forward. It only works on unfinished wood, so paint has to come off first. A quart is 20 to 35 dollars. This is a supplement, not a cure, because the colony is in the ground, not in the post.

The DIY options that are a waste of money

  • Spraying store-bought ant and roach spray on mud tubes. It kills the few termites you can see and tells the colony to route around you.
  • Orange oil, vinegar, or essential oils. There is no evidence any of these reaches a subterranean colony. They make the laundry room smell nice.
  • Diatomaceous earth. It works on insects that walk across it in dry conditions. Termites travel in sealed mud tubes and moist soil. It does nothing.
  • Nematodes. Beneficial nematodes have some lab support against termites, but field results in real yards are inconsistent, and they die in dry or hot soil. Not a plan.
  • Cardboard traps. A wet cardboard "trap" will attract termites. Then what? You have confirmed what you already knew.

The half we did ourselves and the half we did not

Here is the honest version of our termite month. We installed the bait stations ourselves, which took about three hours and cost 140 dollars. We also pulled the damaged baseboard and the bottom six inches of drywall behind the washer, which turned out to be a 40-dollar repair with a scrap of drywall and a new piece of trim, painted to match. The how to paint furniture with chalk paint crowd will be pleased to know the same brushes worked.

We did not trench. Our laundry room sits on a slab that meets a concrete patio, and the only way to treat under it is to drill through the slab every 12 inches and inject. We got three quotes. The lowest was 750 dollars for a full-perimeter liquid treatment including the drilling, with a one-year warranty. For reference, the bottles and a rented hammer drill would have been about 250, and we would have had holes in our patio and no warranty.

So we paid. Six months later the stations show no activity, the pro's annual check is 150, and the patio is intact. That split, DIY the bait and the cosmetic repair and hire the barrier, is the one we would recommend to most families.

Keeping them from coming back

This is the part that is entirely DIY and entirely free, and it matters more than any product:

  1. Fix the moisture. Termites follow water. Our DIY gutters post exists because a clogged downspout was dumping water against that exact laundry room wall. Downspout extensions, graded soil, and a dry crawl space do more than any chemical.
  2. Keep wood off the ground. Firewood 20 feet from the house and up on a rack. No mulch piled against siding; leave a 6-inch gap of bare soil or gravel. Deck posts on concrete piers, not in dirt.
  3. Close the gaps. Caulk where pipes and cables enter the foundation. Screen crawl space vents.
  4. Look once a season. Walk the foundation with a flashlight in spring and fall. Mud tubes take weeks to build, and you will catch them early.

A budget summary

  • Confirming the pest yourself: free, one screwdriver
  • Bait station kit, installed by you: 80 to 200 dollars, plus 20 to 60 a year in bait
  • Liquid trench, done by you where possible: 100 to 250 in product
  • Professional liquid barrier with drilling: 600 to 1,500 for a typical house, usually with a warranty
  • Professional bait system with monitoring: 1,000 to 2,000 to start, 250 to 400 a year
  • Repairing the damage: anywhere from 40 dollars of trim to a structural repair that needs a contractor

If you want the full budget-planner version of a repair like ours, the room makeover budget planner handles the cosmetic side. For the pest side, the rule is simple: bait and prevention are yours, drilling and structure belong to someone with a license, and the fastest fix for termites is a dry foundation.
