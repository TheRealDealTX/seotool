DIY Gutters: Can You Really Install Them Yourself? (What You Need to Know First)

Oh, Mother Mine DIY · October 26, 2026

DIY gutters come up every fall, usually right after the first big rain shows you where the old ones stopped working. The question I get from friends is always the same: can a regular person install gutters, or is this a hire-it-out job? The honest answer is that it depends on your house, your ladder comfort and whether you can make a straight line slope very slightly for forty feet. I will walk through all of it, including the parts I would not do myself.

What a gutter system actually has to do

Gutters only need to do three things. Catch the water coming off the roof, carry it sideways to a downspout, and dump it far enough from the house that it does not soak the foundation. Every DIY gutter failure is one of those three not happening. Water overshoots because the gutter is hung too low. Water pools because there is no slope. Water pours out at the corner of the house because the downspout ends there.

So before you buy anything, stand outside in the rain with an umbrella and watch. Where does water sheet off? Where does it already pool on the ground? That ten minutes is the most useful part of the whole project.

Sectional vs. seamless

What you can buy at a home center is sectional: 10-foot lengths of aluminum or vinyl that snap together with connectors and seal with gutter sealant. What a gutter company brings is seamless: a machine on the truck extrudes one continuous piece to the exact length of each run.

Seamless costs more and has fewer places to leak. Sectional is what DIY means, and it works fine if the joints are sealed properly and you accept that you will reseal one or two of them in a few years. Vinyl is lighter and easier to cut. Aluminum lasts longer and looks better. For a single-story house, I would choose aluminum sectional.

The supply list

Measure every run along the fascia and add 10 percent. Then:

  • Gutter sections, 5-inch K-style for most homes
  • End caps, one pair per run
  • Inside and outside corners where the roof turns
  • Slip joint connectors, one per joint
  • Downspout outlets, one per downspout
  • Downspouts, elbows and straps
  • Hidden hangers with screws, one every 24 inches, closer in snow areas
  • Gutter sealant, the kind rated for aluminum
  • Splash blocks or downspout extensions
  • Tin snips, a hacksaw, a drill, a chalk line, a 4-foot level, a tape measure
  • A ladder with a standoff, and a second person

That last line is not optional. Most gutter injuries are ladder injuries. A ladder standoff keeps you off the gutter and gives you a place to rest the section.

The step that decides everything: the slope

Gutters need to fall toward the downspout about a quarter inch for every 10 feet. Not more, or the gutter looks crooked from the yard. Not less, or water stands and the joints leak.

Snap a chalk line on the fascia. Start at the high end, which is the end farthest from the downspout, about an inch and a quarter below the roof edge. Drop the line a quarter inch every 10 feet to the downspout end. For a 40-foot run that is one inch of fall over the whole length. Check it with the level. Then hang every hanger on that line.

If the fascia is not straight, and on older houses it often is not, follow the chalk line, not the wood.

Putting it together

Assemble as much as you can on the ground. Attach end caps, corners and outlets to the sections on sawhorses, seal every joint inside the gutter with sealant, and let it skin over. Cut the downspout outlet hole with snips, a little smaller than the outlet, then press-fit and seal.

Then, with your helper, lift each section and hook it onto the hangers. Join sections with the slip connectors and seal them from the inside. Work from the high end down so each joint overlaps in the direction the water flows.

Downspouts go on last. Two elbows at the top bring the pipe back to the wall, straps every few feet hold it, and the bottom elbow turns the water away from the house onto an extension or splash block that carries it at least four feet out.

The parts I would hire out

Anything on a second story. The money you save is not worth the fall.

Any run over a steep roof pitch where the ladder has to lean on the gutter itself.

Houses with no fascia board, where hangers go into the rafter tails. That is a roofer's job, because the fix for a bad hanger there involves the roof edge.

Any sign that the fascia is rotten. New gutters on rotten wood fail in a season. Replace the fascia first, which is its own project.

And if the old gutters failed because the roof edge or drip edge is wrong, gutters will not fix it. The water needs to land in the gutter in the first place.

Mistakes I see in DIY gutters

  • Hangers too far apart. Snow and ice pull the gutter loose over one winter.
  • No slope, or slope the wrong way. The telltale is a green stripe of algae in the low spot.
  • Downspout at the corner closest to the door, dumping on the walkway, which then ices in January.
  • Sealing from the outside. Sealant belongs inside the gutter, where the water is.
  • Forgetting the extensions. A downspout that ends at the foundation moves the problem from the roof to the basement.

Gutter guards

If leaves are the reason the old gutters failed, guards are worth considering, but they are a second project. Install the gutters, run them through one season, then decide. Mesh guards that screw to the front lip are the DIY-friendly kind. Full-cover guards are generally installed by a company.

Time and money

A single-story ranch with 120 feet of gutter and four downspouts is a full weekend for two people the first time. Materials for aluminum sectional run roughly 500 to 900 depending on brand and the number of corners. A seamless install for the same house is usually 1,200 to 2,000 installed. The DIY saving is real, and so is the weekend.

Keeping them working

Clean them twice a year, after the leaves drop and after the spring seed pods. Check the downspout extensions every time you mow. Look at the joints after the first hard rain each spring. The Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety has a short homeowner checklist for roof and water intrusion that I keep on the fridge, because most of the damage in a house starts with water that was supposed to be somewhere else.

If this project is bigger than you want, there is no shame in that. Spend the weekend on something that is actually fun, like the terracotta pot makeover or a farmhouse wreath, and let someone with a truck-mounted machine do the gutters. The goal is dry walls and a dry basement, not a merit badge.
