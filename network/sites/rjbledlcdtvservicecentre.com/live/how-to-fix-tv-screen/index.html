How to Fix a TV Screen: What Is Repairable and What Is Not

How to fix a TV screen, fault by fault. Which screen problems are a board or cable repair, which are the panel, what you can safely check at home, what a technician does, and the honest point where a TV is not worth fixing.

Book a repair Email us

How to fix a TV screen is the question behind most of the calls we take, and the honest answer is that it depends entirely on what the screen is doing. "Screen" means the panel, the backlight behind it, the boards that drive it and the cables between them, and a fault in any of those shows up on the glass. Some of those faults are a 700-rupee cable reseat. Some are a board swap done the same day. Some are the panel, and a panel on a mid-size TV costs more than a new TV. This guide goes symptom by symptom, says which is which, tells you what you can safely check yourself, and explains what a technician does on the bench so you know what you are paying for.

One rule before anything else: if the glass is cracked, there is nothing to fix. A cracked panel cannot be repaired, only replaced, and for anything under 55 inches replacement rarely makes sense. Everything below assumes the glass is intact.

Before you call anyone: the five-minute checks

These rule out the faults that are not faults at all.

  1. Try the TV's own menu. If the problem (lines, colour cast, flicker) shows on the menu overlay, the fault is in the TV. If the menu looks perfect, the problem is the source: box, cable, HDMI port, streaming stick.
  2. Swap the HDMI cable and port. A failing cable gives sparkles, dropouts, pink or green tints, and a black screen that comes and goes.
  3. Unplug the TV from the wall for two minutes, then restart. This clears a frozen main board more often than anyone expects.
  4. Check picture settings. "Eco" or "energy saving" modes dim the backlight. A screen that looks dark but fine is often just this.
  5. Shine a torch at the dark screen with the TV on. If you can faintly see the picture, the panel and boards are working and the backlight has failed. That one diagnosis changes everything, as we explain below.

Write down the result of each. The call to a service centre goes much better with "picture visible with a torch, no backlight" than with "the screen is dead."

Fault by fault

Black screen, sound works, picture visible with a torch

This is the backlight. On an LED TV the backlight is a set of LED strips behind the panel, driven by a circuit on the power board. One failed LED usually takes the whole strip or the whole set down, because they are wired in series and the driver shuts off on fault.

Repairable: yes, and it is our most common job. The TV comes apart, the panel is lifted out (the delicate step), the failed strips are replaced, and the driver is checked. In Kolkata, a backlight repair on a 32- to 43-inch set is 1,800 to 3,500 rupees; a 50- to 65-inch set is 3,000 to 6,000. Not a home job: lifting the panel without cracking it takes practice and a clean bench.

Black screen, no sound, no standby light

The power supply board. Capacitors, a blown fuse, a failed switching transistor. Repairable, often at component level, 1,200 to 3,000 rupees. If the standby light is on but the TV will not start, it may be the main board instead; a technician tells which by checking the voltages the power board sends out.

Vertical lines or bands

A single thin vertical line is usually a source driver on the panel edge, which is a panel fault. Multiple vertical bands, a vertical strip of wrong colour, or lines that change with pressure near the bottom edge are often the ribbon connectors between the T-con board and the panel, which clean up and reseat. A wide vertical band of noise or a double image is commonly the T-con board. Reseat: 500 to 900. T-con: 2,000 to 4,500. Single fixed line: panel, see below.

Horizontal lines

One fixed horizontal line is a gate driver on the panel edge. Several lines, flickering lines, or lines that come and go with warm-up point to the T-con or its ribbons. We wrote the whole decision tree in blue horizontal line on a TV screen; it applies to any colour of line.

Half the screen dark, or the picture split

Half the backlight strips (one side failed) if the dark half shows a faint picture with a torch; otherwise one of the two source driver banks on the panel, or the T-con. Backlight: repairable. Driver bank: panel.

Colour wrong: pink, green, blue tint, negative picture

Try the source first. If the menu is tinted too, it is the T-con or the LVDS cable from the main board. Both are repairable; cable reseats are cheap, T-con replacement moderate.

Flickering

Backlight driver (a failing capacitor on the power board makes the LEDs pulse), a loose LVDS cable, or on older sets a failing inverter. All repairable. If the flicker is only on dark scenes and the TV is a recent OLED or local-dimming LED, it may be the TV's dimming algorithm, which is a setting, not a fault.

Dead pixels, bright spots, dark patches

A handful of dead or stuck pixels is the panel; nothing fixes them and manufacturers consider a few acceptable. Bright white spots the size of a coin are the reflector lenses on the backlight LEDs falling off, which is repairable (the lenses are re-glued when the strips are serviced). Dark cloudy patches on an LED TV are usually a bent frame or a panel pressed by a wall bracket: loosen the bracket and see. Burn-in on an OLED is the panel.

Image retention or ghosting that fades

Normal on OLED after a static image; run the panel refresh from the menu. Permanent retention is burn-in, which is the panel.

What the technician actually does

On the bench, a screen fault goes through the same order every time:

  1. Confirm the symptom and check the source, cables and settings, because a surprising number of "faulty TVs" are a bad HDMI lead.
  2. Open the back, inspect for burnt components, bulged capacitors, scorch marks and loose ribbons.
  3. Measure the power board's outputs with the TV on. Missing or sagging rails point to the power board; good rails point downstream.
  4. Test the backlight strips directly with an LED tester, strip by strip.
  5. Swap or bypass the T-con with a known-good unit for that panel.
  6. Only then blame the panel, and show the customer why.

What you can do at home, and what you should not

Safe at home: all the five-minute checks above, loosening a wall bracket, cleaning the screen properly (our screen cleaning guide covers what not to spray), and checking the vents are not blocked.

Not at home, unless you have done it before: opening the back (the power board holds a charge after unplugging), reseating ribbons (the connectors tear), lifting a panel (one twist cracks it), and buying a board online by guessing the part number (T-con boards match the panel code inside, not the TV model).

For the curious, iFixit's television repair guides show the disassembly for many models and are a fair picture of why we charge for the backlight job.

When a TV screen is not worth fixing

Our rule, and we apply it on the phone before a visit: if the repair would cost more than 40 percent of an equivalent new TV, and the set is over five years old, buy new. In practice that means:

  • Backlight, power board, T-con, cable faults: almost always worth repairing on any TV over 32 inches.
  • Single fixed line, dead pixels, burn-in, cracked glass: panel faults. Worth replacing only on large, recent, premium sets where a panel is available at a sensible price.
  • Any fault on a TV under 32 inches: compare the quote to a new set before you spend on a visit.
