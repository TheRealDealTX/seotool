What causes flat feet depends on one question doctors ask first and the internet usually skips: did you always have them, or did they develop? Flat feet you were born with are a shape, extremely common, and for most people never a problem. Flat feet that developed in adulthood are a change, and a change in the shape of a foot has a reason, which is usually a tendon on the inside of the ankle slowly giving up. The two groups get different advice. This guide walks through the causes in both, what each one means for pain and shoes, and which causes you can actually do something about.

If you are not sure which group you are in, our [flat feet vs normal feet](/flat-feet-vs-normal-feet/) explainer has the two-minute wet test and the standing test that tell you whether your arch is flexible (it appears when you sit or stand on your toes) or rigid (it never appears).

## Flat feet you were born with

### Flexible flat feet in children

Nearly all toddlers have flat feet. The arch is padded with fat, the ligaments are loose and the muscles that lift the arch have not developed. Most children develop a visible arch between ages 3 and 6, and some simply never do. A flexible flat foot in a child, one where the arch appears on tiptoe, is a normal variant, not a condition. It runs in families. Pediatric guidance is consistent: no treatment, no special shoes and no inserts for a painless flexible flat foot in a child, because none of them change the eventual shape of the foot and none are needed.

What to watch for: pain, a foot that is rigid (no arch on tiptoe), one foot very different from the other, or a child who avoids running. Those are reasons for a pediatrician or a podiatrist, not for an insole from the pharmacy.

### Ligament laxity

Some people are born with loose ligaments throughout the body. They are the ones who can bend their thumb to their forearm. Loose ligaments let the arch spread under load, which is a flexible flat foot that persists into adulthood. It usually causes no pain. When it does, the answer is strength, not support: the muscles have to do the job the ligaments are not doing, which is what our [flat feet exercises](/flat-feet-exercises/) routine is built for.

### Tarsal coalition

A less common cause, and the one behind most rigid flat feet in teenagers. Two of the bones in the back of the foot are joined by bone, cartilage or fibrous tissue from birth, which locks the arch down. It often shows up as a stiff, painful flat foot around ages 10 to 16 when the connection hardens. This one needs a doctor; it is diagnosed on X-ray or CT and treated with rest, bracing or surgery depending on the severity.

### Other congenital causes

Vertical talus, a rare condition where one bone of the foot points the wrong way, gives a rigid "rocker-bottom" foot from birth and is treated in infancy. Conditions that affect connective tissue (Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Marfan syndrome) or muscle tone (cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy) can also produce flat feet, and in those cases the feet are managed as part of the broader condition.

## Flat feet that developed later

This is the group that matters most for adults who notice a change, and the common thread is the posterior tibial tendon.

### Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction

The posterior tibial tendon runs behind the inside ankle bone and attaches under the arch. It is the main tendon holding the arch up when you walk. When it weakens, stretches or tears, the arch sags, the heel tilts outward and the front of the foot drifts out. Doctors call the result adult-acquired flatfoot, and posterior tibial tendon dysfunction is by far its most common cause.

It usually starts with pain and swelling on the inside of the ankle, worse after standing or walking, before the arch visibly drops. Risk factors are clear: being over 40, being a woman, being overweight, having diabetes or high blood pressure, and a history of ankle injury. Caught early, in the stage where the tendon is inflamed but intact, it responds to rest, an ankle brace or a custom orthotic, calf stretching and strengthening. Caught late, when the arch has collapsed and stiffened, it can need surgery. The difference between those two outcomes is often a few months, which is why a new flat foot with inside-ankle pain is a podiatrist visit, not a shoe-shopping trip.

### Weight

Body weight loads the arch with every step, and the arch is held by ligaments and a tendon that have limits. Gaining weight, especially quickly, is a common reason an arch that was holding stops holding. The good news is that it is one of the few causes that reverses, at least partly, as weight comes down and as the muscles strengthen. Our [calorie deficit guide](/how-to-calculate-a-calorie-deficit/) is the practical starting point most readers use.

### Pregnancy

Pregnancy combines weight gain with relaxin, a hormone that loosens ligaments throughout the body in preparation for birth. Many women find their feet flatten and widen during pregnancy, sometimes permanently, sometimes by half a shoe size. It is normal, it is usually painless, and supportive shoes during the third trimester reduce the strain. The feet often do not fully go back, which is worth knowing before you throw out the comfortable shoes.

### Injury

A broken ankle, a torn ligament, a dislocation in the midfoot (the Lisfranc injury that sidelines athletes) or a fracture that heals in a slightly wrong position can all flatten a foot by changing how the bones sit. An arch that dropped after a specific injury should be assessed, because the fix is often different from the tendon problem.

### Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis attacks the small joints of the foot and the tendons around them, and flat feet are a common result. Osteoarthritis in the midfoot can do the same more slowly. In both cases the flat foot is a symptom of the joint disease, and treating the arthritis is the first step.

### Nerve and muscle conditions

Damage to the nerves that control the foot, from diabetes or from a nerve injury, weakens the muscles that support the arch. Charcot foot, a complication of diabetic neuropathy, can collapse the arch rapidly and painlessly and is a medical emergency. Any sudden change in foot shape in someone with diabetes is a same-week doctor visit.

### Age

Tendons lose elasticity and ligaments stretch over decades. Many people in their 60s and 70s have lower arches than they had at 30, without any single cause. This is the "wear" version of flat feet, and it is managed, not cured: supportive shoes with a firm heel, which our [women's shoes for flat feet](/womens-shoes-for-flat-feet/) guide covers in detail, an insert if it helps, and enough strength work to keep the foot stable.

## Which causes you can change

| Cause | Can you change it? | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Childhood flexible flat foot | No, and you do not need to | Nothing unless painful |
| Ligament laxity | Partly | Strength work, firm shoes |
| Tarsal coalition | Needs a doctor | Imaging, bracing, sometimes surgery |
| Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction | Yes, if caught early | Brace or orthotic, calf stretches, strengthening, podiatrist |
| Weight | Yes | Gradual weight loss, strength work |
| Pregnancy | Partly, with time | Supportive shoes, patience |
| Injury | Depends | Assessment, sometimes surgery |
| Arthritis | Manage the disease | Rheumatologist, supportive footwear |
| Nerve and muscle conditions | Manage the condition | Specialist care, protective footwear |
| Age | Manage | Firm shoes, inserts, strength |

## When flat feet need a professional

Flat feet alone do not. Flat feet plus any of these do:

- Pain on the inside of the ankle or under the arch that lasts more than a few weeks.
- An arch that has visibly dropped in the last year, especially on one side.
- A rigid flat foot at any age.
- Flat feet in someone with diabetes, particularly with warmth, swelling or a change in shape.
- A child with pain, stiffness or a limp.

The American Podiatric Medical Association's [page on flat feet](https://www.apma.org/) describes what an exam involves and when imaging is used. For most adults the first visit is an exam, a look at your shoes, and a conversation about when the change started, which is why knowing the cause matters before you go.

The short version of what causes flat feet: if you have always had them and they do not hurt, nothing needs fixing, and strength plus good shoes is enough. If they are new, the most likely cause is the tendon under the arch, and the earlier it is treated the less it costs you.
