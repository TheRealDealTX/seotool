Rain garden design is the rare garden idea that solves a problem before it decorates anything. Every London house sends roof water down a pipe into a drain, and increasingly that drain is overwhelmed: the summer cloudburst that floods the side return, the winter weeks when the lawn stays a bog. A rain garden intercepts that water, holds it in a shallow planted basin for a day, and lets it soak into the ground where it falls. The planting that results is one of the most generous in the garden, because the bed is watered for free, and it is one of the most interesting to design, because the plants must tolerate standing water on Tuesday and dry gravel by Friday. This is how we approach one, from the roof area to the last iris.

## What a rain garden is, and is not

A rain garden is a shallow depression, typically 100 to 200 millimetres deep, with free-draining soil beneath, planted with species that tolerate periodic flooding. Water enters from a downpipe or a surface channel, pools briefly, and infiltrates. It is not a pond: a rain garden should be dry within 24 to 48 hours of rain. If water stands longer than that, the soil below is too heavy or the basin is too small, and you have built a mosquito nursery.

It is also not a bog garden. Bog plants want permanently wet feet. Rain garden plants want wet feet occasionally and are otherwise ordinary border perennials with deep roots. That distinction decides the plant list.

## Start with the roof

Every rain garden is sized from the roof that feeds it. Measure the footprint of the roof section draining to the downpipe you will intercept: for a typical London terrace, the rear roof slope feeding one downpipe is 25 to 40 square metres. The rule of thumb used by the Royal Horticultural Society and most sustainable drainage guides is a basin area of 20 percent of the contributing roof area on free-draining soil, and 30 to 40 percent on London clay. So a 30-square-metre roof section wants a rain garden of 6 square metres on sand and gravel, and 9 to 12 square metres on clay.

If that is more space than you have, intercept part of the flow. A diverter fitted to the downpipe sends the first flush to the garden and the rest to the drain once the garden is full. It is a 30-pound part and it makes a rain garden possible in a 5-metre-wide plot.

## Where to put it

Three rules, in order:

1. At least 3 metres from the house, and downhill of it. Water soaking in against a foundation is the one way a rain garden does harm.
2. Not over a soakaway, a sewer run or a tree's main root plate. A drain survey or the sewer map from Thames Water will show the first two.
3. Where it will be seen. A rain garden is a planted feature, not infrastructure. The side return, the view from the kitchen, the bed beside the terrace. We have put one where a lawn used to drown and it became the best bed in the garden.

Test the soil before you commit. Dig a hole 300 millimetres deep and 300 wide, fill it with water, and time the drop. If it empties within 24 hours, the site drains well enough. If it is still half full the next morning, you are on clay and will need to replace the subsoil or go for a larger, shallower basin with an engineered overflow.

## The build

A rain garden for a London garden, in section from the bottom up:

- Excavate 400 to 500 millimetres below finished level across the whole basin.
- On clay, lay 150 millimetres of coarse gravel (20 to 40 millimetre) as a drainage reservoir, with a perforated pipe through it leading to an overflow if infiltration is poor.
- Above that, 250 to 300 millimetres of rain garden soil: roughly equal parts sharp sand, topsoil and well-rotted compost. This is the layer that holds water for the plants and lets the excess through. Do not use peat-based compost; it waterlogs.
- Shape the surface as a shallow dish, deepest at the centre, 100 to 150 millimetres below the surrounding ground. The sides should be gentle, no steeper than 1 in 3, so soil does not slump.
- Mulch with 50 to 75 millimetres of gravel or bark. Gravel looks better where water enters and stops the inlet scouring.

The inlet is the part most people get wrong. Water from a downpipe arrives fast, and if it lands on bare soil it carves a channel and dumps silt. Bring it in through a rill of setts or a short run of cobbles, or let the downpipe discharge onto a flat stone that spreads the flow. Our [raised garden layouts](/blog/raised-garden-design-layout.html) note shows how a channel like this can double as a path edge.

The overflow is the part people forget. Every rain garden needs a defined low point on its downhill rim where excess water leaves in a storm, and that point should lead somewhere harmless: a lawn, a gravel area, an existing drain. Set it 50 millimetres below the rest of the rim and line it with stone.

## Planting for wet feet and dry heads

Plants in a rain garden experience three zones, and the plan should follow them.

### The base, which floods

Plants here will stand in water for a day or two several times a year and dry out between. Deep-rooted, tough, and the stars of the garden in our view.

- Iris sibirica, Siberian iris: fine blue or white flower in early summer, grassy foliage, happiest with occasional flooding.
- Carex elata 'Aurea', Bowles' golden sedge, and Carex pendula for bigger plots: evergreen structure that does not mind standing water.
- Lythrum salicaria, purple loosestrife, and its cultivar 'Blush': late summer spires that bees cover.
- Persicaria amplexicaulis: red or pink tails from July to the frosts, unbothered by wet or dry.
- Filipendula ulmaria, meadowsweet: for a wilder planting, scented cream froth in June.

### The slopes, which get wet briefly

Ordinary border perennials with some tolerance for a soaking.

- Hemerocallis, daylilies, in the subtler yellows and apricots.
- Geum rivale and its hybrids for spring.
- Astrantia major for shade on the north side of the basin.
- Deschampsia cespitosa, tufted hair grass, for movement and winter seedheads.
- Eupatorium (now Eutrochium) for height at the back of a larger basin.

### The rim, which stays dry

This is where the rain garden meets the rest of the border, and the planting should blend: Salvia nemorosa, Nepeta, Stipa tenuissima and the plants of a normal sunny London bed. See our note on [naturalistic planting with grasses and perennials](/blog/naturalistic-planting-with-grasses-and-perennials.html) for combinations that run from the dry rim into the wet base without a visible join.

Avoid: lavender, rosemary and most Mediterranean plants anywhere near the base (they rot), shrubs with shallow roots that will be undermined by scouring, and anything invasive in watercourses. Where a rain garden overflows toward a drain, be careful with vigorous water-loving plants that can spread.

## A worked example

A Highgate rear garden, 6 metres wide, London clay. One downpipe draining 32 square metres of roof. We fitted a diverter and built a 9-square-metre rain garden in the shape of a long oval beside the terrace, 150 millimetres deep, with a gravel reservoir and an overflow to the lawn. Water enters along a line of reclaimed setts from the downpipe. Planting: Iris sibirica 'Perry's Blue' and Carex elata 'Aurea' through the base, Persicaria 'Firetail' and Deschampsia on the slopes, a run of Nepeta and Stipa along the rim meeting the existing border. Two years on, the basin has flooded perhaps a dozen times a year and drained within a day each time, the terrace no longer puddles, and the iris in June is the reason visitors ask who did the garden.

Cost for that build, excavation and soil and plants but not the terrace, was in the region of 2,500 to 4,000 pounds. A smaller DIY version in an existing bed, with a diverter and plants, can be done for a few hundred.

## Looking after it

The first year matters. Water the plants in dry spells until they root, because the rain garden only waters itself when it rains. Pull silt out of the inlet after big storms. Cut back the perennials in late winter, not autumn, so the seedheads stand through December. Every few years, check that the basin still drains within two days; if not, the surface has silted and the top 50 millimetres of mulch and soil want replacing.

The Royal Horticultural Society's [rain garden guidance](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-features/rain-gardens) is the best single reference for sizing and the regulatory side, including when a rain garden counts toward the sustainable drainage requirements that planning increasingly asks for.

A rain garden is one of the few features we design that pays for itself twice: it solves a drainage problem the garden already has, and it gives you a bed that looks after its own watering.
