Garden Design for a Small Backyard: The Three Layouts That Work

Garden design for a small backyard, reduced to the three layouts that reliably work in a London plot under 8 metres wide: the diagonal, the room sequence and the single bold shape. Proportions, materials, planting and the mistakes that shrink a small garden.

27 November 2026·Design·7 min read

Garden design for a small backyard is the problem we are asked to solve more than any other, and after enough of them a pattern emerges. Almost every small London garden that works is one of three layouts. Not three styles, which are endless, but three ways of organising the ground: the diagonal, the sequence of rooms, and the single bold shape. Each solves the same difficulty, which is that a rectangle 5 to 8 metres wide and 10 to 20 metres long, seen from one window, reads as a corridor unless something interrupts it. This note sets out the three, how to choose between them, the proportions and materials that make each work, and the handful of mistakes that make a small garden feel smaller.

What a small garden needs from its layout

Before the three, the brief they all answer:

  • A reason to stop looking at the back fence. The eye should land on something before the boundary.
  • A destination. A seat, a table or a studio at the far end gives the garden a journey, and a journey makes a short distance feel longer.
  • Somewhere to sit in the sun and somewhere to sit in the shade, which in a north-south plot usually means one near the house and one at the far end.
  • Planting with height. Small gardens that are all low planting feel like a car park; one tree or a tall multi-stem shrub changes the volume. Our note on choosing trees for a small garden covers the candidates.
  • Storage and the bins, placed so they are reached from the house and not seen from it.

Layout one: the diagonal

Turn the main axis of the garden 45 degrees. Paths, the edges of paving, the long sides of beds and the lawn, if there is one, all run corner to corner rather than parallel to the fences.

Why it works: the longest line in a rectangle is the diagonal. A 6-by-12-metre plot has fences 12 metres long and a diagonal of 13.4. More usefully, a diagonal layout means you never look straight down a fence line, and the beds in the two acute corners become deep enough for real planting, which hides the boundary altogether.

Proportions: paving and lawn on the diagonal, beds filling the triangles left in the corners. Keep the hard surfaces to about 40 percent of the area; the triangular beds take the rest and want to be at least 1.5 metres deep at their widest.

Materials: large-format paving slabs laid on the diagonal grid, or a single poured material (resin-bound gravel, polished concrete) that takes the angle without cuts showing. Avoid small units; the cuts at the fence line multiply.

Planting: tall at the two far corners, medium along the sides, low at the house. Multi-stem amelanchier or a small acer in each far triangle, with evergreen structure beneath so the corners read as mass in winter.

Suits: narrow plots, plots with a straight view from the kitchen to the back fence, gardens where the owners want one generous terrace.

Layout two: the sequence of rooms

Divide the length into two or three spaces, each with its own floor and its own purpose, separated by planting, a change of level or a screen.

Why it works: you cannot take the whole garden in at once, so your mind assumes it is bigger than it is. A terrace by the house, a planted middle you pass through, a seat or a studio at the end: three rooms in 15 metres. Our trellis and screen note covers the dividers.

Proportions: the rooms should not be equal. A common split for a 15-metre garden is 5 metres of terrace, 4 metres of planted transition, 6 metres of lawn or second terrace with the destination. The openings between rooms should be narrower than the rooms, 1.2 to 1.8 metres, so passing through feels like arrival.

Materials: a different floor in each room says "this is a new place" more clearly than any screen. Stone terrace, then a gravel or bark path through the planting, then lawn or a timber deck at the end.

Planting: the middle room does the work. It should be dense enough to hide most of the far room from the house, which means planting to 1.5 metres high at least, with a tree or tall grasses for a screen that moves. The far room can be simpler, because it is seen from inside itself.

Suits: longer plots (over 12 metres), gardens that need to serve several uses (eating, children, a studio), owners who like a garden to be explored rather than viewed.

Layout three: the single bold shape

One large, simple shape in the centre, a circle, an oval or a wide rectangle, with planting filling everything around it to the boundaries.

Why it works: a strong shape gives the eye something to settle on, and the planting that surrounds it hides the fences so the garden's edge becomes green rather than timber. A circle in a rectangle also creates four deep corner beds, like the diagonal, without the angles.

Proportions: the shape should be big. In a 6-metre-wide garden the circle wants to be 4 metres across, leaving a metre of planting each side at the narrowest point and much more in the corners. A timid 2.5-metre circle in the middle of a lawn is the version everyone has seen and nobody likes.

Materials: the shape can be lawn (easy to mow if it is a circle), paving (a circular terrace with a table in the middle is the simplest good small garden there is) or gravel with a tree in the centre. The edge must be crisp: steel edging, a sett border or the clean cut of a slab.

Planting: layered to the fences, with the tallest plants at the back corners and climbers on every boundary. Because the shape is calm, the planting can be exuberant; this is the layout for the naturalistic planting with grasses we are often asked for.

Suits: squarer plots, gardens seen mainly from above (a first-floor kitchen or a flat), owners who want one place to sit and a lot of planting to look at.

Choosing between them

Ask three questions. How long is the plot compared to its width? Under twice as long, the bold shape; over twice, the diagonal or the rooms. How many things must it do? One or two, the shape or the diagonal; three or more, the rooms. How is it seen? From a kitchen at ground level, the diagonal and the rooms reward the view; from above, the bold shape reads best.

Where the answers conflict, the diagonal is the compromise that most often works, which is why it is the layout we draw most.

Levels, light and the back fence

A change of level, even 150 millimetres, is the most effective small-garden device after the layout itself. One step up to the far room or down to a sunken terrace by the house adds a horizon line, and the retaining edge doubles as a seat. Our raised garden layouts note shows how raised beds can make the level change.

Light decides where the seats go. Map the sun at midday and at 6 p.m. in June before placing anything; a north-facing plot wants the main seat at the far end where the evening sun lands, a south-facing one wants it near the house with shade from a tree or a sail.

The back fence should disappear. Paint it a dark colour (near-black or deep green), train climbers across it, and put the tallest planting in front of it. A garden whose boundary you cannot see has no measurable size.

The mistakes that shrink a small garden

  • A path down the middle. It divides the garden into two useless strips and points straight at the fence.
  • Too many materials. Three is the limit: one paving, one path or gravel, one timber. Four and it looks like a showroom.
  • Small plants everywhere. One big thing is worth twenty small ones.
  • A lawn too small to use. Under 3 by 3 metres, replace it with paving or planting; a postage stamp of grass is a maintenance task, not a garden.
  • A fence left orange. Treated timber in its raw colour is the single most common reason a small garden looks cheap.
  • Furniture bought before the layout. The table sets the terrace size; choose the table first, then draw the terrace 1 metre wider on all sides.

The Royal Horticultural Society's small garden design advice is a sensible companion to this note, particularly on plant choice for confined spaces. For a worked example of the room sequence in a London terrace garden, our Belsize town garden is the one we show most.
