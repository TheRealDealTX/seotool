Trellis Design for a Garden: Screens, Walls and What to Grow on Them

Trellis design for a garden, from a London planting studio. Choosing between screens, wall trellis and freestanding frames, proportions that suit small plots, materials that age well and the climbers worth the space.

13 November 2026·Design·8 min read

Trellis design for a garden is usually treated as an afterthought: a panel bought on a Saturday, screwed to a fence on Sunday, and resented by the following spring when the clematis has pulled it off the post. We think it deserves the same attention as a path or a bed edge, because in a London garden trellis is often the largest vertical element you will add. It decides where the eye stops, how much sky you borrow, and whether the planting has a frame to grow into or a fence to fall off. This is how we approach it, from the first question (what is the trellis for) to the last (what will climb it).

Decide what the trellis is doing

Every trellis we specify does one of four jobs, and the design follows the job.

  • Screening. Hiding a neighbour's extension, a shed, the bins, or an overlooked seat. The trellis needs density, height and a plant that holds its leaves.
  • Dividing. Separating a dining terrace from a lawn, or a working area from the ornamental garden. Here you want something you can see through, so the garden still reads as one space.
  • Dressing a wall. Giving a flat boundary wall depth and letting a climber cover it without the mortar suffering.
  • Framing. Marking a threshold with an arch or a pair of posts, so a path has a beginning.

Mixing jobs is where trellis goes wrong. A screen that is also expected to be see-through will do neither well. Be honest about what you need to hide, and let the rest of the garden stay open. Our notes on designing a small courtyard garden cover why openness matters more, not less, as a plot shrinks.

Proportion: the rule of the top third

Trellis fixed to the top of a fence is the commonest arrangement in London, and the commonest mistake is making it too tall. A 1.8-metre fence with 600 millimetres of trellis above it reads as a wall with a hat. The same fence with 300 to 450 millimetres of trellis reads as a fence that has been finished properly.

For a freestanding screen, think in thirds. If the screen is 2 metres high, the bottom third can be solid or densely planted, the middle third is where the eye lands and where the trellis pattern should be clearest, and the top third should be the lightest, so the sky comes through. A screen that is uniformly dense from ground to top is a fence by another name.

Width matters as much. A single 1.8-metre panel standing alone looks like it is waiting for the rest of the fence. Two or three panels with a gap, or a single panel flanked by planting that rises to meet it, feel intentional. In a narrow town garden we often run a screen to about two-thirds of the width and leave a gap, so the space beyond is implied rather than closed off.

Pattern: square, diamond or slatted

Square trellis with openings of 100 to 150 millimetres is the quiet choice. It disappears once planted and suits most London houses, Victorian and modern alike. Diamond trellis is more decorative, catches more light and shadow, and looks best unplanted or lightly planted against a painted wall. Horizontal slatted screens, with 30- to 40-millimetre battens and gaps to match, are the contemporary option. They give excellent privacy from a seated position while letting light through, and they hold their line without a climber.

A note on slatted screens: they want a plant that will thread through rather than smother. Star jasmine and the smaller clematis are right. A rampant rose or a wisteria will hide the slats you paid for within three seasons.

Materials that age well

We specify three materials, in roughly this order.

Western red cedar or larch, left to silver. Cedar is light, straight and naturally durable, and it weathers to a soft grey that sits well with brick and stone. Larch is cheaper, a little heavier, and slightly more variable in colour. Both will last 15 to 25 years untreated if the bottom rail is kept off the ground.

Painted or stained softwood. A planed softwood trellis finished in a dark colour (we use a near-black or a deep green more than any other) recedes visually and makes planting glow against it. The trade is maintenance: a recoat every four to six years. Treated softwood left raw goes an unhappy orange and then grey; it is the budget choice and it looks like one.

Powder-coated steel. Thin steel frames with tensioned wire or mesh give the lightest possible structure, which is valuable where a screen must not feel heavy. The cost is three to five times timber, and in a small garden the lightness is often worth it. Steel also lets you fix a trellis to a wall on stand-offs with almost nothing visible once the climber is up.

Whatever the material, two details are non-negotiable. Posts in the ground go in concrete with the timber set on a post shoe, not buried; a buried post rots at the soil line in a decade. And a trellis against a wall needs 40 to 50 millimetres of air behind it, on battens or stand-offs, so the stems can pass behind and the wall can breathe.

Fixing a trellis to a wall

London brick walls, especially older ones with lime mortar, do not want a climber on them directly. Self-clinging plants pull the face off soft mortar and leave marks when removed. A trellis on battens solves this and gives you a way to take the whole plant down when the wall needs pointing.

Fix horizontal battens at 600-millimetre centres with sleeve anchors into the brick, never the mortar joints. Screw the trellis to the battens. For a wall-mounted wire system instead of trellis, run horizontal wires at 300 to 450 millimetre spacing on vine eyes, tensioned with turnbuckles; this is what we use for wall-trained shrubs and climbing roses, where the pattern of the trellis would fight the pattern of the plant.

What to grow on it

The plant should be chosen with the trellis, not after. Three questions decide it: do you need it evergreen, how much sun does the trellis get, and how much are you willing to prune.

Evergreen and tidy, for screens

  • Trachelospermum jasminoides, star jasmine. Glossy, scented, slow enough to manage, happiest in sun or light shade. The default London screen plant for good reason.
  • Clematis armandii. Fast, evergreen, early white flowers. It wants a big trellis and a hard hand after flowering, or it becomes a thatch.
  • Pyracantha, wall-trained. Not a climber but trains flat on wires or trellis, with berries from autumn into winter. Excellent in shade.

Deciduous and generous, for walls

  • Climbing roses on wires or wide-spaced trellis: 'Madame Alfred Carrière' for a north wall, 'Gertrude Jekyll' for scent in sun.
  • Hydrangea anomala subsp. petiolaris for a shaded wall; it self-clings, so give it a trellis only if the wall is soft.
  • Vitis coignetiae, the crimson glory vine, for autumn colour on a large pergola or a long run of trellis. Not for a small panel.

Light threaders, for slatted screens and dividers

  • Clematis viticella cultivars, cut to the ground each winter, so the screen is clear from January to April.
  • Lathyrus odoratus, sweet pea, for a summer only. Cheap, scented, gone by September, which suits a garden that wants its light back in winter.
  • Akebia quinata, the chocolate vine, semi-evergreen and quick, for somewhere you want cover in a hurry and do not mind cutting back hard.

The Royal Horticultural Society keeps a sensible guide to choosing climbers by aspect and vigour, and it is worth ten minutes before you buy, because the gap between a climber's catalogue height and its real appetite is where most trellis fails.

Three arrangements we return to

A finishing band. 300 millimetres of square cedar trellis along the top of an existing close-board fence, with star jasmine planted at 1.5-metre intervals along the base. Within three years the fence line is a green wall and the garden feels a metre wider. Cost for a 10-metre run, fitted, is typically 600 to 1,100 pounds plus plants.

A screen with a gap. Two slatted panels, each 1.2 metres wide and 1.8 metres high, set 600 millimetres apart on steel post shoes, screening a dining terrace from the house. Clematis viticella threads through; a multi-stem amelanchier stands in the gap. The screen reads as furniture, not boundary. Budget 1,200 to 2,500 pounds fitted in timber, double in steel.

A dressed wall. Black-painted softwood trellis on battens covering a tired brick boundary in a Belsize Park garden, planted with a white climbing rose and a late clematis so the wall carries flower from June to October. We described the planting approach in our note on designing a garden for year-round interest; the trellis is what makes the succession legible.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Trellis fixed flat to a fence with no gap. The plant has nowhere to go and the fence rots behind it.
  • Diamond trellis with a vigorous climber. The pattern vanishes and you have paid for a fence.
  • A screen the full width of a small garden. It halves the space; stop short.
  • Buried posts. Post shoes cost a few pounds and add a decade.
  • Planting the climber in the dry strip at the foot of a wall. Plant 300 to 450 millimetres out, lean the cane in, and water for the first two summers.

Trellis is cheap relative to walls, paving and trees, and it is the quickest way to add height and privacy to a London plot. Designed with the same care as the rest of the garden, it stops being a fixture and becomes the frame the planting grows into.
