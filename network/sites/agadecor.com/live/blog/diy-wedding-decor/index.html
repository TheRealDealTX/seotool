DIY Wedding Decor That Still Looks Designed: What to Rent and What to Make

AGA Décor · October 26, 2026

DIY wedding decor is not a budget category so much as a decision about where your time goes in the three months before the wedding. We design and install decor for weddings across Chicago and the North Shore, and we see DIY work every weekend, some of it wonderful. The couples who are happiest with it made a clear split early: a short list of things they would make themselves, and everything else rented or hired. This is the split we recommend, with the reasons.

Why DIY decor goes wrong

It is rarely the craft. Most people can make a lovely place card or a bud-vase arrangement. The problems are scale, transport and the day itself.

Scale is the one that surprises couples. Twelve centerpieces on a kitchen table looks like a lot. Twelve centerpieces in a 200-guest ballroom looks like twelve small objects in a very large room. Decor reads at room scale, which is why we spend so much time on the flower wall and the ceiling rather than on the tabletop.

Transport is next. Fragile things made over weeks get moved in one car on one morning. Candles melt in a trunk in July. Tall arrangements fall over at the first stop sign.

And the day itself: someone has to install it. If that someone is a bridesmaid at 10 a.m. with hair at 11, the install will be rushed, and rushed decor photographs as rushed decor.

The things worth making

Paper goods. Place cards, menus, table numbers and welcome signs are perfect DIY. They are small, they travel flat, and the personal touch reads as intentional. Order a proof of one item from a print shop, then produce the rest yourself if you like the look.

Favors and welcome bags. Assembled over a few evenings, boxed, delivered to the venue the day before. No install risk.

Bud vases and small florals. Grocery-store stems in bud vases, done the morning of, look fresh and relaxed. This is the one floral task we tell couples they can own. Three stems per vase, odd numbers, a little greenery, water changed before guests arrive.

A personal moment. A photo table, a memory corner, a drink menu written by the groom. One concentrated piece of DIY carries more feeling than a dozen scattered ones.

Anything you would enjoy making even if it did not matter. If you love calligraphy, write the escort cards. If you do not, buy them.

The things worth renting

Large structures. Arches, backdrops, flower walls, ceiling installs, draping. These are heavy, they need hardware and ladders, and they need a crew that knows the venue's rules. Our rental collection exists because these pieces are impractical to own once.

Lighting. Uplighting, string lights at height, pin-spotting the cake. Lighting is the cheapest way to make a room feel finished and the hardest thing to improvise. The up-lighting and stand rentals page covers what most ballrooms need.

Linens. Floor-length linens in the right size change a room. Buying them means owning sixty tablecloths after the wedding. Renting means they arrive pressed.

Candles at scale. Dozens of pillars in hurricanes, or hundreds of votives, need to be real, lit on time and permitted by the venue. Many Chicago venues require enclosed flames, and some require battery candles. Check the venue's own rules first.

Centerpieces for a large room. Twelve or more tall arrangements are a transport and install job, not a craft.

A simple way to decide

For each item, ask three questions. Can it be finished a week early? Does it fit in a shoebox or a flat box? Can it be set out by a guest in under a minute? Three yeses means make it. Any no means rent it or hire it.

Then set a time budget, not just a money budget. Sixty hours of DIY in the final two months is realistic for a couple. Two hundred hours is not, and it turns the last month into a second job.

What it costs, honestly

Rough Chicago-area ranges for a 150-guest reception, so you can compare like with like.

Paper goods made at home: 150 to 400 in materials and printing, 15 to 25 hours.

Bud vases, DIY: 250 to 450 in stems and vessels, one morning. Rented bud-vase arrangements from a florist: 600 to 1,200, installed.

A flower wall, rented and installed: 900 to 2,500 depending on size and florals. Made from scratch: the materials alone cost as much, and the structure rarely survives transport.

Uplighting for a ballroom, 12 to 20 fixtures: 400 to 900 rented and set. Buying consumer fixtures: 600 to 1,000, plus a return trip to collect them.

Floor-length linens for 15 tables: 300 to 600 rented. Bought: 450 to 900 and a closet you will never open again.

The pattern: DIY wins on small, personal, early items. Rental wins on anything large, lit or heavy.

Make the DIY look like part of the design

The trick to DIY that looks intentional is restraint and repetition. Pick two colors and one material and repeat them everywhere the DIY appears. Match the paper goods to the linens. Match the bud vases to the glassware. Keep handmade signs to one style of lettering.

Then let the rented pieces do the structural work. A flower wall behind the sweetheart table and uplighting on the walls make the room feel designed, and the handmade menus on each plate read as the personal layer on top of a finished room, which is exactly what they are. Our wedding flower decoration ideas article shows how the big pieces and the small ones share a palette.

A timeline that works

Twelve weeks out: decide the split. Book rentals and installs. Order paper proofs.

Eight weeks out: finish any DIY that can sit, such as signs, favors and table numbers. Store them boxed and labeled by table.

Three weeks out: confirm install times with the venue and with us or your florist. Confirm candle rules in writing.

One week out: deliver boxed DIY to the venue or to the planner. Buy nothing new.

Day before: stems for bud vases, in buckets, in a cool room.

Morning of: bud vases, then hand everything to the person installing. Go get ready.

When to skip DIY entirely

Some weddings should not have DIY in them, and that is not a judgment. If the venue has a strict load-in window, if the couple is traveling in from out of town, or if the guest count is over 200, the install math no longer works. Our planning and coordination team handles those days end to end, and the couple's job is to show up.

For most weddings, though, the answer is both. Make the small things that carry your handwriting. Rent the big things that carry the room. The decor will look designed because it was.

One last check before the day

A week out, lay every DIY item on a table in the order it will be placed in the room, and photograph it. Send the photo to whoever is installing, with the table plan. That one picture answers nine out of ten questions that would otherwise arrive by text on the morning of the wedding. If something looks thin on the table, it will look thinner in the room; that is the moment to add, not the morning of. And if an item did not survive the week in its box, leave it out. Nothing handmade is improved by being rebuilt at a venue loading dock.
