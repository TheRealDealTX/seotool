Event decoration sounds like it should be self-explanatory, and most people who hire a decor studio for the first time are surprised by how much of the work is invisible. The flowers on the table are the last 10 percent. Before them come site visits, floor plans, load-in schedules, venue rules, power, rigging, fire codes, truck permits, rentals from three different warehouses and a strike crew at midnight. This post explains what a decor studio actually does for a corporate or private event, how that differs from a planner or a florist, what each type of event needs, what it costs in Chicago in 2026, and how to brief us so the first proposal is close.

## Decor studio, planner, florist: who does what

A planner runs the event: timeline, vendors, budget, guests, the day itself. A florist makes arrangements. A decor studio designs and produces the physical environment: everything a guest sees that is not the building or the food. That includes flowers, but also linens, furniture, lighting, backdrops, draping, signage, tabletop, staging and the installation and removal of all of it.

Many studios, including ours, also do florals in-house and offer [planning and coordination](/wedding-planing/) as a separate service. The useful question when hiring is not the label but the scope: who is designing the room, who is sourcing every piece, who is installing it, and who is taking it down. A good brief names one responsible party for each.

## What the work looks like, start to finish

### 1. Brief and site visit

The first conversation covers the event's purpose, the guest count, the venue, the date, the budget range and the mood. Then we walk the venue, ideally at the same time of day as the event, with a tape measure and a camera. We note ceiling heights, rigging points, power outlets, loading access, elevator sizes, where the sun will be, and what the venue already owns. A ballroom with 300 gold chiavari chairs in storage is a different budget from one with none.

### 2. Design and proposal

The design is a floor plan with the decor drawn on it, a palette, reference images for each element, and a line-item proposal. For a corporate event this is often two or three options at different budget levels. For a private event it is usually one direction refined over a round or two. Our [wedding table decor ideas](/blog/wedding-table-decor-ideas/) post shows how the table shape alone changes the design, and the same logic runs through every room.

### 3. Sourcing

Flowers are ordered from wholesalers a week out and conditioned for two days. Rentals (linens, furniture, lighting, structures) are reserved from our own [decor rental inventory](/decor-rental/) and from partner warehouses. Custom pieces (branded backdrops, printed dance floors, built bars) are fabricated. Everything is tracked on one pull list, because the most common failure in event decor is a single missing item discovered at 4 p.m.

### 4. Logistics

This is the part nobody sees. Venue load-in windows are often tight: three hours to transform a hotel ballroom that held a breakfast meeting. We schedule trucks, crew, and the order of installation (rigging and draping first, then furniture, then linens, then florals, then tabletop, lighting focused last). Chicago venues add their own layer: union labor requirements at some hotels and convention spaces, loading dock reservations, certificates of insurance, fire marshal approval for any draping or candles, and street parking permits for trucks in the Loop.

### 5. Installation

A crew of four to twelve depending on scale. The lead carries the floor plan and the photos of every mockup. Centerpieces are placed from a numbered map so table 14 gets the arrangement designed for table 14. Lighting is focused once the room is dressed. We photograph every finished area for the client before guests arrive.

### 6. Event support and strike

For large events someone stays on site to fix a fallen arrangement, relight a candle or move a bar when the DJ's cables arrive late. Then, after the last guest, the strike: everything out, usually within two hours, with rentals sorted for return and florals donated or composted.

## Corporate events: what they need

Corporate event decoration is about brand and function more than romance.

- Brand first. The logo, the palette and the campaign of the moment drive the design. We often work from a brand guide and build backdrops, step-and-repeats, branded bars and signage around it.
- Sightlines and flow. Guests need to see the stage or screen, move between bars and seating, and photograph the sponsor wall. The floor plan matters more than the flowers.
- Durable, repeatable, fast. Corporate events often happen in short venue windows and recur annually. Modular pieces that install in minutes and store well are worth more than one-off installations.
- Lighting carries the room. Uplighting in brand colors, pin spots on tables and a lit stage do more for a corporate room than any arrangement. Our [extras and lighting](/extras/) page lists what we carry.
- Budget: a Chicago corporate reception of 200 guests with branded backdrop, uplighting, lounge furniture, bar decor and low centerpieces runs 12,000 to 30,000 in decor, before AV and rentals of tables and chairs. A gala with staging and ceiling draping runs 40,000 and up.

## Private events: what they need

Private event decoration (milestone birthdays, anniversaries, showers, holiday parties, weddings) is personal and emotional, and the brief is usually a feeling rather than a brand guide.

- One strong idea. The best private events have a single design idea carried through: a color, a flower, a material, a theme held lightly. Our [wedding shower decor ideas](/blog/wedding-shower-decor-ideas/) post shows the venue-first version of this.
- Photo moments. A backdrop, a dressed entrance, a sweetheart or head table. Guests will photograph two or three things; design those three.
- Tabletop detail. Private guests sit longer and look closer. Linens, napkins, place cards and candles are where the money shows.
- Budget: a 100-guest private celebration in a Chicago restaurant or loft runs 6,000 to 15,000 in decor; a full wedding reception runs 15,000 to 40,000 and beyond depending on florals.

## What drives the cost

In rough order: florals (fresh flowers are the most expensive material per square foot of visual impact), installed structures (ceiling draping, large backdrops, hanging installations need rigging and crew), lighting, specialty linens and furniture, and labor and trucks. Guest count moves the tabletop budget linearly; venue difficulty (stairs, no dock, short windows) moves labor. A good studio will show you which line to cut for the biggest saving, and it is almost always florals on tables guests do not sit at.

## How to brief a decor studio

Bring these to the first call and the proposal will be close:

1. Date, venue and the venue's load-in window.
2. Guest count and whether they are seated, standing or both.
3. The purpose of the event in one sentence.
4. A budget range. Studios design to a number; without one the first proposal is a guess.
5. Three to five reference images and, just as useful, two things you do not want.
6. Any brand guide, color code or family tradition that must appear.
7. Who else is involved: planner, caterer, AV company, venue coordinator.

The Events Industry Council's [industry glossary and standards](https://www.eventscouncil.org/) are a useful reference if you are new to the vocabulary; the terms a venue uses (strike, load-in, rigging points, drape kit) all appear there.

Event decoration is design, sourcing and logistics in equal parts, and the studios worth hiring are the ones that talk about the third part as much as the first.
