Portrait Photography Lighting: Five Setups, Mapped Out

Portrait photography lighting explained as five setups you can build with one or two lights. Where each light goes, what it does to the face, the ratios, and the mistakes that show up in the files, with a position map for each.

Editorial7 min readNovember 21, 2026

Portrait photography lighting gets taught as a list of names: Rembrandt, loop, butterfly, split, clamshell. The names are useful once you know what they mean, and useless before, because they describe the shadow on the face rather than where the light stands. This guide goes the other way. Five setups, each described as a position map you could chalk on a studio floor, with what the light does to a face, the second light or reflector that finishes it, the ratios to meter for, and the one mistake we see in files from each. All five can be built with one or two lights and a reflector, in a studio or in a living room with a window.

A convention for the maps. Picture a clock face on the floor with the subject at the centre, facing 6 o'clock, where the camera is. Light positions are given as a clock hour and a height. "Key at 4 o'clock, 45 degrees up" means the main light is to the camera's left, in front of the subject, raised so it looks down at about 45 degrees. That is enough to rebuild any of these without a diagram.

Setup 1: loop lighting, the default portrait

Map: key at 4 or 8 o'clock, 30 to 45 degrees above eye level, 1 to 1.5 metres from the face. White reflector at the opposite side, 7 or 5 o'clock, at face height. Camera at 6.

What it does: a small shadow from the nose loops down and to one side without touching the shadow on the cheek. The face has shape but nothing is hidden. Both eyes catch the light. It flatters almost everyone, which is why it is the default for headshots, editorial portraits and anything where the person has to look like themselves.

Modifier: a 90- to 120-centimetre softbox or octabox, or a window with sheer curtain. Bigger and closer is softer.

Ratio: with the reflector, the shadow side meters about one stop under the key (a 2:1 ratio). Pull the reflector back for 3:1 and more drama.

The mistake: key too high. The loop turns into a long nose shadow into the lip and the eyes go dark. Lower the light until both eyes have a catchlight in the upper half.

Setup 2: Rembrandt, for character

Map: key at 3 or 9 o'clock, 45 degrees up, slightly behind the subject's cheek line. No fill, or a black flag on the shadow side to deepen it. Camera at 6.

What it does: the nose shadow joins the cheek shadow and leaves a small triangle of light under the far eye. Half the face is in shadow. It is the look of old paintings and of every "serious" portrait, and it suits strong faces, older faces and men's editorial work. We used a version of it for the Nas-style grooming portraits where skin texture was the point.

Modifier: a smaller source than loop, a 60- to 90-centimetre softbox or a gridded beauty dish, so the shadow edge has definition.

Ratio: 4:1 or deeper. The shadow side is two or more stops under the key. Meter the lit cheek and let the shadow fall.

The mistake: the triangle disappears because the key is too far round. If the far eye is in full shadow with no triangle, bring the light forward toward 4 o'clock until it reappears.

Setup 3: butterfly and clamshell, for beauty

Map: key at 6 o'clock, directly in front, 45 to 60 degrees up, pointing down at the face. For clamshell, add a second light or a white reflector at 6 o'clock below the face, angled up, at about half the key's power. Camera at 6, shooting between the two.

What it does: a symmetrical shadow under the nose shaped like a butterfly, cheekbones lifted, the under-eye and chin opened by the fill. This is beauty and fashion lighting: it flattens texture and celebrates bone structure. It is unforgiving of a round face, which it rounds further, and it needs a subject who can hold their chin level.

Modifier: beauty dish or small octabox on the key; a reflector or a softbox on the fill. For the white backdrop lookbook work we often run the clamshell with the background lights adding a third element.

Ratio: key to fill 2:1 for classic beauty, 1.5:1 for a flatter, cleaner catalog look.

The mistake: fill stronger than the key. The shadows go upward, the face loses its shape, and the catchlights sit at the bottom of the eye. Keep the lower light at least half a stop under.

Setup 4: split lighting, for drama

Map: key at 3 or 9 o'clock exactly, at eye level or slightly above. Nothing else, or a black flag opposite. Camera at 6.

What it does: one half of the face lit, the other in shadow, with a hard line down the nose. It is graphic and editorial, works on any face shape because it hides half of it, and reads instantly as moody. It is also the easiest setup to get wrong, because any turn of the head changes the whole picture.

Modifier: a hard or semi-hard source, a bare reflector, a gridded dish, a strip box turned vertical.

Ratio: 8:1 or whatever the room gives you. This setup is about shadow, not fill.

The mistake: a lit nose tip on the shadow side. The light is slightly in front of the subject's cheek line. Move it back toward 3 o'clock until the tip of the nose falls into shadow.

Setup 5: short-lit window light, for everything else

Map: subject beside a window, turned so the window side of the face is the side away from the camera (that is "short" lighting). Camera at 6, window at roughly 2 or 10 o'clock. White reflector at face height on the camera side to open the shadow. No flash.

What it does: the narrow, far side of the face is lit and the broad, near side falls into soft shadow, which slims the face and gives depth. With a north-facing window, or any window with the sun off it, the light is a large soft source for free. Most natural-light portraits that look expensive are this.

Modifier: the window. A sheer curtain softens it; a black card on the far side of the subject deepens the shadow.

Ratio: usually 2:1 to 3:1 depending on how close the reflector is. Meter at the lit cheek; expose for it and let the far wall go.

The mistake: a window behind the camera. The face goes flat and the eyes squint. Put the window beside the subject, not behind you.

Metering and ratio, in practice

An incident meter at the subject's face, dome toward the camera, gives the exposure. Then turn the dome toward the key and toward the fill or reflector separately: the difference in stops is your ratio. One stop is 2:1, two stops is 4:1, three is 8:1. If you have no meter, use the camera's spot meter on the lit cheek and the shadow cheek and read the difference. The fashion and editorial production notes in the fashion editorial photography guide cover how these ratios get written into a call sheet so the look stays consistent across a day.

Backgrounds and separation

All five setups are about the face, and the face floats if the background is ignored. Two fixes: distance and a hair light. Keep the subject at least 2 metres from the background so the key does not light it. For separation on dark backgrounds, a small gridded light at 1 or 11 o'clock, high and behind the subject, skimming the hair and shoulder, half a stop to one stop over the key. On white, light the background separately and meter it two stops over, as in the white backdrop post.

A note on faces

Lighting choices are decisions about a person. Loop and short window light suit nearly everyone. Butterfly suits oval and heart-shaped faces and strong cheekbones. Rembrandt and split suit angular faces and anyone who wants to look serious. Round faces want short lighting and a key high enough to shape the cheek. Glasses want the key high and to the side so the reflection leaves the lens. Ask the subject to turn their head slowly through the light before the first frame; the face will tell you which setup it wants.

For the physics behind why a bigger, closer source is softer, and the standard terms, the Professional Photographers of America's education pages cover the fundamentals in more depth than any blog post should. The five maps above are the working version.
