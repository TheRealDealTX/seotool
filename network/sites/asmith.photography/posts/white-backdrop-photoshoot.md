A white backdrop photoshoot sounds like the easiest setup in photography. Hang white paper, point lights at it, shoot. Then the files come back and the background is a dull gray, or it is white but the subject's edges have gone soft and milky, or there is a gradient that no retoucher wants to deal with across 400 frames. Every one of those problems is a ratio problem, and the ratio is measurable before the first frame. This is the method we use for seamless white on footwear, lookbook and editorial jobs in Los Angeles, with the meter numbers, the distances, and the places it goes wrong.

## Why white goes gray

A camera does not know what white is. It records the light that reaches the sensor, and a white backdrop lit to the same intensity as the subject records as a mid-gray, because that is what properly exposed white looks like: a very light gray, around 235 on an 8-bit scale, not 255. To get a background that reads as pure white in camera, it has to receive more light than the subject. Not a little more. Measurably more, and evenly.

The rule is two stops. Light the background so that it meters one and a half to two stops brighter than the key light on the subject. At two stops over, the paper clips to 255 and the subject keeps full detail. Beyond two and a half stops you get the second problem: light bouncing off the paper wraps around the subject, lowers contrast at the edges and gives everything a hazy halo. The window is narrow, and that is why you meter instead of guessing.

## The setup, measured

Our standard seamless white rig for a full-length subject:

- 9-foot seamless white paper, pulled down and swept across the floor at least 10 feet.
- Subject standing 8 to 12 feet in front of the backdrop. Distance is the cheapest tool you have: the farther the subject is from the paper, the less background light spills back onto them, and the easier it is to hold the ratio.
- Two background lights, one each side, in strip boxes or 7-inch reflectors with grids, aimed at the paper at roughly 45 degrees, feathered so their beams cross behind the subject. Strips give even coverage top to bottom for full-length work; reflectors are fine for head-and-shoulders.
- Key light on the subject: a large softbox or octabox, 4 to 6 feet across, positioned high and to one side for portraits, or two sources flanking the camera for flat, catalog-style footwear and product.
- Optional fill: a white V-flat opposite the key, or a second, weaker source at camera.

Meter the background first. With the subject out of frame, hold an incident meter at the paper, dome toward the camera, and take readings at the center, both edges, top and bottom of the frame. Adjust the two background heads until every reading is within a third of a stop. If the center is f/16 and the edges are f/11, you will have a gray vignette no retoucher will thank you for.

Then meter the subject. Dome toward the key light at the subject's face or at the product's surface. Set the key so it reads two stops below the background. Background at f/16 means key at f/8. Set the camera to f/8 and the paper clips cleanly.

## Controlling spill

The two-stop rule only holds if background light stays on the background. Three controls, in order of effectiveness:

1. Distance, again. Every foot between subject and paper helps.
2. Flags. Black V-flats or 4-by-8 foam core placed just outside the frame on both sides of the subject, between the background lights and the subject. They stop the bounce from reaching the subject's edges and restore contrast. This is the single most skipped step and the single biggest difference between amateur and professional white.
3. Grids. A 20- or 30-degree grid on each background head narrows the beam so it hits the paper and nothing else. The cost is harder evenness, which is why we meter five points.

For footwear, where the product sits on the paper, the floor sweep is part of the background and gets the same treatment: meter the floor behind and beside the shoe, and expect to add a low light skimming the sweep from the side to lift it to match. The shadow under the shoe is a decision, not an accident; a soft contact shadow reads as real, a hard one reads as cheap, no shadow reads as a cutout. ## Ratios by job

The two-stop rule is the baseline. The job moves it.

### Lookbook and e-commerce

The client wants pure white at 255 so garments can be cut out or dropped onto any page. Background at plus two stops, flat key from two sources near camera, fill to open the shadows, and the flags doing most of the work. Shoot tethered and check a histogram: the right edge should hit the wall without a spike creeping back into the subject's highlights. We walked through the production side of this in the [Fila lookbook casting and movement](/fila-spring-2017/) piece.

### Editorial portrait on white

Here a touch of gray is a style, not a fault. Many editorial photographers run the background at plus one stop, so it records as a soft, slightly textured off-white that holds the paper's sweep. If you want that, meter for it deliberately and keep it consistent across the day. Nothing is worse than a story where half the frames are 255 and half are 240.

### Product and still life

Small products allow a different trick: light the background from behind through translucent acrylic or use a light table, and the ratio problem mostly disappears because the background is its own light source. For harder goods, the [Florsheim still life](/florsheim-shoes/) notes cover how we handle polished surfaces that reflect the white.

## Camera settings that keep it honest

- Shoot RAW. The two-stop margin is forgiving in RAW and brutal in JPEG.
- Base ISO. Noise in the background is the easiest way to make white look dirty.
- Shutter at or below sync speed, 1/200 or 1/250, so ambient contributes nothing. Kill the studio's overhead lights; a tungsten ceiling fixture puts a warm cast into the shadows that reads as mud.
- Custom white balance off a gray card under the key light. Auto white balance will chase the paper and shift skin tones frame to frame.
- Check for clipping with the camera's highlight warning, but trust the histogram, not the blinking.

## The five mistakes that make white gray

1. Background and key at the same power. The commonest one. Meter them separately.
2. Subject too close to the paper. The background lights hit the subject and the subject's shadow hits the paper.
3. No flags. The edges go hazy and the blacks in the clothing lift.
4. Uneven background. One light stronger than the other, or heads too close to the paper, giving hot spots.
5. Fixing it in post. Pushing levels to force 240 up to 255 drags the subject's highlights with it and leaves a halo at every edge. The retoucher's time costs more than the ten minutes of metering that avoids it.

## A note on the meter

A handheld incident meter is still the fastest way to set ratios, and a used one costs less than a day of retouching. If you do not own one, the camera's spot meter on the paper will do: the paper should read two stops over the camera's chosen exposure. B&H's photography guides, including their [lighting tips and solutions](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/explora/photography/tips-and-solutions), cover the basics of incident metering for anyone starting out, and the method has not changed in forty years.

White is the least forgiving background in the studio because every error shows. It is also the most repeatable once the numbers are written down. Meter five points on the paper, set the key two stops under, flag the sides, and the files come back white.
