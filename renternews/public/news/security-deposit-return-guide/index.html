How to Get Your Security Deposit Back: A Renter's Guide

Document move-in and move-out, know your state's return deadline, and dispute unfair deductions. Examples from California, New York and Texas law.

Renter News Staff September 25, 2026 · 4 min read
Share

Key points

  • Deadlines differ by state: for example, 21 days in California, 14 days in New York and 30 days in Texas.
  • Photos and a written condition checklist at move-in and move-out are your strongest evidence.
  • Landlords generally cannot charge for normal wear and tear, and several states impose penalties for bad-faith withholding.

A security deposit is often the largest single sum a renter hands over, and getting it back can depend on paperwork you created a year or more earlier. Deposit rules are set by state law, and some cities add their own. The examples below show how rules differ; always check the law where your rental is located, or call a local legal aid office or tenant hotline.

How state deadlines compare

Every state sets its own rules for when and how a landlord must return a deposit. Three large states illustrate how much they vary:

  • California: The California courts' self-help guide says landlords have 21 days after move-out to return the full deposit or send an itemized statement of deductions with the balance. If deductions exceed $125, copies of invoices or receipts must be attached. Tenants can also request a pre-move-out inspection.
  • New York: Under General Obligations Law section 7-108, deposits are generally capped at one month's rent. Landlords must provide an itemized statement and return any remaining balance within 14 days after the tenant leaves, or forfeit the right to keep any of it. Tenants may request inspections before moving in and before moving out.
  • Texas: The Texas Attorney General says tenants must give the landlord a forwarding address to receive the deposit, and the landlord must return it, minus any deductions, within 30 days. Withholding requires an itemized list describing the damage.

Other states use different time frames, caps and interest requirements. Your state attorney general or housing agency usually publishes a tenant guide.

Step 1: Document everything at move-in

  • Take date-stamped photos and video of every room, including floors, walls, appliances, inside cabinets, windows, bathroom fixtures and any existing damage.
  • Fill out a move-in condition checklist, ask the landlord to sign it, and keep a copy. If your state offers a formal move-in inspection (New York does, for example), request one.
  • Email the photos to yourself or the landlord so there is a dated record.
  • Keep your lease, deposit receipt and any later repair requests in one folder.

Step 2: Understand wear and tear vs. damage

Landlords can typically deduct for unpaid rent and for damage beyond normal wear and tear, but not for ordinary aging of the unit. The Texas Attorney General gives a useful illustration: carpet that wears thin from a year of normal foot traffic is wear and tear, but carpet ruined by a leaking water bed is damage the tenant may be charged for. Faded paint, minor scuffs and loose door handles generally fall on the wear-and-tear side; holes in walls, broken fixtures and pet stains often do not.

Step 3: Move out the right way

  1. Give proper notice. Check your lease for required notice periods. The Texas Attorney General notes many leases require 30 days' notice as a condition of returning the deposit.
  2. Request a pre-move-out inspection if your state allows one, and fix what you reasonably can.
  3. Clean thoroughly and return the unit to the condition you received it in.
  4. Repeat your photo and video walkthrough after the unit is empty and clean.
  5. Return all keys and get a written acknowledgment.
  6. Give a forwarding address in writing. In some states, including Texas, this is required before the deadline starts to matter.

Step 4: If the deposit does not come back

  • Wait out the deadline, then send a written demand letter. Cite your move-out date, the state deadline, the amount owed and a date by which you expect payment. Attach your photos if you are disputing specific charges.
  • Challenge unsupported deductions. Ask for receipts or invoices; California, for instance, requires them for deductions over $125.
  • Know the penalties. In California, tenants can seek up to twice the deposit amount in additional damages if a landlord kept it in bad faith. In New York, a landlord who willfully violates the deposit rules can be liable for punitive damages of up to twice the deposit. Other states have their own penalty rules.
  • Use small claims court. Deposit disputes are a common small claims matter, and you generally do not need a lawyer. Check your state's dollar limit and filing deadline.
  • File a complaint. Many state attorneys general accept landlord-tenant complaints.

After a fire

If a fire forces you out and the lease ends, ask the landlord in writing how your deposit will be handled. Deposit laws still apply, but whether you owe rent, and for how long, depends on your lease and state law.

Quick checklist

  1. Photograph the unit at move-in and move-out.
  2. Keep a signed condition checklist.
  3. Look up your state's deadline and rules.
  4. Give written notice and a forwarding address.
  5. Send a demand letter if the deadline passes.
  6. Consider small claims court.
