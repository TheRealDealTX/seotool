The Dos and Don'ts of Collecting Artists' Prints

How to buy original prints with confidence: what edition numbers mean, original prints vs. reproductions, how to check paper and signatures, and how to care for prints at home.

CollectingFirst published March 22, 2009Updated October 10, 20267 min read

Prints are how most people start collecting real art. They're original works of art, often made by artists whose paintings cost ten times as much, and they come with a vocabulary that can feel designed to confuse. Here's a field guide.

First, what counts as an original print?

An original print is an artwork conceived for the printmaking process: a woodcut, linocut, etching, lithograph, screenprint, monotype or collagraph. The artist (or a printer working closely with them) creates the matrix and pulls the impressions. Each print is an original, even though there are several.

A reproduction is a photographic copy of an artwork that exists in another form, typically a painting. Offset posters and most giclées are reproductions. They aren't bad, but they're a different thing at a different price.

The dos

  • Do ask how it was made. Artists love this question. "Is this an etching or a reproduction of one?" is perfectly polite.
  • Do look closely. Etchings have a plate mark, a bevelled dent in the paper around the image. Relief prints show ink sitting on the surface, sometimes with a slight emboss on the back. Under a loupe, inkjet and offset reproductions dissolve into tiny regular dots.
  • Do read the margin. Hand-pulled prints are normally signed and numbered in pencil, not ink, below the image.
  • Do buy what you'll live with. Investment value is unpredictable. Daily pleasure isn't.
  • Do keep the paperwork. A receipt, a certificate or even an email describing the work and edition helps with insurance, resale and your own records.

The don'ts

  • Don't pay original-print prices for a "limited edition" reproduction. A signature on a poster doesn't make it an etching.
  • Don't fixate on low edition numbers. In a properly printed hand-pulled edition, 1/30 and 27/30 are equivalent.
  • Don't frame with ordinary mat board or tape. Acidic board browns paper. Ask for acid-free or rag mats and hinges, and UV-filtering glazing.
  • Don't hang works on paper in direct sun, above radiators or in bathrooms. Light fading is permanent.
  • Don't trim margins. Wide paper margins are part of the work, and cutting them can halve a print's value.

Reading an edition

MarkingMeaning

Where to start

Studio open days, art walks and university print sales are the best places to meet printmakers and see work in person. In southern Arizona, a Bisbee gallery walk is a fine introduction. Before you buy, read our notes on affordable originals vs. giclées, and when it comes home, hang it at the right height.

Questions

What does 12/50 mean on a print?

It is the twelfth impression of an edition limited to fifty. Lower numbers aren't inherently more valuable for hand-pulled prints, because every impression in a good edition should match.

What is an artist's proof (A/P)?

An impression set aside for the artist outside the numbered edition, usually no more than about 10% of the edition size. They're legitimate originals and often priced a little higher.

Is a giclée an original print?

A giclée is an inkjet reproduction. It can be a lovely object, but it reproduces an artwork made in another medium rather than being one in its own right. Collect it knowing that.
