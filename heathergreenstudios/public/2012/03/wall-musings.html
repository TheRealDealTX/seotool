Wall Musings: The Eight-Hour Wall Drawing

Why make a drawing directly on a gallery wall in a single day? Notes on wall drawing as performance, materials that work on drywall, and how to plan a durational drawing.

Studio LifeFirst published March 3, 2012Updated October 10, 20265 min read

A wall drawing has no frame, no buyer and no future: it gets painted over when the show ends. That's exactly what makes it free. Give yourself one working day, a blank wall and an audience walking in and out, and the drawing becomes part performance and part meditation.

Why eight hours?

A fixed duration is a powerful constraint. It removes the question "is it finished?" (it's finished when the time is up) and replaces it with a better one: "what can I discover in the time I have?" Time-lapse it, and eight hours of mark-making compresses into two hypnotic minutes of a wall slowly filling up.

Planning the day

  • Start with a system, not an image. A rule like "one line per breath," "only arcs from the floor" or "trace every shadow at the top of each hour" gives you a way to begin and a way to keep going.
  • Map the wall. Snap a few chalk lines for the horizon or margins, then leave the rest to chance.
  • Work in blocks. Fifty minutes on, ten off. Step back at every break; walls look completely different from across the room.
  • Invite interruption. If visitors are welcome to talk, their conversations become part of the piece.

Letting it go

Photograph the wall in even daylight from straight on before it's painted out. The photograph becomes the record; the drawing itself was the event. If impermanence suits you, look into process-focused practice more broadly. It's a useful antidote to the pressure of producing objects.

Questions

What materials work for drawing directly on walls?

Graphite, charcoal, pastel, chalk, acrylic paint markers and diluted acrylic all work on primed drywall. Test first and plan for paint-over afterward.
