Smoke Odor Claims

Smoke Odor Insurance Claims

A room can look clean and still smell like smoke. Here is how persistent smoke odor is typically documented and addressed in a property insurance claim.

Updated: September 28, 2026

Request a Free Claim ReviewCall +1 (844) 537-1427

Smoke odor is one of the most common complaints after a fire, and one of the hardest to pin down in a claim. Walls get cleaned, contents come back from the cleaner, and the smell is still there.

Odor matters in a claim because it can point to residue that is still present in materials, in concealed spaces, or in the HVAC system. It can also be a sign that cleaning or deodorization did not fully work. At the same time, odor on its own does not automatically prove a particular kind of damage or contamination. It is a reason to look more closely and document what is found.

Why smoke odor lingers

Smoke odor often persists because it is held in materials rather than just in the air. Porous materials tend to hold odor more than nonporous ones. Common sources include:

  • Drywall and flat-painted surfaces
  • Insulation in walls and attics
  • Carpet and pad
  • Upholstered furniture and mattresses
  • Clothing, bedding, and curtains
  • Unsealed wood, including subfloor and framing
  • Ductwork and HVAC components

Odor can also come from residue in places that were not cleaned, such as wall cavities, cabinet interiors, attic spaces, or the ductwork. Identifying the source is often a technical question for qualified restoration or HVAC professionals.

Odor in a property claim

In a claim, odor raises practical questions:

  • Which rooms and materials still have odor?
  • Was deodorization included in the carrier's estimate, and where?
  • Was it performed, and did it work?
  • Is odor coming from a specific material, space, or system?
  • Does the estimate address the materials that seem to be holding the odor?

A persistent odor complaint may lead to additional inspection, specialist input, or a closer look at whether certain materials need to be replaced rather than cleaned. Whether that happens, and what the claim ultimately includes, depends on the facts of the loss, the documentation, and your policy.

How to document smoke odor

Odor is harder to document than visible damage because you cannot photograph it. That makes written records and consistency more important.

Ways to document odor

  • Keep a dated log of where odor is noticeable, room by room
  • Note which materials or items seem to hold the odor
  • Record whether odor gets stronger when the HVAC system runs, on warm days, or in closed rooms
  • Note odor before and after any cleaning or deodorization
  • Photograph the areas and items you are describing, even if the photos only show location
  • Keep records of any cleaning or deodorization work and when it was done
  • Save written findings from restoration, HVAC, or other qualified professionals
  • Put odor concerns in writing to the carrier

Having more than one person note odor in the same places over time can help show it is consistent and not a one-time observation.

Questions to raise with the carrier

When odor is part of your concerns, it helps to ask specific questions in writing rather than simply reporting that the house still smells. For example:

  • Which rooms and materials does the estimate include for deodorization?
  • Were the attic, wall cavities, and HVAC system considered as possible odor sources?
  • Will the carrier reinspect areas where odor remains after cleaning?
  • What information would the carrier need to review replacement of specific materials or items?

Written questions create a record and make it easier to follow up on each point.

Specialist input

Depending on the loss, qualified professionals may be able to help identify where odor is coming from and what may address it. That may include:

  • Restoration professionals who can evaluate whether materials responded to cleaning and deodorization
  • HVAC professionals who can evaluate whether the system is holding or distributing residue
  • Industrial hygienists or other testing professionals on larger or more complicated losses

We do not perform these evaluations. When findings from qualified professionals are available, we use them as part of the claim documentation. Not every odor complaint requires specialist involvement; it depends on the loss.

Odor and the HVAC system

If odor seems stronger when the heating or air conditioning runs, the HVAC system may be worth evaluating. Smoke can be drawn into returns and ductwork during or after a fire, and residue in the system can be recirculated through the building.

Whether the system is included in the claim depends on qualified findings and the policy. See HVAC smoke damage claims for more. We do not provide HVAC repair or cleaning instructions.

Odor in contents

Clothing, bedding, furniture, rugs, and other contents often hold odor. Some items respond to specialized cleaning. Others may not.

If contents come back from a cleaning company still smelling like smoke, document it. Note the item, the date it was returned, and the odor. Ask the carrier in writing to review those items. For more, see smoke-damaged contents.

Cleaning vs. replacement when odor persists

Odor is often at the center of cleaning vs. replacement discussions. A carrier's estimate may include deodorization and cleaning. If odor remains after that work, the question becomes whether the materials holding it need further treatment or replacement.

There is no automatic answer. It depends on the materials, what was tried, the results, qualified findings, and the policy. Clear documentation of what was done and what remained is what helps most. Our page on soot damage claims covers how residue affects porous and nonporous materials.

Is smoke odor covered by insurance?

Many policies cover smoke damage connected to a covered cause of loss, and odor may be part of that damage. Coverage depends on your policy language and the facts. Ask the carrier to explain its position in writing.

Does a smoke smell mean my home is contaminated?

Not necessarily. Odor can indicate residue in materials or systems, but it does not by itself establish a particular condition. Qualified professionals can evaluate the property if there are concerns.

How do I prove smoke odor in a claim?

Keep a dated, room-by-room log, note which materials hold odor, record odor before and after cleaning, and gather written findings from qualified professionals where available.

What if odor comes back after deodorization?

Document it with dates and locations and notify the carrier in writing. It may be a reason for further review of the affected materials or systems.

Can the HVAC system spread smoke odor?

It can, if smoke or residue entered the system. A qualified HVAC professional can evaluate it.

Do you remove smoke odor?

No. We are a public adjusting company. We handle the insurance claim; odor removal is done by restoration contractors.
