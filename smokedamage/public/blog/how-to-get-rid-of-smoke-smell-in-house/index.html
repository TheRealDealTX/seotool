Smoke Odor

How to Get Rid of Smoke Smell in House: A Step-by-Step Plan

Smoke odor lives in residue on walls, fabrics, and the HVAC system. This step-by-step plan covers what to clean, what to replace, what to seal, and when a fire claim changes the approach.

By Joseph DittmanPublished November 4, 2026Smoke Odor

Request a Free Claim ReviewCall +1 (844) 537-1427

If you're searching for how to get rid of smoke smell in house rooms, here's the short answer: the smell comes from residue, not from the air. Smoke leaves a thin film of soot and oily particles on walls, ceilings, fabrics, and inside the HVAC system. The odor keeps coming back until that residue is cleaned off or sealed in. Airing out the house and running a good air purifier help, but they treat the symptom. Cleaning surfaces, laundering or replacing soft goods, changing filters, and sealing what can't be cleaned are what actually remove the smell.

This guide walks through that process in order. It covers lingering odor from cigarettes, cooking fires, a fireplace backdraft, wildfire smoke, or a small house fire. If the smoke came from a fire that may be covered by your insurance, read the section on claims before you start scrubbing or throwing anything away. Our smoke odor claims page covers that side in more depth.

The short version

  • Make sure the house is safe to enter and protect yourself while you work.
  • Ventilate, then replace the HVAC filter so the system stops recirculating odor.
  • Clean hard surfaces top to bottom: ceilings, walls, cabinets, floors.
  • Launder, deodorize, or professionally clean fabrics and soft goods.
  • Run a HEPA air purifier with a real activated carbon filter.
  • Seal surfaces that still smell after cleaning with an odor-blocking primer.
  • Call a professional when the odor is heavy, widespread, or tied to a fire claim.

Why smoke smell lingers in a house

Smoke is a mix of tiny particles and gases. The particles settle on every surface they touch, and the gases soak into porous materials like drywall, wood, carpet, upholstery, and insulation. When the temperature or humidity changes, some of that residue releases odor again. That's why a room can seem fine one day and smell strongly the next, especially on a hot, humid Texas afternoon.

Different smoke leaves different residue:

  • Cigarette and cigar smoke leaves a sticky, yellow-brown film that builds up over years and can bleed through paint.
  • Protein smoke from burned food is often nearly invisible but has a strong, sour odor that clings to cabinets and fabrics.
  • Wood and paper smoke from fireplaces and wildfires tends to leave dry, powdery soot that smears if you wipe it wrong.
  • Synthetic smoke from burning plastics and household materials leaves oily, black residue that is harder to clean.

That is why any plan for how to get rid of smoke smell in house spaces has to start with the residue. Knowing the source helps you pick the right cleaner and set realistic expectations. Light odor from a single burned meal may fade with ventilation and basic cleaning. Years of indoor smoking or a structure fire usually take much more work. Our soot damage page explains how residue types are described in insurance claims.

Step 1: Make sure it's safe and protect yourself

If the smoke came from a fire, don't go back in until the fire department or another official says the building is safe. Fire can weaken floors and ceilings, and utilities may need to be checked first.

Soot and smoke residue can irritate your eyes, skin, and lungs. While you clean:

  • Wear nitrile or rubber gloves and eye protection.
  • Consider a well-fitted N95 respirator, especially when cleaning soot or vacuuming.
  • Keep children, pets, and anyone with asthma or other breathing problems out of the work area.
  • Never mix cleaning products, especially bleach and ammonia, which can release dangerous gases.
  • Read and follow the label on every product you use.

If you have health concerns about smoke exposure, ask your doctor. If residue is heavy or covers large areas, a professional restoration company has equipment and training this guide can't replace.

Step 2: Ventilate and stop recirculating the odor

Start by moving smoky air out. Open windows on opposite sides of the house to create a cross-breeze, and put a box fan in one window facing out. Run bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans. Skip this step on days when outdoor air quality is poor, such as during a nearby wildfire.

Next, deal with the HVAC system. Your heating and cooling system pulls air from every room, so a dirty filter can keep spreading odor after you clean. Turn the system off while you work if residue is heavy, then put in a fresh filter before running it again. Many homeowners choose a MERV 13 pleated filter, which is designed to capture finer particles than basic fiberglass filters. Check that your system can handle a higher-rated filter, since some older units may lose airflow. A widely reviewed option is this MERV 13 (MPR 1900) pleated air filter, 16x25x1, 2-pack. Buy the size printed on your current filter.

Change the filter again after the main cleaning is done, and keep checking it for a few weeks. If odor returns every time the system runs, the ductwork itself may hold residue. Our HVAC smoke damage page explains why duct cleaning and inspection often come up after a fire.

Step 3: Clean hard surfaces from top to bottom

This is the step that does most of the work. Residue on walls, ceilings, and cabinets is usually the main source of lingering odor. Work from the ceiling down so dirty drips land on areas you haven't cleaned yet.

Dry-clean loose soot first

If you can see powdery soot, don't start with a wet rag. Water can smear soot into paint and make stains worse. Instead, vacuum loose soot with a HEPA vacuum and a brush attachment, holding the nozzle just above the surface. Then wipe with a dry-chemical sponge, sometimes called a soot sponge. These are made of vulcanized rubber and lift dry residue without water. Wipe in one direction, and cut or flip to a clean section often. One option is the ProPlus Dry Cleaning Sponge.

Then wash with a degreasing solution

After dry cleaning, wash surfaces with warm water and a cleaner that cuts grease. Options people commonly use include:

  • A mild dish soap and warm water for light residue.
  • A 50/50 mix of white vinegar and warm water for odor on hard, sealed surfaces.
  • A trisodium phosphate (TSP) cleaner or TSP substitute for heavier, oily residue. Use gloves, follow the label, and rinse well.

Test any cleaner on a hidden spot first, especially on flat paint, wallpaper, and finished wood. Use two buckets, one for cleaning solution and one for rinse water, and change the water when it turns gray. Dry surfaces with clean towels.

Don't skip the hidden surfaces

Smoke residue collects in places people rarely clean. Make a pass over:

  • Tops of cabinets, door frames, and window trim
  • Ceiling fan blades and light fixtures
  • Blinds, window sills, and window tracks
  • Inside closets, cabinets, and drawers
  • Light switch plates, outlet covers, and vent covers
  • Baseboards and the backs of doors

Step 5: Treat the air with HEPA and activated carbon

Once the main cleaning is done, an air purifier can help with what's left. Look for two things:

  • A True HEPA filter to capture fine smoke particles.
  • A real activated carbon filter to adsorb odor-causing gases. Carbon is what handles smell. A thin carbon-dusted sheet holds far less than a thick carbon bed or pellet filter.

Size the purifier for the room. Manufacturers usually list a room size or a clean air delivery rate (CADR), and a unit that's too small will struggle. Run it continuously for the first days, and replace the filters on schedule, because a saturated carbon filter stops removing odor. One widely reviewed model with True HEPA and a carbon filter is the WINIX 5510 Air Purifier.

Passive odor absorbers can help in closets, bathrooms, and small rooms. Bags of activated bamboo charcoal or open boxes of baking soda absorb some odor over time. They won't fix a heavily smoke-damaged house, but they're inexpensive and easy to place. An example is this 600 g bamboo charcoal air purifying bag.

A note on ozone generators

You'll see ozone generators recommended online for smoke odor. Be cautious. Ozone is a lung irritant, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency advises against using ozone generators in occupied spaces. Professional restorers who use ozone or hydroxyl equipment typically do so in an empty building with specific safety procedures. If you think you need that level of treatment, it's a job for a professional, not a DIY step.

Step 6: Seal what cleaning can't fix

Sometimes walls and ceilings still smell after a thorough cleaning. That usually means residue soaked into the drywall or wood. Painting over it with regular paint rarely works, because the odor and stains can bleed through.

Instead, clean the surface, let it dry completely, and apply an odor-blocking primer before you repaint. Shellac-based primers are a long-standing choice for smoke odor and nicotine stains. They have strong fumes while wet, so ventilate well and follow the label. A common option is Zinsser B-I-N Advanced Synthetic Shellac Primer (1 quart). For lighter odor, some people prefer a water-based product like Zinsser Odor Killing Primer, which has less smell during application.

Sealing is the last step, not the first. Primer locks in whatever is underneath, so clean the residue off first. And if the house had a fire, check with your insurance adjuster before sealing or repainting, because they may want to inspect or test surfaces first.

How to get rid of smoke smell in house after a fire

A fire changes the job in two ways. The residue is usually heavier and spread farther, and there may be an insurance claim that depends on what the damage looked like before cleanup. Here's how to keep both in mind:

  1. Report the loss promptly. Most policies require prompt notice. Our fire damage insurance claim tips cover the first steps.
  2. Document before you clean. Take wide photos and video of every room, then close-ups of residue on walls, ceilings, vents, and contents.
  3. Make reasonable temporary efforts. Ventilating and preventing further damage are usually expected. Keep receipts for supplies you buy.
  4. Don't throw items away without a record. Photograph and list anything you discard, and ask the adjuster how they'd like it handled.
  5. Ask about professional cleaning. Insurers often work with restoration companies. Ask what's included and what happens if odor remains.

Whether smoke odor is covered depends on your policy and the cause of the smoke. Our article is smoke damage covered by insurance? explains the policy sections that usually decide it. To get a sense of how far smoke spread through the home, the smoke damage scope calculator helps you organize affected rooms and surfaces.

When DIY isn't enough

DIY cleaning works well for light, localized odor. Consider professional help when:

  • Odor is strong in several rooms or the whole house.
  • You see soot inside vents, on ceilings, or in the attic.
  • Smell returns every time the HVAC system runs.
  • Residue is oily, black, or comes from burned plastics.
  • Anyone in the home has breathing problems or other health concerns.
  • The smoke came from a fire that may be part of an insurance claim.

Restoration companies handle cleaning and deodorizing. A licensed public adjuster handles a different job: documenting the loss and presenting the claim to the insurer on the policyholder's behalf. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get rid of smoke smell in a house?

It depends on the source and how far the residue spread. Light odor from a single cooking incident may fade within days of ventilating and cleaning. Years of indoor smoking or a house fire can take weeks of cleaning, laundering, and sealing, and sometimes professional restoration.

Does vinegar get rid of smoke smell?

Washing hard, sealed surfaces with a mix of white vinegar and warm water can help remove light residue and odor. Bowls of vinegar left out may absorb a little odor from the air, but they do not remove residue from walls, fabrics, or ducts, which is where most lingering smell comes from.

Will an air purifier remove smoke smell by itself?

Usually not. A purifier with True HEPA and a substantial activated carbon filter can reduce particles and odor in the air, but residue on surfaces keeps releasing smell. Purifiers work best after surfaces, fabrics, and filters have been cleaned or replaced.

Can I just paint over smoke-damaged walls?

Regular paint alone often lets odor and stains bleed through. Clean the residue first, let the surface dry, and apply an odor-blocking primer before painting. If the smoke came from a fire, check with your insurance adjuster before sealing or repainting.

Are ozone generators safe for smoke odor?

Ozone is a lung irritant, and the EPA advises against using ozone generators in occupied spaces. Professionals who use ozone treatment typically do so in an empty building with safety procedures. It is not a recommended DIY step for an occupied home.

Should I clean smoke damage before the insurance adjuster comes?

Take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, such as ventilating, but document everything with photos and video first. Avoid discarding items or sealing surfaces before the insurer has had a chance to see them, and ask the adjuster how they want cleanup handled.
