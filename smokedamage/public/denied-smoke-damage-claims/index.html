Smoke Damage Claim Denied? What to Review Next

A denial letter is the carrier's stated position on your claim, based on the information it had at the time. Here is how to read it, what to ask for, and what documentation may matter if you want the decision looked at again.

Updated: September 28, 2026

Getting a denial after a fire or smoke event is frustrating, especially when you can still smell smoke in the house or see soot on the walls. But a denial is not always the end of the conversation. It is the insurer's position based on what it reviewed, how it read the policy, and what it believed the facts showed.

Sometimes that position holds up. Sometimes the claim file was missing something. The first job is to understand exactly why the claim was denied, in writing, before deciding what to do next.

This page explains the general categories of denials we see on smoke damage claims, how to read a denial letter, and when it may make sense to talk to a public adjuster or an attorney.

Common reasons a smoke damage claim may be denied

Every policy is different, and every loss has its own facts. The categories below are general. Whether any of them applies to your claim depends on your policy language and what happened.

Coverage position

The carrier may take the position that the policy does not cover the type of loss, the property involved, or the circumstances. This can come up with detached structures, certain personal property, business property under a homeowners policy, or properties that were vacant for a period. Endorsements and definitions matter here, so the full policy, not just the declarations page, is where the answer usually lives.

Causation questions

Smoke claims often turn on cause. If the insurer believes the residue or odor came from something other than the reported fire, such as candles, a fireplace, cooking, cigarette smoke, or long-term wear, it may deny the claim or part of it. Neighboring-fire and wildfire smoke claims can raise causation questions too, because the damage is less obvious than a burned room.

Documentation gaps

A claim that was reported with few photos, no testing, and no room-by-room description is harder for anyone to evaluate. If the carrier's inspection was brief, or happened after cleaning had started, the file may not reflect what was actually there. A denial based on "no damage observed" sometimes reflects what was documented more than what exists.

Timing issues

Policies generally require prompt notice of a loss and set conditions for cooperating with the claim. If the claim was reported well after the event, or requested information was not provided, the insurer may cite those conditions. Texas law and your policy set timelines; check your policy and TDI resources rather than relying on general rules of thumb.

Exclusions

Policies contain exclusions and limitations. Some that can come up near smoke claims include wear and tear, gradual deterioration, pollution or contamination language, and exclusions tied to certain sources of smoke. How an exclusion applies depends on its exact wording and the facts of the loss.

Policy language matters

Two policies that look similar can treat the same smoke loss differently. We don't give categorical coverage answers because the specific wording, endorsements, and facts of each loss drive the outcome.

How to read a smoke damage denial letter

A denial letter usually contains more useful information than it first appears to. Read it slowly and mark up a copy.

Look for:

  • The specific reason. Is it a coverage position, a causation finding, an exclusion, a condition of the policy, or a lack of information?
  • The policy provisions cited. Letters often quote or reference specific sections. Find those sections in your full policy and read them in context, including definitions.
  • What the decision relied on. Look for references to an inspection, a consultant or lab report, photos, or a recorded statement.
  • Whether it is a full or partial denial. Some letters deny one part of a claim, such as contents or HVAC, while accepting another.
  • Any invitation to submit more information. Many letters say the carrier will reconsider if new information is provided.

If the letter is vague, that is worth noting. "Not covered under the policy" without a provision or explanation is hard to respond to.

Request the basis for the denial in writing

If you don't understand the reasoning, ask for it in writing. A short, polite written request works well:

  • Ask the carrier to identify the policy provisions it relied on.
  • Ask for copies of any inspection reports, photos, or consultant reports referenced in the decision.
  • Ask what specific information, if any, would change or add to its review.
  • Ask for a certified copy of your full policy if you don't already have one.

Keep a copy of every request and every response. Email is fine; it creates a record with a date. Phone calls are useful, but follow each one with a short email summarizing what was discussed.

Documentation that may help a re-review

If the denial rests on a lack of information or a disputed fact, organized documentation is usually where a re-review begins. What matters depends on the reason given, but these are common:

Documentation that may support a closer review

  • Date-stamped photos and video of every affected room, including ceilings, vents, closets, and attic areas
  • Close-up photos of soot, residue, discoloration, and odor-affected materials
  • A room-by-room written description of conditions
  • Contents lists with descriptions, locations, and condition notes
  • Any fire department report or incident number
  • Independent testing or consultant findings, if obtained
  • Receipts for emergency expenses and temporary living costs
  • Your communication log with the carrier
  • A copy of the carrier's estimate and inspection report, if provided

For causation questions, the timeline matters. When did the fire happen, when did you first notice smoke or odor, and when was the loss reported? Photos taken early, before any cleaning, often carry more weight than later ones.

For documentation-based denials, a detailed scope of the damage may show conditions that were not captured in the original inspection. Our smoke damage scope calculator can help you think through which areas and systems may be affected. Areas that often get missed include HVAC systems, attic insulation, and contents.

Partial denials are common

Many smoke claims are not fully denied. Instead, the carrier accepts part of the loss and declines the rest. It may pay for cleaning a few rooms but deny HVAC cleaning, or accept structural damage but decline odor-affected contents.

A partial denial should be read the same way as a full one. Identify what was declined, why, and what information the decision relied on. If you believe the payment is too low rather than a portion being denied, our page on underpaid smoke damage claims covers how to compare an estimate to documented conditions.

When a public adjuster may be appropriate

A licensed public adjuster represents the policyholder, not the insurance company. Our work is about the claim itself: documenting the loss, reviewing the policy and claim file, preparing a documented claim position, and communicating with the carrier.

A public adjuster may be a good fit when:

  • The denial appears to rest on missing or incomplete documentation.
  • The carrier's inspection did not include every affected area.
  • The dispute is about the scope or extent of smoke damage.
  • You need help organizing a large contents claim.
  • You don't have the time or background to prepare the claim yourself.

We are not a restoration company. We don't clean, repair, or rebuild. We document, prepare, present, and negotiate claims. No outcome is guaranteed, and whether a denied claim can be reconsidered depends on the policy and the facts.

In Texas, public adjusters must be licensed by the Texas Department of Insurance. You can read more on our Texas public adjuster rules page.

When an attorney may be appropriate

Some denials raise legal questions that a public adjuster cannot and should not answer. If the dispute involves the interpretation of policy language in a way that may affect your legal rights, allegations of misrepresentation, a lawsuit, or questions about deadlines to take legal action, an attorney can advise on your legal rights.

It is not always one or the other. In some claims, a public adjuster handles documentation and the claim presentation while an attorney advises on legal issues. If we review a claim and believe it needs legal advice, we will tell you that plainly.

What not to do after a denial

A few practical points:

  • Don't discard damaged items before they are documented, and ask the carrier whether it wants to inspect them.
  • Don't assume the denial is final without reading it carefully and understanding the basis.
  • Don't wait indefinitely. Policies and Texas law set timeframes that may apply. Review your policy and TDI resources, and ask an attorney if you have questions about legal deadlines.
  • Don't argue by phone alone. Put important questions and responses in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a denied smoke damage claim be reconsidered?

Sometimes. Many carriers will review new or additional information. Whether a reconsideration is possible, and whether it changes the outcome, depends on why the claim was denied, your policy language, and the facts of the loss.

What should I ask for after my claim is denied?

Ask for the specific policy provisions the carrier relied on, copies of inspection or consultant reports referenced in the decision, a certified copy of your full policy, and what information would help its review. Put the request in writing.

Is a public adjuster or an attorney better for a denied claim?

It depends on the reason for the denial. A public adjuster works on documentation, scope, and claim presentation. If the dispute raises legal questions or you are considering legal action, an attorney can advise on your legal rights.

My insurer said no smoke damage was found. What does that mean?

It usually means the inspection did not document damage the carrier considered covered. Residue in HVAC systems, attics, closets, and contents can be missed in a brief inspection. Detailed photos, room-by-room notes, and sometimes testing may help a closer review.

Can wildfire or neighboring-fire smoke claims be denied?

They can be, often on causation or extent-of-damage grounds. These claims may need stronger documentation because the damage is less visible than a direct fire. Coverage depends on the policy and facts.

Does it cost anything to have my denial reviewed?

Our initial claim review is free. If you decide to hire us, fees are set in a written contract, and Texas regulates public adjuster contracts and compensation.
