## Smoke damage in a home is rarely limited to one room

A residential smoke damage claim usually starts with a single event: a kitchen fire, an electrical fire, a fire next door, or smoke from a wildfire nearby. What follows is often broader than it first looks.

Smoke moves through open doorways, attic spaces, wall cavities and the HVAC system. It settles on ceilings, walls, cabinets, flooring and belongings. Odor can remain in carpet, insulation and soft goods long after the visible smoke is gone.

## Who we help with residential claims

Residential property comes in several forms, and each has its own coverage questions.

:::cards
### Homeowners
Single-family homeowners typically carry a policy that addresses the dwelling, other structures, personal property and loss of use. Limits and terms vary by policy.

### Condo owners
Condo unit owners often have their own policy for interior finishes, improvements and contents, while the association carries a master policy. Where one ends and the other begins depends on the documents.

### Townhome owners
Townhomes can be structured like single-family homes or like condos, depending on the community. The governing documents and policies decide which applies.

### Landlords and rental owners
Rental property owners usually carry a landlord or dwelling policy. It often addresses the structure and may address lost rental income, depending on the form.

### Tenants
Tenants generally need their own renters policy for their belongings. The landlord's policy is typically focused on the building and the landlord's property.

### Owners of second homes
Vacation homes and second homes may have different policy terms, including provisions related to occupancy. Those terms matter in any claim.
:::

## Homeowners: what a typical residential claim includes

A homeowners smoke damage claim often has several parts. Which ones apply, and what limits and conditions govern them, depends on your policy.

- **The dwelling.** Walls, ceilings, floors, cabinets, fixtures, insulation, the HVAC system and other built-in parts of the home.
- **Other structures.** Detached garages, sheds, fences and similar structures, where the policy provides coverage.
- **Personal property.** Furniture, clothing, electronics and everything else you own inside the home. See [smoke damaged contents](/smoke-damaged-contents/).
- **Loss of use or additional living expenses.** Extra costs to live elsewhere while the home is not habitable, where the policy provides it.

Each part is usually handled with separate documentation and sometimes separate estimates. A strong claim treats each one carefully instead of letting one overshadow the others.

### Areas that are often underestimated

In residential smoke claims, a few areas come up again and again:

- Rooms far from the fire that were reached by smoke through open doors or the HVAC system
- Attic insulation and spaces above ceilings
- Inside cabinets, closets and drawers
- The HVAC system itself. See [HVAC smoke damage](/hvac-smoke-damage/)
- Odor that remains after initial cleaning. See [smoke odor claims](/smoke-odor-claims/)
- Soot residue on textured ceilings and walls. See [soot damage claims](/soot-damage-claims/)

## Condos, townhomes and HOA questions

Smoke claims in condos and some townhome communities raise a question single-family owners rarely face: whose policy covers what?

In general terms, a condo or homeowners association typically carries a master policy on the building or common elements. The unit owner typically carries a separate policy for their own unit interior, improvements, and belongings. But the dividing line varies. Some master policies cover the building "bare walls," some cover more of the original interior, and the association's governing documents can shift responsibility further.

We cannot give you a categorical answer without reviewing both the association's documents and the policies involved. Some questions we typically look at:

- What the master policy covers, and what it leaves to unit owners
- What the declaration and bylaws say about maintenance and insurance responsibility
- Whether your unit policy includes coverage for improvements and betterments
- Whether your unit policy includes loss assessment coverage, and on what terms
- Whether smoke from a neighboring unit changes which policy responds first

When a fire starts in another unit, several policies may be involved at once. Keeping your own documentation organized helps no matter which policy ends up responding.

:::note Get copies of the governing documents
If you own a condo or a townhome in an association, request the association's declaration, bylaws and a copy of the master policy declarations. They are often essential to sorting out who is responsible for what.
:::

## Landlords and rental property owners

If you own a rental home, duplex or small rental property, your claim may look different from an owner-occupant's.

Landlord and dwelling policies typically focus on the structure. Some include coverage for lost rental income while the property cannot be rented. Some include limited coverage for landlord-owned items like appliances or furnishings. The details depend on the policy form and endorsements.

Things that help a rental property claim:

- A copy of the lease and the monthly rent amount
- Records showing the property was rented at the time of the loss
- An inventory of landlord-owned items in the unit
- Documentation of the timeline for the unit to become rentable again

### Tenants have separate policies

Tenants' belongings are generally not part of the landlord's claim. A tenant typically needs their own renters policy for their personal property and any additional living expenses. Coordinating with tenants is still useful, since both claims involve the same event and the same property, but each policyholder deals with their own insurer.

## Additional living expenses (ALE)

If smoke damage makes a home unlivable, many homeowners and renters policies provide loss of use coverage, often called additional living expenses. Whether it applies, and for how long, depends on the policy and the facts.

In general, ALE is meant to cover the increase in living costs above what you normally spend. That can include temporary housing, extra food costs, mileage and similar items, depending on the policy's terms and limits.

Documentation matters here as much as anywhere:

- Keep every receipt for temporary housing, meals and extra expenses
- Record your normal monthly costs so the increase can be shown
- Track mileage if your commute or daily travel changed
- Keep written communication with the insurer about ALE approvals

Ask your insurer early how it wants ALE documentation submitted. Our [smoke damage claim process](/smoke-damage-claim-process/) page explains where ALE usually fits in the overall timeline.

## Frequently asked questions

:::faq
### Does homeowners insurance cover smoke damage?
Many homeowners policies address smoke damage, but coverage depends on the specific policy language, exclusions and the facts of the loss. We review the policy before drawing any conclusions about your claim.

### My neighbor's house caught fire and smoke got into mine. Can I file a claim?
You may be able to file with your own insurer. Smoke from a nearby fire can affect a home that never caught fire. Whether and how your policy responds depends on its terms, so it is worth reviewing and documenting the damage promptly.

### As a condo owner, do I file with my policy or the association's?
It depends on the master policy, your unit policy and the association's governing documents. In many cases both policies are involved in different ways. Reviewing all three is the best way to find out.

### I rent my home out. Does my policy cover my tenant's belongings?
Generally, a landlord policy does not cover a tenant's personal property. Tenants typically need their own renters policy. Your policy's terms control what it includes.

### How long will additional living expenses be paid?
That depends on your policy's limits and terms and on how long the home is not livable. Keep detailed records of your expenses and ask your insurer how it wants them submitted.

### Do I need a public adjuster for a residential smoke claim?
Not always. Some claims are straightforward. A public adjuster can be helpful when the damage is widespread, the estimate seems incomplete, or the documentation is overwhelming. A free review can help you decide.
:::
