## Public adjusters

:::faq
### What does a Smoke Damage Public Adjuster do?
A public adjuster represents the policyholder in an insurance claim. On a smoke or fire claim, that means reviewing the policy, inspecting and documenting the damage, preparing an estimate, organizing contents information, and presenting and negotiating the claim with the insurance company. We do not do repairs or cleaning. Learn more on our [About](/about/) page.

### Does a public adjuster work for my insurance company?
No. A public adjuster is licensed to represent the policyholder. The adjuster your insurance company sends, whether a staff adjuster or an independent adjuster hired by the carrier, works on behalf of the insurer.

### Can a public adjuster guarantee a larger settlement?
No. No public adjuster can guarantee a result, and you should be cautious of anyone who promises one. What a public adjuster can do is document the loss carefully and present the claim with organized support. The outcome still depends on the policy, the facts, and the insurer's evaluation.

### How are public adjusters paid in Texas?
Public adjusters in Texas are typically paid a contingency fee, meaning a percentage of the claim payment, under a signed written contract. Texas regulates public adjuster contracts and compensation. The fee and terms should be spelled out clearly in the contract before you sign. See [Texas public adjuster rules](/texas-public-adjuster-rules/) for more.

### Is a public adjuster the same as a restoration company?
No. A restoration company performs cleaning, mitigation, and repair work. A public adjuster handles the insurance claim: documentation, estimating, presentation, and negotiation. We are not a restoration contractor and we do not perform repairs on claims we adjust.

### Do I need a public adjuster or an attorney?
It depends on the situation. A public adjuster helps prepare and negotiate the claim itself; an attorney gives legal advice and can represent you in legal disputes, including litigation. If your situation involves legal questions, such as a lawsuit or allegations about the claim, talk to a licensed Texas attorney. We are not a law firm and do not give legal advice.

### Can a public adjuster review a claim after the insurance company already inspected?
Yes. Many policyholders contact a public adjuster after the carrier's inspection, often after receiving an estimate that seems incomplete. We can review the estimate, inspect the property, and compare what was documented against what we find. What can be done at that stage depends on where the claim stands and what the policy requires.
:::

## Smoke and soot damage

:::faq
### Does homeowners insurance cover smoke damage?
Many homeowners policies include smoke as a covered cause of loss, but coverage depends on the specific policy language, exclusions, and the facts of the loss. Some policies treat smoke from certain sources differently. The only reliable answer comes from reading your policy against what happened. See [smoke damage claims](/smoke-damage-claims/).

### Can smoke damage be covered without direct fire damage?
In some cases, yes. Smoke can damage a property even when the fire was in a neighboring unit, a nearby building, or a wildfire miles away. Whether that damage is covered depends on how your policy defines and treats smoke and on the evidence of damage to your property.

### Can smoke damage affect rooms far from the fire?
Yes. Smoke moves through hallways, stairwells, wall cavities, and air systems, and can leave [soot](/soot-damage-claims/) and [odor](/smoke-odor-claims/) in rooms that look untouched. That is why a room-by-room inspection of the whole property matters, not only the area around the origin.

### Can smoke affect HVAC systems?
Yes. When a system is running during a fire, or when smoke is drawn into returns, residue can be deposited in ductwork, registers, and components, and the system can spread odor to other rooms. Whether and how that is addressed in a claim depends on documentation and the policy. See [HVAC smoke damage](/hvac-smoke-damage/). We do not give HVAC repair advice; a qualified HVAC professional should evaluate the system.

### Can you help with wildfire smoke?
Yes. Wildfire smoke can affect properties well outside the burn area, leaving residue and odor inside homes and businesses. Coverage depends on the policy and on documented damage to your property. See [Texas fire and smoke events](/texas-fire-smoke-events/) for information on recent incidents.
:::

## Contents

:::faq
### Can smoke-damaged furniture be replaced?
Sometimes. Whether furniture is cleaned or replaced depends on the material, the extent of residue and odor, whether cleaning is likely to restore it, and how your policy handles personal property. Upholstered and porous items often raise different questions than hard surfaces. See [smoke-damaged contents](/smoke-damaged-contents/).

### What happens to smoke-damaged electronics?
Smoke residue can get inside electronics, and some residues are corrosive. Whether an item is cleaned, tested, or replaced depends on its condition, professional evaluation, and the policy. Document each item with photos, make, model, and age where possible.

### Can a public adjuster help with contents?
Yes. We help policyholders build organized inventories with descriptions, quantities, ages, and supporting photos, and we present the contents portion of the claim to the insurer. Contents are often the most time-consuming part of a smoke claim. See [smoke-damaged contents](/smoke-damaged-contents/).

### How should I inventory personal property?
Go room by room. For each item, record a description, quantity, brand or model if known, approximate age, and where you bought it if you remember. Photograph items in place before anything is removed if it is safe to do so. Our [contents RCV/ACV calculator](/tools/contents-rcv-acv-calculator/) can help you organize values.

### What if my insurance company says items can be cleaned?
Cleaning is a legitimate option for many items, and it is often the carrier's first approach. If you believe items were not restored by cleaning, or were not suitable for cleaning in the first place, document their condition after cleaning, including residue and odor, and ask for a closer review. The outcome depends on the evidence and the policy.
:::

## Claim problems

:::faq
### How do I document smoke damage?
Photograph every room, including closets, cabinets, attics, and HVAC registers, and take close-ups of residue on surfaces. Keep notes on odor and where it is strongest. Save every document and receipt related to the loss. See [the smoke damage claim process](/smoke-damage-claim-process/).

### What documents should I save after a fire?
Keep your policy and declarations page, all letters and emails from the insurer, estimates, photos and videos, receipts for temporary housing, meals, and other expenses, invoices from any contractors or restoration companies, and fire department information if available. Keep copies in one place, ideally digital.

### What if my smoke damage claim was underpaid?
Start by comparing the carrier's estimate to the damage you can document. Underpayments often come from missing rooms, missing line items, contents questions, or valuation differences. A public adjuster can review the estimate and present supporting information for a closer review. See [underpaid smoke damage claims](/underpaid-smoke-damage-claims/).

### What if my smoke damage claim was denied?
Read the denial letter carefully and note the policy provisions it cites. A denial may be based on coverage language, the cause of loss, timing, or the evidence available. A public adjuster can review whether additional documentation may be relevant, and in some situations an attorney may be the right person to consult. See [denied smoke damage claims](/denied-smoke-damage-claims/).

### What if my claim is already closed?
A closed claim is not always the end of the discussion. Depending on the policy, the timing, and the circumstances, it may be possible to request that the claim be reopened or to submit additional information. Policies contain time limits and other deadlines may apply, so it is worth having the claim reviewed promptly.

### Do I have to use the insurer's preferred contractor or vendor?
Generally, insurers may recommend contractors or vendors, but whether you are required to use them depends on your policy and the circumstances. Read your policy and any correspondence carefully, and ask the insurer in writing if it is unclear. The choice can affect how work is scoped and documented, so keep records of everything.

### What should I do first after a fire?
Safety comes first. Follow the instructions of fire officials and do not re-enter the property until it is cleared. Then notify your insurance company, document the damage with photos and video when it is safe, and keep receipts for any expenses you incur. We do not give cleanup or safety instructions beyond that; follow the guidance of qualified professionals.
:::

## Values and depreciation

:::faq
### What is RCV?
RCV stands for replacement cost value: the cost to repair or replace damaged property with materials or items of like kind and quality, without deducting for depreciation. Whether and how RCV is paid depends on your policy.

### What is ACV?
ACV stands for actual cash value. It is commonly calculated as replacement cost minus depreciation, though how ACV is defined and calculated depends on the policy. Many policies pay ACV first and the remainder later under certain conditions.

### What is depreciation?
Depreciation is a reduction in value to account for age, wear, and condition. In a claim, it is the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value. How depreciation is calculated can significantly affect a contents claim, so it is worth reviewing line by line.

### What is recoverable depreciation?
Recoverable depreciation is depreciation that may be paid later, typically after an item is actually repaired or replaced, if your policy provides replacement cost coverage. Policies often set conditions and time limits for recovering it, so read yours carefully. Our [contents RCV/ACV calculator](/tools/contents-rcv-acv-calculator/) shows how these values relate.

### What is Additional Living Expense?
Additional Living Expense, sometimes called ALE or Loss of Use, is coverage that may pay increased living costs when your home is not livable because of a covered loss, such as temporary housing and extra meal costs above normal. Limits and conditions vary by policy. Keep receipts for everything.
:::
