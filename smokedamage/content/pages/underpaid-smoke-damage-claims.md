Smoke damage is harder to measure than a burned wall. Residue settles on ceilings, inside closets, behind furniture, and into ductwork. Odor gets into soft materials. Two people can walk through the same house and write very different estimates.

That is why many smoke damage claims that feel underpaid are really questions of scope. What did the estimate include, what did it leave out, and what method of repair or cleaning did it assume?

This page walks through how to review a carrier's estimate against documented conditions and where the gaps often appear.

## Start with the estimate itself

Ask for the carrier's full estimate, not just the payment summary. A full estimate usually lists each room or area, line items with quantities and unit prices, and totals for depreciation and any amounts held back.

Then read it room by room. For each area, ask:

- Is this room in the estimate at all?
- Do the measurements look right?
- Does the line item match what you see, such as cleaning versus sealing versus replacement?
- Are ceilings, walls, floors, trim, doors, cabinets, fixtures, and windows each addressed?

A payment that feels too low often traces back to a handful of specific line items or missing areas. Finding them is more useful than disputing the total.

## Compare line items to documented conditions

The strongest review lines up each estimate item with photos and notes of the actual condition. If the estimate says "clean walls" for a room where the paint is discolored and odor persists after cleaning, that difference should be documented.

Useful documentation includes:

- Photos of each surface, including close-ups showing residue
- Wipe tests or residue photos on light-colored surfaces
- Notes on odor by room and material
- Any testing or consultant findings, if obtained
- Contractor or restoration company scopes and invoices

Keep the comparison factual. The point is to show what exists, not to argue about the carrier's intent.

## Rooms and areas that are often left out

Smoke travels. Areas far from the fire can be affected, and they are also the easiest to miss during a short inspection. Common gaps include:

- **Attic spaces and insulation**, where smoke can settle and odor can linger
- **HVAC systems and ductwork**, which can move residue throughout the home. See [HVAC smoke damage](/hvac-smoke-damage/).
- **Closets, cabinets, and drawers**, including their contents
- **Rooms on other floors** or at the opposite end of the building
- **Garages and detached structures**, depending on the policy
- **Light fixtures, outlets, and ceiling fans**
- **Window treatments, carpet pad, and upholstery**

## Cleaning versus replacement

Many smoke estimates assume cleaning. Sometimes that is appropriate. Sometimes cleaning does not fully address residue, staining, or odor, and a different method may be needed.

The question is not which is more expensive. It is which method the documented condition supports. Relevant factors can include:

- The type of residue and the material it is on
- Whether earlier cleaning attempts removed staining and odor
- Porous materials such as drywall, insulation, carpet, and fabric
- Whether matching or uniform appearance is an issue, depending on the policy

If cleaning has already been tried and residue or odor remains, document it with photos and notes. That record can support a request for a closer review of the method the estimate assumes. Policy language and the facts decide how these questions are resolved.

## Contents claims

Contents are often the most time-consuming part of a smoke claim and one of the most common sources of underpayment. Items may be left off the inventory, grouped too broadly, or valued without enough detail.

A strong contents inventory lists each item with:

- Description, brand, and model where possible
- Room location
- Approximate age and quantity
- Condition and type of damage
- Replacement cost of a similar item

Clothing, bedding, electronics, books, and soft furnishings are easy to under-list. Items in closets and storage areas are frequently missed. See [smoke-damaged contents](/smoke-damaged-contents/) for more detail.

## Depreciation, ACV and RCV holdback

Many property policies pay in two stages. The first payment is often the **actual cash value (ACV)**, which is the replacement cost minus depreciation. The remaining amount, sometimes called the **replacement cost holdback** or recoverable depreciation, may be paid after the item is repaired or replaced, depending on the policy.

When reviewing depreciation, look at:

- Whether depreciation was applied to items where the policy may not allow it
- Whether the age and condition used are reasonable
- Whether labor was depreciated, which depends on policy language and applicable rules
- What the policy requires to recover the holdback, and any timeframes it sets

Policy wording controls these questions, so read the loss settlement section of your policy carefully. Our [contents RCV / ACV calculator](/tools/contents-rcv-acv-calculator/) can help you see how depreciation affects a contents total.

| Term | What it generally means |
|---|---|
| RCV (replacement cost value) | Cost to replace with a similar new item or repair with like materials |
| Depreciation | Reduction for age, wear, and condition |
| ACV (actual cash value) | Generally RCV minus depreciation; policy definitions vary |
| Holdback / recoverable depreciation | Amount that may be paid after repair or replacement, per the policy |

## Supplements

A supplement is a request for additional payment based on damage or costs not included in the original estimate. Supplements are common on smoke claims because hidden damage often appears once cleaning or repairs begin.

A supplement is stronger when it includes:

- A clear description of what was found and where
- Photos showing the condition
- A line-item scope or invoice
- An explanation of why it was not visible or included earlier

Submit supplements in writing and keep copies. Ask the carrier to confirm receipt.

## Reopening a claim

If a claim was closed and you later find additional damage, many policies allow the claim to be reopened or supplemented. Whether that is possible depends on your policy, the facts, and any applicable timeframes. Texas law and your policy set timelines; check your policy and [TDI resources](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/consumer/), and ask an attorney if you have questions about legal deadlines.

## Frequently asked questions

:::faq
### How do I know if my smoke damage claim was underpaid?
Compare the carrier's full estimate to the conditions in each room. Look for missing rooms, missing surfaces, cleaning where residue or odor remains, incomplete contents, and depreciation that seems inconsistent. Specific gaps are more useful than the total alone.

### Can I ask the insurer to review its estimate again?
Yes, you can request a closer review in writing and provide supporting documentation such as photos, scopes, and invoices. Whether the estimate changes depends on the policy and the facts.

### What is a supplement on a smoke damage claim?
A supplement is a request for additional payment for damage or costs not in the original estimate, often found once cleaning or repairs begin. It works best with photos, a scope, and a clear explanation.

### Why was depreciation taken from my payment?
Many policies pay actual cash value first and hold back recoverable depreciation until repair or replacement. How depreciation applies depends on your policy's loss settlement terms.

### Are HVAC systems included in smoke damage estimates?
Sometimes they are and sometimes they are missed. Whether HVAC cleaning or related work is covered depends on the policy and documented conditions. See our HVAC smoke damage page for more.

### Can I reopen a smoke damage claim that was already paid?
Many policies allow supplements or reopening when additional damage is found. It depends on your policy, the facts, and any applicable timeframes.

### Do you do the cleaning or repairs?
No. We are public adjusters, not a restoration company. We document, prepare, present, and negotiate claims on behalf of policyholders.
