Good bikepacking food is calorie-dense (100+ calories per ounce), easy to eat on the move, and planned around where you can resupply. On the bike, eat a small amount every 30 to 60 minutes rather than big meals; long days in the saddle can burn 3,000–5,000+ calories, and almost no one can replace all of that, so the goal is to keep the deficit small. Carry your snacks where you can reach them without stopping.

How much you need to eat

Calorie burn depends on body weight, terrain, bike and load, pace and hours of riding. A few useful planning numbers:

  • On the bike: roughly 200–300 calories per hour is a common target for sustained riding. Some riders tolerate more, especially from mixed real food and drink mix.
  • Per day: long, hilly bikepacking days commonly burn 3,000–5,000 calories or more. Plan to carry and eat 3,000–4,500 calories a day, adjusted for your size and appetite.
  • Food weight: at 100–125 calories per ounce, that's about 1.5–2.5 lb (0.7–1.1 kg) of food per day.

Choosing food by calorie density

Fat has about 9 calories per gram; carbohydrate and protein about 4. That's why nuts and oils are the lightest way to carry energy, and why fresh fruit and canned food are heavy for what they give you. Check the label: calories per serving divided by the serving weight in ounces.

Eating on the bike

  • Start eating in the first hour and keep going. If you wait until you're hungry or weak, you're already behind.
  • Use a timer or landmarks — every 30–45 minutes, or every climb — as a reminder.
  • Keep snacks within reach. Stem or "feed" bags beside the handlebars and a top tube bag like the ROCKBROS top tube bag let you eat without stopping. If you have to dig into a seat bag, you won't eat enough.
  • Mix sugar and real food. Gels and chews work for hard climbs; nuts, bars and tortillas hold you longer between.
  • Replace salt. In heat you can lose a lot of sodium in sweat. Salty snacks or electrolyte drink mix help prevent cramps and the nausea that comes from water alone.

Meals at camp: stove or no stove

With a stove

A small canister stove makes hot dinners and morning coffee possible, which matters more on cold or wet trips than the few ounces it weighs. An integrated system like the Jetboil Flash cooking system boils water fast and efficiently, which is all most bikepacking meals need. Good hot meals:

  • Instant ramen with olive oil and a foil pack of tuna or chicken
  • Instant mashed potatoes with cheese and bacon bits
  • Couscous with dried vegetables and a bouillon cube
  • Freeze-dried meals (lightest and easiest, but expensive)
  • Oatmeal with powdered milk, nut butter and dried fruit for breakfast

Stoveless

Going stoveless saves weight, fuel and time, and many riders on routes with frequent towns never cook. Options:

  • Cold soaking: add water to couscous, instant refried beans, ramen or oats in a screw-top jar and let it soak 30–60 minutes while you ride or set up camp.
  • Wraps: tortillas with peanut butter and honey, or cheese and salami.
  • Ready-to-eat: bars, trail mix, and pouches of tuna or chicken.

A long-handled spoon such as the Sea to Summit long-handled spoon reaches the bottom of meal pouches and jars without getting food on your hands.

Planning resupply

Most bikepacking routes pass through towns, gas stations and small stores, and resupply strategy determines how much you carry.

  1. Map your resupply points before the trip, with distances between them. Route guides usually list them.
  2. Check hours. Rural stores may close early, on Sundays, or for the season. Don't plan to arrive at 6 p.m. and assume it's open.
  3. Carry enough to reach the next store plus a buffer — extra calories for a breakdown, a closed store, or a slow day.
  4. Eat a real meal in town when you can, and buy for the next stretch only after you've eaten. Shopping hungry leads to buying too much of the wrong thing.
  5. Mail drops work for remote routes with long gaps between stores, but post office hours constrain your schedule.

Gas-station resupply that works

  • Peanuts, cashews and trail mix
  • Peanut butter or nut butter pouches
  • Tortillas, bagels, honey buns, pastries
  • Candy bars, cookies, chips
  • Jerky, meat sticks, string cheese
  • Electrolyte drinks and something fresh to eat right there — a banana, a sandwich, chocolate milk

Vegetarian, vegan and other diets

  • Vegetarian / vegan: nuts, seeds, nut butter, instant refried beans, hummus powder, textured vegetable protein, couscous and coconut oil cover calories and protein. Vegan options are harder to find at gas stations; carry more between towns.
  • Gluten-free: rice tortillas, instant rice, instant potatoes, corn chips, and many bars. Plan resupply carefully, since small stores may have few options.
  • Low-carb or high-fat: possible for steady, moderate riding, but most riders perform better on hard climbs with some carbohydrates. Don't experiment on a big trip.

Whatever you eat, test it on long training rides first. A trip is not the time to discover that a new bar upsets your stomach.

Fresh food

Fresh food is heavy, but a little goes a long way for morale. Apples, carrots, and hard cheese survive a day or two in a bag. Eat fresh items first and save shelf-stable food for later in each stretch. Avoid raw meat or dairy that needs refrigeration unless you'll eat it within hours.

Packing and storing food

  • Front of the bike: snacks for the day in feed bags and the top tube bag.
  • Frame bag: the heaviest food, since weight low and centered handles best.
  • Seat bag and handlebar roll: dinner, breakfast and lighter, bulky items.
  • Repackage food into zip bags to remove boxes and air, and to keep things dry.
  • At night, store food according to local regulations. In bear country that may mean a bear-resistant container, bear box, or a proper hang; in many areas it also means keeping food, trash and scented items away from your tent. Small rodents will chew through bags left in panniers.
Safety: Some public lands require approved bear-resistant food storage. Check the rules for every land unit your route crosses — national parks, national forests and state lands can differ — before you choose your bags.

Water and electrolytes

Food and water planning go together: dehydration ruins appetite, and salty food makes you thirstier. On hot days you may need 0.5–1 liter or more per hour. Know where water sources are, carry a filter, and carry enough to reach the next source with margin.
