To measure torso length for a backpack, have a helper run a soft measuring tape from the C7 vertebra (the bony bump at the base of your neck) down your spine to the line between the tops of your hip bones (the iliac crest). That number, usually somewhere between 15 and 23 inches for adults, is what backpack sizes are based on, not your height. Most brands size roughly S at 16–18 in, M at 18–20 in and L at 20–22 in, but each manufacturer's chart is slightly different.

Why torso length matters more than height

A backpacking pack transfers most of its weight to your hips through the hip belt, with the shoulder straps mainly keeping it close to your back. For that to work, the distance between the hip belt and the shoulder harness has to match the distance between your hips and shoulders.

  • Pack too long: the hip belt sits on your hips, but the shoulder straps rise above your shoulders and leave a gap. The load pulls backward and the pack feels unstable.
  • Pack too short: the shoulder harness sits low and the load drops onto your shoulders, or the hip belt rides up onto your stomach. Shoulder and neck pain follow within a few miles.

Height is a poor guide because people of the same height have different leg-to-torso proportions. A 5'10" person with long legs can need a smaller pack than a 5'7" person with a long torso.

What you need

  • A flexible tape measure. A cloth or vinyl sewing tape follows the curve of your spine. A rigid metal tape gives a less accurate number. Any soft tape works, like this flexible measuring tape.
  • A helper. Measuring your own back is possible but less accurate.
  • A thin shirt, or no shirt, so bulky clothing doesn't hide your landmarks.
  • Optional: a washable marker or two small pieces of tape to mark the landmarks.

Step by step: measuring your torso

1. Find the C7 vertebra

Stand up straight and tilt your head forward, chin toward your chest. Run your fingers down the back of your neck: the most prominent bump where your neck meets your shoulders is C7. If two bumps stand out, it's usually the upper one that moves when you tilt your head; the lower one (T1, the first thoracic vertebra) stays put. Mark C7 with tape.

2. Find the iliac crest

Place your hands on your hips with your thumbs pointing toward your back and your fingers forward. Slide your hands down until you feel the top of your hip bones; that bony shelf is the iliac crest. It's usually higher than most people expect, closer to your belly button height than to your belt line.

Your thumbs, pointing backward, now mark the ends of an imaginary horizontal line across your lower back. The point where that line crosses your spine is the bottom of your torso measurement.

3. Measure between the two points

Stand up straight with your head level. Your helper holds the end of the tape at C7 and runs it down along the curve of your spine to the point between your thumbs. Read the number in inches.

4. Measure twice

Repeat the measurement. If the two readings differ by more than half an inch, find the landmarks again and do a third. Use the average.

5. Measure your hips too

While you have the tape out, measure around your body at the iliac crest (not at your waist or belt line). Many packs offer hip belts in different sizes, and the backpack size calculator uses both numbers.

Common measuring mistakes

  • Measuring to the belt line. Pants usually sit below the iliac crest, which adds an inch or more and points you to a pack that's too long.
  • Slouching or arching. Stand naturally upright with your head level; tilting your head forward is only for finding C7.
  • Measuring over a thick jacket. Bulky clothes hide landmarks.
  • Using a straight rigid tape. Follow the curve of your spine with a soft tape.

Why brand charts differ

Every manufacturer designs its suspension a little differently, so one brand's medium might cover 18–20.5 inches while another's covers 17.5–19.5. Some packs come in only two or three sizes with overlapping ranges, and some have adjustable torsos covering a wide span.

If you're between sizes

  • Choose an adjustable-torso pack if you can. Many backpacking packs, including the Osprey Atmos AG 65, let you slide the harness up or down.
  • Try both sizes loaded with 15 to 25 pounds of weight. Walk around, and use stairs if you can.
  • Prioritize hip belt position. The belt should center on your iliac crest. If the shoulder straps fit but the belt is wrong, the pack is the wrong size.

Daypacks under about 30 liters are often one-size or come in two sizes, and fit is less critical because loads are light. Small daypacks like the Osprey Talon 22 daypack still come in torso-based sizes, so your measurement is useful there too.

Fitting the pack once you have it

Load the pack with the weight you'll actually carry, loosen every strap, then tighten in this order:

  1. Hip belt: center the padded wings over your iliac crest and tighten firmly. Most of the weight should now rest on your hips.
  2. Shoulder straps: pull them snug so they wrap over the top of your shoulders with no gap, but don't haul the weight off your hips.
  3. Load lifters: the small straps from the top of the shoulder straps to the pack. Snug them so they angle back at roughly 45 degrees, which pulls the load close to your body.
  4. Sternum strap: clip it about an inch below your collarbones and tighten just enough to keep the shoulder straps in place without restricting breathing.

Signs the torso length is right: the shoulder straps attach to the pack an inch or two below the top of your shoulders, the load lifters can angle up and back, and you can loosen the shoulder straps briefly without the hip belt slipping.

Tip: On the trail, shift weight between your hips and shoulders through the day by slightly loosening or tightening the shoulder straps. Small changes prevent hot spots on long days.

Special cases

Short torsos

Look at women's-specific or XS packs regardless of gender; they're built with shorter torso ranges and narrower shoulder straps. Make sure the hip belt can still tighten enough.

Long torsos

Check whether a model comes in XL or tall before buying. A pack that's too short for you puts weight on your shoulders no matter how you adjust it.
