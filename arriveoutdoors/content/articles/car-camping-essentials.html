Car camping essentials come down to five groups: a warm, comfortable place to sleep, a simple kitchen, a camp setup for light and shelter, safety and first aid, and a few vehicle supplies so a dead battery doesn't end your trip. Because you're not carrying it on your back, you can bring heavier and more comfortable gear than backpackers do, but a checklist still matters: the most common trip-ruiners are a forgotten lighter, a pad that's too thin and not enough water.

Sleep system

A good night's sleep makes or breaks a car camping trip, and it's where spending a little more pays off.

  • Tent with rainfly, stakes and a footprint or ground tarp. A tent rated for one more person than you have gives room for bags. Or sleep in the vehicle: see the Honda CR-V camper guide for a setup that works in most small SUVs.
  • Sleeping bag rated about 10°F below the lowest temperature you expect. Use the sleeping bag and pad temperature guide to match the rating to your trip.
  • Sleeping pad with enough insulation (R-value) for the season. A thick self-inflating pad or a foam-plus-air combination is far warmer than a bare air mattress.
  • Pillow: bring your pillow from home. There's no weight penalty.
  • Extra blanket for cold nights or to sit around the fire.
  • Earplugs and an eye mask for busy campgrounds and early sunrises.
Tip: Air mattresses feel great in the store and cold at camp, because the air inside pulls heat from your body. Below about 50°F (10°C), put a closed-cell foam pad or a wool blanket between you and the mattress.

Camp kitchen

Cooking

  • Two-burner propane stove and enough fuel. A standard 1-pound propane canister lasts about an hour or two of cooking on high depending on the stove; bring a spare.
  • Lighter and backup matches in a waterproof container (the single most commonly forgotten item).
  • Pot, frying pan and a kettle or coffee maker.
  • Cooking utensils: spatula, large spoon, tongs, sharp knife and small cutting board.
  • Plates, bowls, mugs and cutlery for each person.

Food and water

  • Cooler with ice or ice packs. Pre-chill it the night before, and keep drinks in a separate cooler so the food cooler stays closed.
  • Water: at least 1 gallon (about 4 liters) per person per day for drinking and cooking, plus extra for dishes. A 5- to 7-gallon jug with a spigot is easier than many small bottles.
  • Meals planned by day so you don't overpack. The camp meal planner estimates how much food your group needs.
  • Coffee or tea, salt, pepper, oil and any spices you use.

Cleanup

  • Collapsible wash basin or two (wash and rinse), biodegradable soap, a sponge and a quick-dry towel.
  • Trash and recycling bags. Pack out everything if the campground has no bins.
  • Paper towels and a few zip-top bags for leftovers.
Safety: Never run a stove, heater or grill inside a tent or vehicle. Carbon monoxide builds up quickly in enclosed spaces. In bear country, store food, coolers and scented items in a hard-sided vehicle or a bear locker as the campground requires; never in your tent.

Camp setup and comfort

  • Camp chairs for everyone.
  • Folding table if your site doesn't have a picnic table, or for a separate cooking area.
  • Headlamp for each person, with spare batteries or a charged battery.
  • Lantern for the table and tent.
  • Tarp or canopy with cord, for shade and rain. A 10x10-foot tarp over the picnic table makes a wet trip bearable.
  • Mallet for stakes in hard ground, plus a few spare stakes.
  • Multi-tool or knife, and duct tape.
  • Firewood bought locally (moving firewood spreads invasive insects, and many areas ban it), plus fire starters. Check fire restrictions before you go and know how to put out a campfire completely.
  • Power bank or portable power station for phones and lights.

Clothing

  • Rain jacket, even if the forecast is dry.
  • An insulated layer and a warm hat; nights are often 20 to 30°F colder than daytime highs, especially in the desert and mountains.
  • Camp shoes or sandals and a dry pair of socks for every day.
  • Sun hat and sunglasses.

Hygiene

  • Toothbrush, toothpaste and personal toiletries.
  • Toilet paper and hand sanitizer.
  • Biodegradable wipes for a quick clean-up.
  • Sunscreen and insect repellent.

Safety and navigation

  • First aid kit with bandages, blister care, pain relievers, antihistamines, tweezers for ticks and splinters, and any personal medications.
  • Offline maps: download maps for your area before you lose signal. A paper map of the region is a good backup.
  • Campsite reservation and permits, saved offline or printed.
  • Whistle for each child, and a plan for what to do if someone gets separated. See what to do if you get lost in the woods.
  • Weather check: look at the forecast for the campsite itself, not the nearest town, using the trail weather forecast.
  • Fire extinguisher (a small vehicle-rated one) if you'll be cooking a lot.

Vehicle essentials

Many campsites are at the end of long gravel roads with no cell service, so your car is part of your safety gear.

  • Spare tire checked for pressure, plus the jack and lug wrench. Know how to use them.
  • Jumper cables or a portable jump starter. Running lights and chargers off the car battery overnight is the most common way to get stranded.
  • Tire pressure gauge and a small 12V air compressor if you'll drive on dirt roads.
  • Full tank of gas before you leave the last town.
  • Extra water beyond your camp supply, in case you're stuck.
  • Small shovel for digging out of sand, mud or snow.
  • Paper towels and a trash bag for the drive home.

If you sleep in the vehicle

  • Sleeping pad or SUV mattress sized to your cargo floor.
  • Window covers for privacy and mesh screens so you can crack the windows for airflow.
  • A battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm.
  • Bins to move gear to the front seats at night.

How to pack the car

  • Use bins by category: kitchen, sleep, camp, clothes. It's faster to find things and to pack up.
  • Load in reverse order: things you'll need last (sleeping bags) go in first; things you need on arrival (tent, chairs, water) go in last, near the hatch.
  • Keep heavy items low and forward so they don't shift or become dangerous in a sudden stop.
  • Leave a "day bag" in the front seat with snacks, water, sunscreen, maps and a layer.
  • Keep a permanent camping bin stocked with the small stuff (lighter, utensils, headlamps, first aid) so most of your checklist is already packed for the next trip.
