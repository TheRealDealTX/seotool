Cloud Peak is the 13,167-foot (4,013 m) high point of Wyoming's Bighorn Mountains, at the center of the Cloud Peak Wilderness in Bighorn National Forest. The standard route is non-technical but long: roughly 24 miles round trip from the West Tensleep Trailhead via Mistymoon Lake, finishing with a tiring boulder field. Most people climb it as a two- or three-day backpacking trip in July through early September.

The Bighorns rise abruptly from the plains between Sheridan and Buffalo on the east and the Bighorn Basin on the west. Unlike much of the Rockies, the high country here sees relatively light traffic, and the wilderness holds hundreds of lakes in glacier-carved basins. For background on the area and its climate, see the Cloud Peak Wilderness destination guide.

Getting to the trailhead

The West Tensleep Trailhead is the most-used starting point. From US 16 (the Cloud Peak Skyway between Buffalo and Ten Sleep), take the signed Forest Service road north to West Tensleep Lake. The road is gravel and usually passable for regular cars in dry summer conditions. The trailhead sits around 9,000 feet, so if you're coming from low elevation, spending a night at a nearby Forest Service campground first is an easy way to acclimate.

Other trailheads, including Battle Park on the west side and several on the east side of the range, offer longer or quieter approaches. They're worth considering if you want a loop or more solitude, but the West Tensleep route is the most straightforward first visit.

The standard route: West Tensleep to the summit

Trailhead to Mistymoon Lake

The trail climbs gradually along the West Tensleep drainage, past lakes and meadows, to Mistymoon Lake near treeline, roughly 6 to 7 miles from the trailhead. This is the usual high camp. It is popular, so arrive early in the afternoon if you want a good site, and camp on durable ground well away from the water.

Mistymoon to the summit

From Mistymoon, the route heads toward the southwest slopes of Cloud Peak. The trail fades and you follow cairns across tundra and then up a long boulder field of large, sometimes unstable blocks. This section is slow: plan on one mile per hour or less. Most of it is class 2, but if you drift off the best line near the top you can end up on steeper, more exposed easy class 3 ground. If the terrain suddenly gets harder, look around for cairns before pushing on. Our guide to scrambling classes explains the difference.

Typical itineraries

  • Two days: hike to Mistymoon Lake on day one; summit and hike out on day two (a very long second day).
  • Three days: hike to Mistymoon; summit and return to camp; hike out. This is the more comfortable and safer plan, because it lets you start the summit day before dawn without a heavy pack.
  • Single day: strong, acclimated hikers occasionally do it in one push. It is a very long day at altitude with little margin for weather.

Permits and wilderness rules

Cloud Peak Wilderness asks visitors to fill out a free registration at the trailhead; there is no paid permit for most trips. Standard wilderness rules apply: no motorized or mechanized equipment (including bikes and drones), and group-size limits. Campfire restrictions apply in parts of the wilderness and can tighten during dry periods. Rules change, so check the Bighorn National Forest website or call the ranger district before your trip.

Follow Leave No Trace practices: camp at least 200 feet from lakes and streams where possible, bury human waste in catholes 6 to 8 inches deep at least 200 feet from water, and pack out everything else.

Wildlife and safety

  • Bears: the Bighorns are black bear country. Store food, trash and scented items in a bear-resistant canister or hang them properly, and cook away from your tent.
  • Moose: common in willow-lined valleys. Give them plenty of room; a moose with ears pinned back is warning you.
  • Water: lakes and streams are plentiful, but treat everything. A filter like the Sawyer Squeeze water filter works well; see our Sawyer Squeeze gravity setup guide for filtering at camp.
  • Altitude: headache, nausea and poor sleep at Mistymoon are common for people coming from low elevation. Descending is the reliable fix if symptoms get worse.
  • Navigation: above Mistymoon the route is cairned but not a maintained trail. In cloud, the boulder field all looks the same. Carry a map and offline GPS, and know what to do if you get lost.
Safety: Afternoon thunderstorms are common in the Bighorns in summer, and the upper boulder field and summit offer no shelter. Start your summit push early, ideally before dawn from Mistymoon, and turn around if clouds start building by late morning. Wet lichen-covered boulders are also extremely slippery.

When to go

The practical season is July through early September. In June and early July, snow commonly covers parts of the route above Mistymoon and creek crossings run high; an early-season attempt may need traction and snow skills. By September, nights at Mistymoon can drop below freezing and early snowstorms are possible. Mosquitoes are typically worst in July.

Check the trail weather forecast for the trailhead and summit, and plan for cold, wet weather even in midsummer.

Gear for a Cloud Peak trip

  • Backpacking pack sized for two or three nights; a 50 to 65 liter pack like the Osprey Atmos AG 65 carries a bear canister comfortably. Make sure it fits your torso: see how to measure torso length.
  • A light daypack or summit sack for the summit day from camp.
  • Trekking poles for stream crossings and the descent, such as the Black Diamond Trail Sport trekking poles. Stow them on the boulder field so your hands are free.
  • Warm sleep system: a bag rated to around freezing or below for summer nights near treeline.
  • Layers: rain shell, insulated jacket, hat and gloves. Expect the summit to be roughly 15°F colder than the trailhead, and wind makes it feel far colder.
  • Bear canister and, if you choose, Counter Assault bear spray carried where you can reach it.
  • Sturdy footwear with good grip for the boulders.
