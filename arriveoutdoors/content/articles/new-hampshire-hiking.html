The best New Hampshire hiking is in the White Mountains: the Franconia Ridge Loop and the Presidential Range for big above-treeline days, Lonesome Lake, Arethusa Falls and the Welch-Dickey Loop for shorter outings, and Mount Monadnock in the south for a classic half-day summit. These are rocky, steep trails, slower than their mileage suggests, and the high peaks have weather that has killed hikers in every month. Plan around the parking pass, the season and the summit forecast, not just the distance.

Try it: Trail Weather Forecast shows wind, freezing level and a go/no-go hike score for any trailhead. For anything above treeline, also check the higher-summits forecast from the Mount Washington Observatory.

Franconia Ridge Loop

This is the hike people mean when they say "the best hike in New Hampshire." The loop combines the Falling Waters Trail, the Franconia Ridge Trail (part of the Appalachian Trail), the Greenleaf Trail and the Old Bridle Path, starting from the Lafayette Place area off the Franconia Notch Parkway (I-93).

  • Direction: Most hikers go up Falling Waters and down Old Bridle Path. Falling Waters passes several waterfalls and is steep, wet and slabby, which is safer to climb than descend. Old Bridle Path gives a gentler descent with views of the ridge you just walked.
  • The ridge: From Little Haystack (4,760 ft) over Mount Lincoln (5,089 ft) to Mount Lafayette (5,260 ft) you're above treeline for well over a mile, with no shelter from wind or lightning.
  • Greenleaf Hut: The AMC hut below Lafayette on the descent sells snacks and water in season and is a good bail-out point.
  • Parking: The lots fill very early on summer and fall weekends. Parking on the parkway shoulder is prohibited and enforced.
Safety: Stay on the trail and the rock above treeline. The alpine plants on Franconia Ridge and the Presidentials are rare and fragile. Turn back before the ridge if thunderstorms, high wind or cloud with freezing temperatures are forecast; there is no quick way off.

Mount Washington and the Presidential Range

At 6,288 feet, Mount Washington is the highest peak in the northeastern United States, and it's notorious for some of the most severe weather in the US. Storm tracks converge on the range, and the summit can be in hurricane-force wind, freezing fog and near-zero visibility while the valley is sunny and mild. The trail signs at treeline warn hikers to turn back in bad weather, and people have died of exposure there even in summer.

Main routes

  • Tuckerman Ravine Trail from the AMC Pinkham Notch Visitor Center (east side): the most popular route, about 4.2 miles one way, climbing through a glacial cirque with a steep headwall. The headwall section can close in spring because of snow and ice; use Lion Head Trail when it does.
  • Ammonoosuc Ravine Trail (west side): passes waterfalls and steep slabs to the AMC Lakes of the Clouds Hut, then climbs the summit cone. Often paired with the Jewell Trail for a loop.
  • Presidential Traverse: a one-way hike of roughly 20 miles over Madison, Adams, Jefferson, Washington, Monroe, Eisenhower and Pierce, with a car shuttle. Most of it is above treeline. Only attempt it with a stable forecast and a bail-out plan.

The summit has a state park building and is reachable by the Auto Road and the Cog Railway in season, so expect crowds on top. Don't count on the summit building as shelter: it isn't always open, especially outside summer.

How to decide to go up

  • Read the Mount Washington Observatory's higher-summits forecast the morning of your hike, plus the National Weather Service Gray, Maine office forecast.
  • Carry a warm hat, gloves, an insulated jacket, a windproof shell and wind pants, even in July. The wind chill calculator shows how fast 40°F and 50 mph becomes dangerous.
  • Set a turnaround time and use it. If you can't see the next cairn, go down.

Shorter and family-friendly hikes

Lonesome Lake

From Lafayette Place, the Lonesome Lake Trail climbs steadily to a mountain pond at about 2,700 feet with a view straight across to Franconia Ridge. A short loop circles the lake, and the AMC Lonesome Lake Hut sits at the south end. It's the best "big view, moderate effort" hike in Franconia Notch and a good choice for kids who can handle a steady climb.

Arethusa Falls

In Crawford Notch State Park, the Arethusa Falls Trail climbs through hardwood forest to a waterfall around 140 feet tall, one of the tallest in New Hampshire. It's best in spring and after rain. For a longer loop, return via Frankenstein Cliff for a view over the notch.

Welch-Dickey Loop

This loop near Waterville Valley climbs two modest peaks with long sections of open granite ledge and views that feel bigger than the elevation. Most hikers go counterclockwise, climbing Welch first so the steepest slabs are uphill. The ledges are slick when wet, and the trailhead is a national forest fee site.

Mount Monadnock

Mount Monadnock (3,165 ft) in Jaffrey rises alone above southern New Hampshire, so its bare summit has a view far out of proportion to its height. It's famously busy. The White Dot Trail from the state park headquarters is the most direct route; the White Cross Trail is a slightly gentler return. Monadnock State Park does not allow pets, and on busy days parking may need to be reserved in advance through NH State Parks.

The 48 four-thousand-footers

New Hampshire has 48 peaks on the Appalachian Mountain Club's official 4,000-footer list. To count, a summit must reach 4,000 feet and rise at least 200 feet above the col connecting it to a higher neighbor. Hikers who climb all 48 can apply to the AMC Four Thousand Footer Club; there's a separate winter list for people who climb them all between the winter solstice and the spring equinox.

  • Good first 4,000-footers: Mount Tecumseh, Mount Pierce, Mount Osceola and Cannon Mountain are among the shorter ones, though "short" still means steep and rocky.
  • Peak-bagging tip: Franconia Ridge gives you two list peaks (Lincoln and Lafayette) in one loop; Little Haystack doesn't count because it doesn't rise 200 feet above its col.
  • Track your hikes and conditions on the AMC's website, outdoors.org.

AMC huts and camping

The Appalachian Mountain Club runs eight huts in the White Mountains, a day's hike apart along or near the Appalachian Trail: Lonesome Lake, Greenleaf, Galehead, Zealand Falls, Mizpah Spring, Lakes of the Clouds, Madison Spring and Carter Notch. In the main season most serve dinner and breakfast with bunkrooms; some stay open in a self-service mode off-season, where you bring your own food and sleeping bag. Reservations open months ahead and popular summer and fall dates go quickly; book through outdoors.org.

Car campers have national forest campgrounds along the Kancamagus Highway and elsewhere, plus state park campgrounds such as Lafayette Place in Franconia Notch. Backpackers can use designated tent sites and shelters, and camp elsewhere in the national forest following the Forest Protection Area rules, which restrict camping near trails, roads, water, huts and above treeline. Read the current rules at fs.usda.gov/whitemountain. For overnight gear, the packing list generator builds a checklist for your trip, and cold weather camping covers shoulder-season nights.

Parking passes and rules

  • White Mountain National Forest: Many trailheads are designated fee sites where you must display a recreation pass. Day, 7-day and annual passes are sold online, at ranger stations and at many local stores, and federal interagency passes (such as America the Beautiful) are accepted. Not every trailhead requires one. Current fees and the list of sites are on the forest's website.
  • State parks: Franconia Notch, Crawford Notch and Monadnock are New Hampshire state parks with their own rules and, at Monadnock, a day-use fee and possible parking reservations.
  • Hike Safe: New Hampshire can bill hikers for rescue costs when a rescue results from negligence. The state's voluntary Hike Safe card limits that liability and helps fund search and rescue.

During mud season, the Forest Service asks hikers to stay off high-elevation trails because saturated soil erodes quickly and walking around mud widens the trail. If you hike, walk through the mud, not around it. See the White Mountains destination guide for monthly temperatures and the October weather guide for foliage-season planning.

What to carry in the Whites

  • The hiker essentials: map and compass or offline map, headlamp, extra food and water, rain shell, insulating layer, first aid kit and a fire starter.
  • Sturdy footwear with good grip for wet granite; trekking poles for the descents.
  • Microspikes from October through May on most peaks; snowshoes in deep snow.
  • A warm hat and gloves on any hike above treeline, in any month.
  • Water treatment. Many streams are reliable, but treat everything.
