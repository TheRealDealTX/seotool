Cold weather camping is comfortable when you get four things right: enough insulation under you (pad R-values add up, so stack them), a sleeping bag chosen by its comfort rating rather than its lower limit, dry layers you adjust before you sweat or shiver, and steady food and water. Most cold nights are caused by the ground and by moisture, not by a bag that's too thin. Know the early signs of hypothermia and frostbite, and never bring a fuel-burning heater into the tent.

Start with the right first trip

For a first cold-weather trip, choose a campground you can drive to, with a forecast low around 25–35°F (−4 to 2°C), calm wind, and no precipitation. The car is your backup: extra blankets, a warm place to change, and a way out if the night goes badly. Many national parks keep at least one campground open in winter; check the park's site on nps.gov for winter operations, road closures and services.

Check the forecast for both temperature and wind. Wind steals heat far faster than still air, and 25°F with a 15 mph wind feels much colder than 25°F and calm.

Your sleep system

Sleeping bag ratings: comfort vs limit

Bags tested to the EN 13537 or ISO 23537 standard list two key numbers:

  • Comfort: the temperature at which a typical cold sleeper can sleep comfortably in a relaxed position.
  • Limit (lower limit): the temperature at which a typical warm sleeper can sleep curled up for about eight hours without waking cold.
  • Some bags also list an extreme or risk rating. That's a survival number, not a sleeping number.

Many bags are marketed by the limit number. A "20°F bag" may have a comfort rating closer to 30°F. If you sleep cold, buy by the comfort rating and choose one at or below the coldest low you expect. If a bag lists only one number with no standard, add a margin of 10°F or more.

Down is lighter and more compressible for the same warmth; synthetic insulation keeps more of its warmth when damp and costs less. For car camping, a heavier synthetic bag is fine. In either case, a liner adds a few degrees, and a puffy jacket draped over the top of the bag adds more.

Pad R-values stack

On frozen ground, the pad matters as much as the bag. The insulation beneath you is crushed flat by your body weight, so the pad does nearly all the work of stopping heat loss to the ground.

Pads are rated by R-value (higher is warmer). Since 2020 most major brands test to the ASTM F3340 standard, so numbers are comparable across brands. R-values add together: a closed-cell foam pad with R 2 under an inflatable pad with R 4 gives you about R 6.

Expected lowSuggested total pad R-valueExample
Above 40°F (4°C)2–3A summer inflatable or foam pad
25–40°F (−4 to 4°C)4–5A three-season insulated inflatable
10–25°F (−12 to −4°C)5–6Insulated inflatable plus a foam pad
Below 10°F (−12°C) or sleeping on snow6+Winter inflatable plus a foam pad

Treat these as starting points; cold sleepers should go higher. Put the foam pad on the bottom: it protects the inflatable from punctures and still works if the inflatable fails. An air mattress without insulation is the coldest thing you can sleep on.

Habits that make a big difference

  • Eat a fatty snack and drink something warm right before bed. Your body burns fuel to make heat all night.
  • Pee before bed, and again if you wake needing to. Holding it makes you colder. Many winter campers keep a clearly marked pee bottle in the tent.
  • Change into dry base layers and socks for sleeping. Don't sleep in the damp layers you hiked in.
  • Wear a warm hat and pull the hood drawcord snug around your face.
  • Breathe out of the bag, not into it. Breath moisture dampens insulation over a night.
  • Use a hot water bottle. Fill a leak-proof hard bottle with hot (not boiling) water, close it tight, put it in a sock, and keep it at your core or between your thighs.
  • Do a few sit-ups or jumping jacks before getting in, so you start warm. Don't work up a sweat.

Layering: stay warm without sweating

The goal is to never get soaked with sweat and never let yourself get chilled. Adjust layers early and often.

LayerWhatNotes
BaseMerino or synthetic long-sleeve top and long underwearNo cotton; it stays wet and cold
MidFleece jacket or light synthetic insulated jacketWear while moving if it's very cold
InsulationThick down or synthetic puffy (hood helps)Put it on the moment you stop moving
ShellWaterproof or windproof jacket and pantsBlocks wind and snow
ExtremitiesWarm hat, buff or balaclava, liner gloves plus insulated gloves or mittens, wool socks, insulated bootsBring a spare pair of gloves and socks

The rule of thumb: start cool. If you're warm standing at the trailhead, you'll be sweating in ten minutes. Shed a layer before you overheat, and add your puffy as soon as you stop.

Tip: Keep tomorrow's base layers, gloves and phone in the sleeping bag overnight. Cold boots can be stored in a stuff sack in the foot of the bag or vestibule, but put the liners inside the bag.

Food, water and stoves

Eat more

You burn more energy staying warm. Plan for more calories than you would in summer, with plenty of fat — nuts, cheese, butter, chocolate — and hot meals morning and night. The Camp Meal & Food Weight Planner can help you size food for cold-weather trips.

Stay hydrated

Cold, dry air pulls moisture out with every breath, and you don't feel as thirsty. Dehydration makes you more vulnerable to cold injuries. Drink on a schedule, and use warm drinks to make it easier.

  • Carry bottles upside down in an insulated sleeve so ice forms at the bottom, not in the lid.
  • At night, keep bottles in the tent or sleeping bag.
  • Hollow-fiber water filters can be destroyed by freezing. Keep yours in your sleeping bag at night, or switch to chemical treatment or boiling in sustained freezing temps. Our Sawyer Squeeze guide explains why.

Stoves in the cold

Canister stoves (isobutane-propane mixes) lose pressure as temperatures drop toward and below freezing. Keep the canister warm — in a jacket pocket before cooking or in the sleeping bag overnight — and use a canister stand. For sustained temperatures well below freezing, a liquid-fuel (white gas) stove is more reliable. Large propane car-camping stoves handle cold better than small backpacking canisters.

Safety: Never run a stove, grill, lantern or fuel-burning heater inside a tent or closed vestibule. Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless and can kill in an enclosed space; tents also catch fire quickly. Cook outside with the vestibule fully open, and keep a carbon monoxide detector in any vehicle or camper you sleep in with a heater.

Beating condensation and frost

Each person releases a surprising amount of moisture overnight through breath and skin. In cold air it condenses on the inside of the tent, then freezes into frost that rains down on you when the wind shakes the walls.

  • Ventilate. Keep the top vents open and crack a door, even when it's cold. A slightly cooler, drier tent beats a slightly warmer, wet one.
  • Pick the right spot. Low, damp hollows and lakeshores collect cold, humid air. Slightly higher ground with a light breeze has less condensation. Trees overhead help.
  • Keep wet gear out. Store snowy boots and wet shells in the vestibule.
  • Don't cook inside. Besides the CO risk, boiling water fills the tent with steam.
  • Wipe it down. Use a small towel or sponge to clear frost before it melts in the morning.
  • Dry your bag. On multi-day trips, drape the sleeping bag in the sun at lunch or open it in the car to let moisture out; down loses loft as it absorbs moisture night after night.

Setting up camp in the cold

  • Arrive with daylight to spare. Winter days are short; check sunset with the Sunrise & Sunset Calculator.
  • Pitch out of the wind, behind trees, a slope or a boulder, but not under dead branches loaded with snow.
  • On snow, stamp out a platform with boots or snowshoes and let it set for 20–30 minutes before pitching. Use snow stakes or bury stuff sacks filled with snow as deadman anchors.
  • Change into dry layers and put on your puffy as soon as you stop moving.
  • If fires are allowed, a campfire is a morale boost, but plan to stay warm without it. Our guide on starting a fire with wet wood helps when everything is damp.

Hypothermia and frostbite: know the signs

Hypothermia

Hypothermia happens when your body loses heat faster than it can produce it. It can occur above freezing, especially if you're wet, tired, or in wind. The CDC lists these warning signs:

  • Shivering
  • Exhaustion or feeling very tired
  • Confusion
  • Fumbling hands
  • Memory loss
  • Slurred speech
  • Drowsiness

Act early: get the person out of wind and wet, replace wet clothing with dry layers, put them in a sleeping bag on an insulated pad, and give warm, sweet drinks if they're fully alert and able to swallow. Warm the core (chest, neck, head, groin) first. If shivering stops while they're still cold, or they become confused, very drowsy or unresponsive, that's a medical emergency — call 911 or use satellite SOS.

Frostbite

Frostbite most often affects fingers, toes, nose, ears, cheeks and chin. Warning signs per the CDC include:

  • Redness or pain in an area, the earliest warning (frostnip)
  • Numbness
  • White or grayish-yellow skin
  • Skin that feels unusually firm or waxy

Get out of the cold and seek medical care. Don't rub or massage the area, and don't use a stove, fire or heating pad to rewarm it — numb skin burns easily. Rewarm only if there's no chance it will refreeze; thawing and refreezing causes more damage. Check that hypothermia isn't also present, since it's the more urgent problem.

Safety: Tell someone your plans and when you'll be back, carry a way to call for help where cell service is unreliable, and set a firm bail-out rule — for example, if anyone's gear gets soaked or someone can't get warm, you pack up and head to the car or home. Cold weather camping checklist

  • Bag with a comfort rating at or below your expected low, plus a liner
  • Two pads (closed-cell foam + insulated inflatable) totaling enough R-value
  • Dry sleep clothes, warm hat, and thick socks
  • Puffy jacket, shell, spare gloves and socks
  • Insulated water bottles and a leak-proof bottle for hot water
  • Stove suited to the cold, extra fuel, and a lighter kept warm
  • High-calorie food and hot drink mixes
  • Headlamp with lithium batteries (they handle cold better) kept warm
  • Small towel for frost, and a shovel if there's snow
