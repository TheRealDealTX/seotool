Grand Mesa fishing is mostly trout fishing on high, cold lakes: the mesa east of Grand Junction, Colorado, holds more than 300 lakes and reservoirs at around 10,000 feet, and many are stocked with rainbow, brook and cutthroat trout. The easiest starting points are the lakes along Highway 65, such as Island, Ward and the Mesa Lakes area, and the best fishing usually comes right after ice-out in late spring to early summer and again in September.

Why the Grand Mesa is different

The Grand Mesa is one of the largest flat-topped mountains in the world, and most of it lies within the Grand Mesa National Forest. Its top is a rolling plateau of spruce-fir forest, aspen and meadows dotted with lakes, many of them small reservoirs built to store water for farms and towns below. That has a few practical effects for anglers:

  • Short seasons. At about 10,000 feet, the lakes freeze for much of the year and ice-out comes late.
  • Changing water levels. Many reservoirs are drawn down through late summer for irrigation, so a lake that's full in June can have wide muddy margins by September.
  • Lots of choice. If one lake is crowded or slow, another is usually a short drive or walk away.

Island Lake

One of the largest lakes on the mesa, right along Highway 65. It has shore access and is popular with anglers in small boats, kayaks and float tubes. Expect company on summer weekends.

Ward, Alexander and Twin Lakes

A cluster of lakes in the same area with campgrounds and cabins nearby. Good for a base camp where you can fish a different lake each morning and evening.

Mesa Lakes

A group of small, connected lakes on the north edge of the mesa, close to the highway on the climb up from the Grand Junction side. Easy shore fishing with short walks between lakes.

Backcountry lakes

Some of the mesa's smaller lakes are only reachable by rough road, ATV route or trail. Pressure drops quickly once you're a mile from the pavement. A Forest Service map or the free Motor Vehicle Use Map tells you which roads are open to which vehicles.

Tip: Stop at the Grand Mesa Visitor Center on Highway 65 for current road conditions, maps and local advice on which lakes are fishing well. Lakes that were stocked recently can be very different from the same lake a month later.

What you can catch

  • Rainbow trout: the most widely stocked fish on the mesa and the most common catch in roadside lakes.
  • Brook trout: common in smaller, colder lakes and streams; often abundant and small.
  • Cutthroat trout: present in a number of lakes and prized by anglers.
  • Splake: a brook trout and lake trout hybrid stocked in some lakes, capable of growing larger than the other trout here.

Stocking varies year to year. Colorado Parks and Wildlife publishes fishing information and stocking reports on cpw.state.co.us.

Licenses and regulations

Fishing licenses, bag and possession limits, and lake-specific rules are set by Colorado Parks and Wildlife and change over time. Several Grand Mesa waters carry special regulations, such as artificial flies and lures only or different size and bag limits. Before you fish, check the current Colorado fishing regulations and buy your license through Colorado Parks and Wildlife, and read any signs posted at the lake.

When to fish: season by season

Ice-out (late spring to early summer)

Ice typically leaves the mesa's lakes in late spring or early summer, later in big-snow years and on higher, shaded lakes. The weeks right after ice-out are often the best of the year: trout cruise the shallows and feed actively in cold water. Roads and trails may still be muddy or snowbound, so check conditions before you drive up.

Summer

Fishing is good but more tied to time of day. Fish the first and last couple of hours of daylight near the surface; in the middle of the day, go deeper or look for inlets and shaded shorelines. Afternoon thunderstorms are common, which matters if you're on the water. The sunrise and sunset calculator helps you plan for the early and late windows.

Fall

September brings cooler water, active fish, fewer people and golden aspen. Reservoir levels may be low. Nights are cold, and early snow is possible by late September or October.

Winter

Several lakes near Highway 65 are popular for ice fishing. Ice thickness varies across a lake and through the season; never assume it's safe because others are on it. Colorado Parks and Wildlife has ice safety guidance on its website. If you camp in winter, our cold weather camping guide covers staying warm at high elevation.

Tactics that work

Spin fishing

Small inline spinners, spoons and small jigs catch trout across the mesa. A 6- to 7-foot light or ultralight spinning rod with 4- to 6-pound line covers most situations. A ready-made combo like the Ugly Stik GX2 spinning combo and a Panther Martin trout spinner kit is enough to get started. Vary your retrieve speed and depth until fish respond, and check whether the lake you're on allows bait before using it.

Fly fishing

In summer, watch for rising fish in the morning and evening and match the size of whatever is hatching. When nothing is rising, small nymphs under an indicator or slowly stripped leeches and woolly buggers work along drop-offs. A float tube lets you reach weed edges and deeper water that shore anglers can't.

Reading a lake

  • Inlets bring cold, oxygenated water and food: start there.
  • Drop-offs where shallow flats meet deeper water hold cruising fish.
  • Wind-blown shorelines collect insects; fish the side the wind is blowing toward.

Keep tackle organized in a small box like the Plano StowAway tackle box so you can move between lakes quickly.

Camping and staying on the mesa

  • Forest Service campgrounds sit near several of the main lakes along Highway 65. Many can be reserved on recreation.gov; others are first-come, first-served.
  • Dispersed camping is allowed in parts of the national forest away from developed sites. Check the local ranger district for where it's permitted and any fire restrictions.
  • Cabins and lodges on the mesa and in nearby towns like Cedaredge and Mesa offer a roof over your head.

Staying safe at 10,000 feet

Safety: Get off the water and away from open shorelines when thunderstorms approach; lightning is a real risk on exposed lakes. Wear a life jacket in boats and float tubes, since water at this elevation stays cold enough to cause cold shock even in summer.
  • Altitude: if you've come from low elevation, take it easy the first day and drink plenty of water.
  • Sun: UV is strong at 10,000 feet and reflects off water. Wear a hat, sunglasses and sunscreen.
  • Cold nights: even in July, nights can be near freezing. Pack warm layers and a sleeping bag rated well below the forecast low.
  • Bugs: mosquitoes can be thick in early summer near still water.
