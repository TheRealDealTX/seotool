Yes, you can hike El Capitan. You can't walk up the famous 3,000-foot granite face (that is a technical big-wall climb that takes most climbing parties several days), but you can reach the summit on trails that approach from the back side of the formation. The two main routes start in Yosemite Valley via the Upper Yosemite Fall Trail or at Tamarack Flat off Tioga Road, and both are long, strenuous days of roughly 15 or more miles round trip.

The face vs. the trail

El Capitan's southwest and southeast faces are among the most famous rock climbs in the world. Routes like The Nose are climbed with ropes, protection and often several nights spent on portaledges; Alex Honnold's 2017 rope-free ascent of Freerider is famous precisely because it was so extraordinary. None of that is a hike.

The back (north) side of El Capitan is different. It slopes gently into the forested uplands above the Valley, and trails lead right onto its rounded granite summit. From the top you look straight across the Valley at Cathedral Rocks and up toward Half Dome, and you can walk out onto the summit slabs where climbers finish their routes.

Safety: The summit slopes away gradually and then drops off thousands of feet. There is no railing and no clear edge until it's too late. Stay well back from where the granite starts to steepen, and keep a close hand on children.

From Yosemite Valley via the Upper Yosemite Fall Trail

This is the classic and hardest version. The Upper Yosemite Fall Trail starts near Camp 4 and switchbacks up the north wall of the Valley. The NPS lists the climb to the top of Upper Yosemite Fall as about 7.2 miles round trip with about 2,700 feet of elevation gain, and that is only the first part. From the top of the fall you continue west on the trail toward Eagle Peak, then on to El Capitan through forest and over granite. Expect a total of well over 3,500 feet of climbing.

The payoff is that you pass the top of Yosemite Falls along the way. The cost is that the first few miles are steep, exposed to afternoon sun, and busy. On the descent, those same switchbacks are hard on knees; trekking poles help.

From Tamarack Flat

Tamarack Flat Campground sits off Tioga Road, a few miles east of Crane Flat. The trail follows the route of the old Big Oak Flat Road down to Cascade Creek, then climbs through forest to the top of El Capitan. Because you start around 6,300 feet, there's less total elevation gain than from the Valley, but note that the climb comes on the return as well as the way out.

This route only works when Tioga Road and the side road to Tamarack Flat are open, which usually means summer and early fall. Tioga Road closes for winter and its opening date varies widely with snowpack; check current road conditions.

Overnight options

Many people split the Valley route into two days, camping in the forest above the rim. Any overnight stay in the Yosemite Wilderness needs a wilderness permit, reserved through recreation.gov, and you must carry food in a bear-resistant container. Camping on the summit slabs themselves is not a good idea: it's exposed to wind and lightning, and you'd be in the way of climbers topping out.

What to expect on the hike

  • A long day. At an average hiking pace, either route takes most people 8 to 10 hours or more. Run your own numbers in the hiking time calculator.
  • Altitude. The summit is around 7,570 feet. That's not extreme, but visitors coming from sea level will feel it on the climb.
  • Heat and sun. The Upper Yosemite Fall switchbacks face south and can be very hot by midday in summer.
  • Water. Yosemite Creek and Cascade Creek can be reliable early in the season and very low by late summer. Treat any water you collect; a Sawyer Squeeze water filter is light enough to carry on a day hike, and our Sawyer Squeeze guide covers how to use it.
  • Navigation. The trails are signed at junctions, but the upland forest looks similar for miles. Carry a map or offline map app.

When to go

Late spring through fall is the normal season. In early season, the upper Valley route can hold snow and ice and creeks run high; by late summer the trails are dry but water is scarcer. Afternoon thunderstorms are possible in summer, and being on an open granite summit during lightning is dangerous. Check the monthly weather guides and the trail weather forecast before you go, and start early so you're off the summit by early afternoon.

What to bring

  • A comfortable daypack with room for 3 or more liters of water, food and layers. The Osprey Talon 22 daypack is a good size for this hike.
  • Water and treatment. Use the hiking water calculator to estimate how much to carry between sources.
  • Trekking poles for the steep descent, such as the Black Diamond Trail Sport trekking poles.
  • A headlamp, even if you plan to be back by dark. Long days run late. The Black Diamond Spot 400 headlamp is bright and light.
  • Sun protection: hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, and a sun hoodie for the exposed switchbacks.
  • Layers: a wind or rain shell and a warm layer for the summit.
  • Navigation and first aid, plus a fully charged phone. Cell service is unreliable above the rim.

Safety tips

  • Set a turnaround time and stick to it, especially on the Valley route, where you still have a long, steep descent after the summit.
  • Stay back from the edge of the summit slabs and off any wet granite.
  • Store food properly and never feed wildlife; Yosemite's black bears are skilled at getting into food.
  • Tell someone your route and return time, and know what to do if you get lost in the woods.
  • Don't try to descend by any route other than the trail. Climbers leave the summit via a descent that involves steep slabs and rappels; it is not a hiking route.
