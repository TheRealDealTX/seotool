To start a fire with wet wood, split the wood to get at the dry interior, turn that dry wood into fine shavings and feather sticks, build the fire on a platform off the wet ground, and add fuel slowly from match-thin to wrist-thick. Rain soaks into the outer layer of a log far more than its core, so the dry fuel you need is usually inside the wet wood you have. Before any of that, confirm fires are allowed: a rainy day doesn't lift a burn ban.

Safety: Check current fire restrictions for your campground or forest before you strike a match — on the managing agency's website and at the campground entrance. Use an existing fire ring where one exists, keep water and a shovel next to it, and never use gasoline, lantern fuel or other accelerants to "help" a damp fire. They can flash and cause serious burns.

Why wet wood won't catch

A fire needs heat, fuel and oxygen. Water steals heat: before wood can burn, the moisture in and on it has to boil off, and that takes energy away from your small flame. That's why a match that would light a pile of dry twigs simply goes out on damp ones. The fix is to give your flame very little water to fight at the start — the driest, thinnest fuel you can make — and to build heat gradually until the fire can dry larger wood itself.

Two kinds of "wet" behave very differently:

  • Surface-wet wood (rained on overnight, damp from fog) is dry a few millimeters in. This is very workable.
  • Waterlogged or rotten wood (soft, crumbly, lying in puddles, spongy when you squeeze it) is wet throughout. Skip it.

What to bring when rain is likely

The most reliable wet-weather fire starts at home. Pack these in a zip-top bag or small dry bag:

  • Two lighters. A butane lighter works when matches won't, but it struggles when very cold; keep one in a pocket.
  • Long-burning fire starters: wax-based tinder cubes, fatwood sticks, or homemade cotton balls smeared with petroleum jelly. You want something that burns for a minute or more, not a few seconds like paper.
  • A knife and a small saw. A fixed-blade or sturdy folding knife for shaving feather sticks; a folding saw for cutting dead branches into lengths you can split.
  • Dry tinder from home: dryer lint, shredded cardboard, or a few sheets of newspaper in the bag.
  • Purchased firewood stored under your car or a tarp, rather than relying on what you find.

Buy firewood near where you'll burn it. Moving firewood spreads invasive insects and diseases, and many parks and states restrict out-of-area wood.

Where to find drier fuel

Where gathering is allowed (rules vary — many national parks permit only dead and down wood, some ban gathering entirely, and Leave No Trace practice is to never cut live trees or break branches off standing trees), look for:

  • Dead twigs still attached low on conifer trunks, sheltered by the canopy above. They snap cleanly with a crack when dry enough to use. If a twig bends instead of snapping, it's too wet or still alive.
  • Downed wood held off the ground on rocks or other logs, where air can move around it.
  • The underside of a downed log or wood under thick tree cover, which often stays drier.
  • Pitchy wood from old pine stumps (fatwood), which is saturated with resin and lights even when damp — use it only where collecting is allowed.

Collect more of the small stuff than you think you need. Most failed fires run out of pencil-thick sticks before the bigger wood has caught.

Split and prepare the wood

Split to the dry core

Split firewood into quarters, then split one or two of those quarters again into thin pieces. The fresh faces you expose should look pale and feel dry. Baton a knife through small pieces (tap the spine with another piece of wood) or use a hatchet on larger rounds.

Carve feather sticks

Take a dry, split stick about thumb-thick and shave long, thin curls down its length, stopping before each curl breaks off so they bunch at the bottom. Make three or four. The thin curls catch a flame quickly and burn long enough to light the stick they are attached to. Also shave a pile of loose curls as extra tinder.

Tip: Keep prepared tinder and kindling inside your jacket or under a tarp until the moment you light. Ten minutes of drizzle can undo all your prep.

Build and light it, step by step

  1. Clear the fire ring of standing water and soggy ash as best you can.
  2. Build a platform. Lay a row of wrist-thick sticks side by side across the bottom of the ring. This keeps your tinder off wet ground and lets air flow underneath.
  3. Set your fire starter and tinder in the middle of the platform, with feather sticks leaning over it.
  4. Build a small teepee or lean-to of the thinnest kindling over the tinder, leaving a gap on the upwind side so you can reach in and air can enter.
  5. Light the fire starter from the upwind side, shielding the flame with your body.
  6. Feed slowly. As flames climb into the kindling, add more of the thinnest pieces, then the next size up. Add a new size only when the current one is burning well on its own. Rushing to big wood is the most common reason a fire goes out.
  7. Give it air. If it smokes heavily and dims, gently blow at the base of the flames or open up the structure. Wet fires often need more oxygen, not more wood.
  8. Dry the next pieces. Stack the next round of fuel near the fire — close enough to steam, never on top of the flames until you're ready to add it.

Once you have a bed of glowing coals, the fire can dry and burn larger, damper pieces on its own. Expect more smoke than usual; it's mostly steam.

If it's still raining

  • Pitch a tarp high above the fire area — at least 6 to 8 ft (2 to 2.5 m) above the flames and angled so smoke can escape — or skip the fire. Tarp fabric melts and burns, so keep it well clear of heat and sparks.
  • Never build a fire under a tent vestibule or inside any shelter.
  • In steady rain, a stove is the smarter choice for hot food and drinks. A fire is a comfort, not a requirement.

Leave No Trace and fire rules

The Leave No Trace principle on campfires, from lnt.org, boils down to a few habits:

  • Use existing fire rings, fire pans or mound fires; don't build new rings.
  • Keep fires small.
  • Burn only dead and down wood no thicker than an adult wrist, and only where collecting is allowed.
  • Burn wood all the way to ash, put it out completely, and pack out any trash — foil and food scraps don't burn cleanly.
  • Consider a stove instead of a fire, especially in high-use or fragile areas and above treeline.

Fire restrictions are separate from LNT and are legally binding. They are set by the land manager (a national park, national forest, BLM office, state, or county) and can change daily during dry spells. Remember that a wet campsite doesn't mean wet fuels a mile away — burn bans often stay in place through a short rain.

Put it out properly

A fire built from wet wood can leave dense, half-burned logs that hold heat for hours. When you're done, drown it, stir it, and feel it until everything is cold to the touch. Our guide to putting out a campfire walks through each step.
