If you get lost in the woods, stop moving and use STOP — Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. In most cases the safest plan is to stay where you are, call 911 or trigger a satellite SOS if you can, make yourself easy to see and hear (three whistle blasts is the distress signal), and put your energy into staying warm and dry until searchers arrive. Walking around trying to find the trail is how a short detour turns into a multi-day search.

The STOP method

STOP is taught by search-and-rescue groups and land agencies because panic is the real danger in the first few minutes. Your heart rate spikes, you start moving fast, and you stop noticing where you've been.

S — Stop

As soon as you realize you don't know where you are, stop walking. Sit down. Drink some water and eat something. You are almost certainly not as far from the trail as it feels, and each extra step in the wrong direction widens the area searchers have to cover.

T — Think

Ask yourself: When did I last know exactly where I was? How long ago was that, and which way was I heading? Did I cross a stream, climb, or turn at a fork? Who knows my plan, and when will they expect me back?

O — Observe

Take stock of your situation and your surroundings:

  • Time and light: how much daylight is left? (Check sunset before you hike with the Sunrise, Sunset & Daylight Calculator.)
  • Weather: is rain, wind, or a temperature drop coming?
  • Your body: injuries, how much water and food, warm layers?
  • Your phone: any signal? Battery percentage? Does your map app show your GPS position even without service? Offline maps often do.
  • Landmarks: can you hear a road, see a ridgeline, find footprints or trail markers nearby?

P — Plan

Decide on one clear course of action and commit to it. For most people, most of the time, that plan is: call for help, stay put, signal, and stay warm.

Why staying put is usually right

Search teams start at your last known point — your car, your trip plan, your last text — and work outward. A person who keeps moving can drift out of the search area, burn energy, and get injured on rough ground. Stay where you are if:

  • Someone knows your route and expected return time
  • You are injured, exhausted, or with a child
  • It's dark or will be within an hour
  • The weather is bad or getting worse
  • You've called 911 or sent an SOS and been told to stay

Moving can make sense only if you are certain of a destination: you can see or hear a road you recognize, you can retrace your steps a short distance to a known point, or your GPS position on a map clearly shows a nearby trail. If you do move, mark your direction (scratch an arrow, stack rocks, tie a bandana) so searchers can follow.

Many SAR groups teach children a simple version: "hug a tree" — pick a tree, stay with it, and answer when people call your name.

When and how to call for help

Don't wait until it's dark or you're in trouble. If you're lost and can't confidently reach a known point, call 911. Rescuers would much rather walk you out in daylight than search at night.

Phone calls and texts

  • In the US, a phone will try to connect a 911 call on any carrier's network in range, so try even if you have no bars on your own carrier.
  • Many areas accept text-to-911. A text can sometimes get through when a call won't. If text-to-911 isn't available, you'll get a bounce-back message.
  • Give your location as precisely as you can. Your phone's compass or map app shows latitude and longitude; read them out.
  • Try from higher ground or an open area, but don't wander far to do it.

Satellite SOS

With no network at all, you still have options:

  • Recent iPhones and some Android phones offer emergency SOS via satellite. You need a clear view of the sky; the phone guides you to point it.
  • A satellite messenger such as the Garmin inReach Mini 2 sends an SOS with your GPS coordinates to a monitoring center and lets you text back and forth with rescuers (requires a subscription).
  • A personal locator beacon (PLB) sends a one-way distress signal with your location through the international search-and-rescue satellite system, no subscription needed.

Save your battery

Once you've reached help, turn on low-power mode, dim the screen, close apps, and switch to airplane mode between check-ins unless rescuers ask you to keep the phone on. Keep the phone warm in an inside pocket; cold drains batteries fast.

Signal so searchers can find you

Anything in threes is a recognized distress signal.

  • Whistle: three sharp blasts, then a pause, repeated. A whistle carries much farther than a voice and costs no energy. A pealess whistle like the Fox 40 Classic safety whistle works when wet. Clip it to your pack strap.
  • Light: three flashes of a headlamp or phone light at night.
  • Color: spread bright clothing, a rain jacket, or an emergency blanket in an opening where it can be seen from above.
  • Ground signals: lay out a large X or V with rocks, branches or logs in a clearing — big enough to see from the air, several yards across.
  • Signal mirror: on sunny days, a mirror flash can be seen from aircraft at long distances.
  • Answer calls. When you hear searchers, shout back and blow your whistle. People who are lost sometimes hide from searchers out of embarrassment or fear; don't.

Only build a signal fire where it's legal and safe, and never in dry, windy conditions — a wildfire makes everything worse.

Stay warm, dry and hydrated

Most lost-person emergencies become dangerous because of exposure, not hunger. People can go days without food; hypothermia can set in within hours in wet, windy weather even at 50°F (10°C).

Shelter and warmth

  • Put on every layer before you get cold. Stay out of the wind.
  • Get off the ground: sit on your pack, a foam pad, or a thick pile of dry leaves or boughs. The ground pulls heat out quickly.
  • Use natural shelter — the downhill side of a big tree, a rock overhang, a dense thicket — rather than spending hours building something elaborate.
  • An emergency bivy like the SOL emergency bivvy weighs a few ounces and traps body heat. It's worth carrying on every day hike.
  • Watch for the "umbles": stumbling, mumbling, fumbling and grumbling are early signs of hypothermia. If you see them, focus everything on getting warm and dry.

Water

Ration sweat, not water: drink what you have when you're thirsty and move as little as possible during the heat of the day. If you run out, drinking untreated water from a stream is a risk of illness days later, but dehydration is a more immediate danger in a survival situation. Treat it if you have any means to.

Food

Food helps you stay warm, but don't eat unknown plants, berries or mushrooms. Eat what you carried in small amounts.

Mistakes that make it worse

  • Pushing on to "find the trail" without knowing where it is.
  • Following a stream downhill by default. Drainages often lead into cliffs, waterfalls, and dense brush. It's sometimes useful if a map shows the creek reaches a road, but it isn't a universal rule.
  • Climbing high without a reason. It can help you get a phone signal or see landmarks, but it costs energy and exposes you to wind.
  • Splitting up a group. Stay together.
  • Traveling at night. Twisted ankles and falls happen far more often in the dark.
  • Waiting too long to call. Calling early is not an overreaction.

How to avoid getting lost

Before you go

  • Leave a trip plan with someone reliable: trailhead, route, who's with you, what you're wearing, your car, and a firm "call for help if you haven't heard from me by" time.
  • Download offline maps for the area on your phone and carry a paper map. Charge your phone and bring a small battery bank.
  • Know your time. Estimate how long the hike will take with the Hiking Time Calculator and set a turnaround time. Check sunset and the trail weather forecast.
  • Carry the basics even on short hikes: headlamp, warm layer, rain shell, extra food and water, whistle, emergency bivy, and a small first-aid kit.

On the trail

  • Check your map at every junction, and look back occasionally so you recognize the route on the return.
  • Notice handrails — streams, ridges, roads — and "catching features" that tell you you've gone too far.
  • Stop as soon as the trail feels faint. If you haven't seen a blaze, cairn or clear tread in a few minutes, go back to the last one you saw.
  • Be careful off trail and on scrambles, where routes are harder to follow. Our class 3 scramble guide covers route-finding on rocky terrain.
  • Learn basic map and compass. A baseplate compass like the Suunto A-10 compass is light and never runs out of battery, but only helps if you've practiced with it before you need it.
