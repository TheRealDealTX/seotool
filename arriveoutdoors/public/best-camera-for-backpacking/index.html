Best Camera for Backpacking: Choosing One and Carrying It on the Trail

Whether a phone, a pocket compact, a rugged waterproof camera or a mirrorless body is right depends on what you shoot and how much weight you will tolerate. This guide covers the trade-offs, the models worth shortlisting, and how to carry a camera so you actually use it.

The best camera for backpacking is the one light and tough enough that you keep it out instead of buried in your pack. For most hikers, that means a compact mirrorless body with one small zoom lens, a premium pocket compact, or simply a recent phone. Then carry it on a shoulder-strap clip or a sling, so a shot takes three seconds instead of a pack-off stop.

Do you need a camera at all?

Start here, because the lightest camera is the phone you already carry. Modern phones produce excellent landscape and people photos in good light, handle high-contrast scenes with automatic HDR, and do surprisingly well at night. A dedicated camera is worth carrying when:

  • You want wildlife or distant peaks. Optical zoom beats phone cropping by a wide margin past roughly 3x to 5x.
  • You want large prints or heavy editing. Bigger sensors hold more detail in shadows and highlights.
  • You want controls you can use with gloves on and a viewfinder that works in bright sun.
  • You need your phone's battery for navigation. A separate camera keeps your map device charged.

If none of those apply, spend the money on a good pack and a power bank instead.

Camera types compared

Premium pocket compacts

The ultralight choice: a sensor noticeably bigger than a phone's in a body that fits a hip-belt pocket. The Sony RX100 VII pairs a 1-inch sensor with a long built-in zoom (24–200mm equivalent), which makes it one of the few pocket cameras useful for wildlife. The Ricoh GR III goes the other way, with a larger APS-C sensor and a fixed 28mm-equivalent wide lens and no zoom, favored by landscape and street shooters who want the smallest possible package. Older RX100 generations are cheaper and still capable; check which zoom range each version has, because it changed between generations.

Trade-offs: small batteries, limited weather resistance, and tiny controls with gloves on.

Rugged waterproof compacts

If your trips involve river crossings, rain, paddling or kids, a rugged compact like the OM System Tough TG-7 shrugs off drops, rain and submersion without a case. It also has a well-regarded close-focus mode for wildflowers and insects. Image quality trails the larger-sensor options, especially in low light. Check the current depth, drop and freeze ratings before trusting it in a river.

Action cameras

The GoPro HERO13 Black is built for video: waterproof, very wide, with strong stabilization, and mountable on a pack strap, chest or pole. Tiny wearables like the Insta360 Go Ultra are even lighter for point-of-view clips. Neither replaces a stills camera for landscapes or wildlife, and both drain batteries quickly when recording, so plan charging around them.

Compact mirrorless (APS-C)

The sweet spot for most photographers who want real image quality. Bodies like the Sony a6700, the Sony a6400 with the 16-50mm kit lens, the Nikon Z50 II and the Fujifilm X-T5 accept interchangeable lenses, focus quickly and handle dawn light far better than a phone. The older Sony a6000 remains a capable budget body on the used market. Weather sealing, stabilization and battery life differ a lot between these models, so compare them directly on the manufacturer pages.

The weight discipline that matters most: one lens. A small standard zoom covers 90% of trail shots. Add a light telephoto only if wildlife is the point of the trip.

Full-frame mirrorless and DSLRs

Full-frame bodies like the Nikon Zf deliver the best low-light and night-sky results, at a cost in weight and lens size. Among DSLRs, the Pentax K-70 has long been noted for weather sealing and cold tolerance, but any DSLR plus lenses is heavy by backpacking standards. Choose these if photography is the main purpose of the trip, not an add-on.

How to carry a camera while hiking

How you carry the camera decides how often you use it. A camera in the pack body gets used at the summit and nowhere else. Here are the main systems, from most to least popular among backpackers.

Shoulder-strap clip

A clip like the Peak Design Capture bolts to your backpack's shoulder strap. A plate on the camera's tripod socket slides into it and locks. The camera rides high on your chest, does not swing, and releases with a button press.

  • Best for: mirrorless bodies and compacts on day hikes and backpacking trips.
  • Check: that the clip fits your strap's width and thickness. Very thin or heavily contoured straps may not hold it well.
  • Watch for: a heavy lens levering the camera forward, and the camera pressing on your sternum strap. Shift the clip up or down an inch to fix both.
  • Tip: mount it on the side opposite your shooting hand's pole, or the side that faces the slope less often on narrow trails.

Sling and shoulder straps

A quick-adjust sling like the Peak Design Slide Lite worn crossbody keeps the camera at your hip and lets you cinch it tight while walking, then loosen it to shoot. It works with or without a pack. Never hike with a camera hanging from your neck: it bounces, strains your neck and swings into rock when you lean forward.

Many hikers combine the two: clip for walking, sling attached as a backup tether so a fumbled release does not end in a drop.

Chest harness

Harness systems (Cotton Carrier is a well-known brand) hold the camera flat against your chest with straps across both shoulders. They carry heavy bodies and telephoto lenses more comfortably than a single strap clip, and some work without a backpack. The downsides are added heat, bulk, and possible conflict with your pack's sternum strap. They make the most sense for wildlife photographers carrying big lenses.

Hip-belt or top-loader case

A padded holster on your hip belt or worn chest-high protects the camera from rain and impacts while staying reachable. It is slower than a clip but better in scrambling terrain, sand and heavy rain. Pocket compacts fit in many hip-belt pockets without a separate case.

Wrist straps and tethers

For action cameras and compacts used near water, cliffs or from a boat, a wrist strap or leash is cheap insurance. Clip a leash from the camera to a pack strap whenever you shoot over drops.

Safety: Stow the camera before scrambling or crossing exposed ledges. A camera on your chest shifts your balance, blocks your view of your feet, and can catch on rock as you move. Put it in the pack or a closed case on class 3 and harder terrain, and never step backward to frame a shot near an edge.

Weather and dust protection

  • Rain: even weather-sealed cameras benefit from a fitted rain cover or a simple shower-cap-style cover over the clip-mounted camera. In steady rain, move it into a case or dry bag.
  • Dry bag or zip bag inside the pack: keep the camera, spare batteries and cards in a small dry bag in the pack overnight and during river crossings.
  • Condensation: moving a cold camera into a warm tent fogs it inside and out. Seal it in a zip bag before bringing it in, and let it warm up slowly before opening the bag.
  • Dust and sand: change lenses with your back to the wind, inside the pack lid if possible, or skip lens changes entirely in desert wind.
  • Silica gel: a packet or two in the camera bag helps on multi-day wet trips.

Batteries, cards and cold

  • Cold drains lithium-ion batteries. In cold weather, keep spares in an inside jacket pocket and sleep with them in your sleeping bag. A battery that reads empty when cold often recovers some charge once warmed. See cold-weather camping for keeping electronics alive overnight.
  • Power bank sizing: if your camera charges over USB, a power bank shared with your phone usually beats carrying many spare batteries. Check whether your camera can charge in-body or needs an external charger.
  • Cards: carry a spare memory card in a separate place from the camera, so a lost camera does not mean lost photos.
  • Cleaning: a microfiber cloth in a zip bag and a small blower handle most trail cleaning. Never wipe a gritty lens dry; blow off the grit first.

Tripods and accessories

Most backpackers can skip a full tripod. For self-portraits, night skies and waterfall long exposures, a flexible mini tripod like the JOBY GorillaPod 3K kit wraps around branches and pack frames, or you can look at lightweight travel tripods if you shoot stars often. Some trekking poles accept a camera mount, which doubles as a monopod.

Wet, misty places like Olympic reward a weatherproof setup; big-view parks like Glacier and Yosemite reward getting to viewpoints at first and last light. For making the people in your trail photos look good, see how to pose for hiking pictures.

Frequently asked questions

Is a phone good enough for backpacking photos?

For most hikers, yes. Recent phones handle landscapes, people and low light well. A dedicated camera earns its weight mainly for wildlife and distant subjects (optical zoom), big prints, or more control over exposure.

What type of camera is best for backpacking?

A compact mirrorless body with one small zoom lens is the best balance of image quality and weight for most photographers. A premium pocket compact suits ultralight hikers, and a rugged waterproof compact suits wet trips and paddling.

How do I carry a camera while hiking?

The most popular method is a clip that mounts the camera on your backpack shoulder strap, which keeps it accessible and stops it swinging. Sling straps, chest harnesses and hip-belt cases are the main alternatives.

How many batteries should I bring backpacking?

It depends on the camera and how much you shoot, but many photographers carry one spare per two to three days, plus a power bank if the camera charges over USB. Cold drains batteries faster, so keep spares warm.

Should I bring a tripod backpacking?

Only if you shoot night skies, long exposures or self-portraits. A small flexible tripod or a lightweight travel tripod covers most needs; many hikers skip it and brace the camera on rocks or a pack.
