Class 4 Scramble: Where Scrambling Becomes Climbing

Class 4 scrambling is simple climbing with serious exposure, where a fall is likely to be fatal. Here is how to know if you're ready, what skills you need, and how classic routes compare.

A class 4 scramble is simple climbing: steep rock where you use your hands on every move, the holds are smaller and less obvious than on class 3, and a fall in exposed sections would probably not stop until the bottom. Many parties climb class 4 unroped, but it is the point on the Yosemite Decimal System where a rope, and the skills to use it, become a reasonable choice rather than an overreaction.

What makes terrain class 4

The YDS class scale runs from class 1 (trail walking) to class 5 (technical climbing). If you're new to the scale, the class 3 scramble guide has the full class 1–5 table. Class 4 typically means:

  • You face the rock. On class 3 you can often scramble up looking ahead; on class 4 you turn in and climb like a ladder, moving hands and feet deliberately.
  • Exposure is serious. Steep faces, narrow ridges and traverses above large drops. The defining feature is consequence: a slip would likely be fatal.
  • Holds are smaller and need reading. You choose where to place a foot rather than just stepping up.
  • Route-finding is harder. Class 4 routes often have multiple lines, and getting off route usually means getting onto harder, looser rock.

The boundary between class 4 and 5.0 is subjective. Short "class 4" steps on one peak can be harder than "easy fifth class" elsewhere, and guidebooks don't always agree. Read recent trip reports and take the hardest description seriously.

Are you ready for class 4?

Before your first class 4 route, you should be able to say yes to all of these:

  • You have done several class 3 routes, including long, exposed ones, and downclimbed them comfortably.
  • You stay calm and keep moving efficiently when there is a big drop under your feet.
  • You can navigate off-trail and recognize when you have drifted off route.
  • You have climbed indoors or outdoors on a rope and understand basic movement on steep rock.
  • Someone in your party can build an anchor, belay and rappel, or you are going with a guide.

If any of those is a no, the gap is better closed with a guided climb, a mountaineering club course or roped practice at a crag than by learning on a 14er.

Skills you rely on

Precise footwork

Place your foot where you mean to, weight it smoothly, and keep your heel low on small edges. Shoes with sticky rubber and a stiff edge help a lot. Most people overgrip with their hands when nervous, which tires forearms quickly; trusting the feet is the cure.

Three points of contact, always

Move one limb at a time. On class 4, a single slipping foot with only two points on the rock is how accidents begin.

Testing holds

Colorado class 4 is notorious for loose rock. Knock on blocks, look for cracks behind flakes, and pull down rather than out. Never trust a hold just because it is polished from traffic.

Downclimbing

Descending class 4 is harder than ascending: you can't see your footholds as well and fatigue sets in. Downclimb facing in on anything steep, and if you are unsure you can reverse a move, don't make it on the way up.

Rope basics

Many class 4 parties carry a light rope (often a 30 to 60 meter single or a thin rope for rappelling), a few slings, carabiners and a harness. It is only useful if you can tie in, belay, build an anchor from slings or existing fixed gear, and rappel with a backup. Learn this from qualified instruction, not from a video at the trailhead.

Classic class 4 routes

Colorado's 14ers include the best-known class 4 standard routes in the country. Each of these is rated class 4 on its standard line and has seen fatal accidents.

  • Capitol Peak, Northeast Ridge: crosses the Knife Edge, a short but very exposed ridge. The rock beyond it is loose. Widely considered one of the hardest standard 14er routes.
  • Little Bear Peak, Hourglass: a steep gully where rockfall from parties above is the main danger. Helmets and timing matter more here than almost anywhere.
  • North Maroon Peak and Pyramid Peak: long routes on the Maroon Bells' layered, loose sedimentary rock, with constant route-finding.
  • Sunlight Peak: a mostly class 3 route ending at an exposed class 4 summit block that many people choose not to stand on.

Outside Colorado, class 4 sections appear on many Sierra Nevada and Teton routes. In Grand Teton National Park, the Grand Teton's standard routes go beyond class 4 into fifth class and are usually climbed roped or guided.

Gear for class 4

  • Helmet: essential. Rockfall is constant on popular class 4 routes. The Black Diamond Half Dome helmet and the Petzl Boreo helmet are durable hardshell-style options.
  • Approach shoes: sticky rubber and a stiff edge, such as the La Sportiva TX4 approach shoes.
  • Gloves: thin leather-palmed gloves like the Black Diamond Crag gloves protect hands and help on handlines or rappels.
  • Rope kit (if you know how to use it): harness, belay/rappel device, locking carabiners, slings and a rope suited to the route's longest rappel.
  • The rest: navigation, headlamp, layers, first aid, food and water, and a satellite messenger. Most class 4 routes are remote.

Safety principles

Safety: On class 4, the consequences of a single mistake are usually fatal. Turn back if the rock is wet, icy or snow-covered, if storms are building, or if anyone in the party is moving slowly or feeling unsure. The mountain will be there next season.
  • Start early. Summer thunderstorms in the Rockies typically build in the afternoon. Aim to be off the exposed terrain before then, which on long routes means starting well before sunrise. The sunrise and sunset calculator helps plan a pre-dawn start.
  • Check the forecast for the summit, not the town. Use the trail weather forecast for storm risk, wind and freezing level.
  • Set a turnaround time. Estimate realistic timings with the hiking time calculator, then add margin for slow scrambling.
  • Manage rockfall. Keep the group close together in gullies, avoid climbing directly below other parties, and yell "Rock!" for anything that moves.
  • Don't climb above your ability to descend. If you are not sure you can reverse a move, don't make it.
  • Leave a plan. Share your route and return time, and know what to do if you get lost or stranded.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping class 3. Jumping from class 2 hiking to class 4 because a peak is on a list.
  • Following the person ahead. Other parties are often off route. Navigate for yourself.
  • Underestimating the descent. Most accidents happen on the way down, when people are tired and rushing to beat weather.
  • Carrying a rope nobody can use. A rope without belay and rappel skills adds weight and false confidence.
  • Ignoring conditions. A dry class 4 route in August can be a serious snow climb in June.

Frequently asked questions

What is a class 4 scramble?

Class 4 is simple climbing on steep rock with significant exposure. You use your hands on every move, holds are smaller than on class 3, and a fall in an exposed section would likely be fatal. Many parties use a rope, especially for descending.

Do you need a rope for class 4?

Not always, but many parties carry one. Experienced scramblers often climb class 4 unroped, while others belay or rappel the hardest pitches. A rope only helps if everyone knows how to belay, build an anchor and rappel.

What is the difference between class 4 and 5.0?

Class 4 has large enough holds and low enough angle that most competent scramblers climb it without protection. Fifth class begins where the rock is steep and technical enough that a rope and protection are normally used.

Is Capitol Peak class 4?

Yes. The standard Northeast Ridge route on Capitol Peak in Colorado, which crosses the famous Knife Edge, is rated class 4 and is considered one of the hardest standard routes on the Colorado 14ers.
