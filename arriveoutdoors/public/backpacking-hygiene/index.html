Backpacking Hygiene: How to Stay Clean on the Trail and at Camp

Clean hands prevent most backcountry stomach bugs. Here is a light hygiene kit, how to wash without a shower, and how to handle bathroom needs the Leave No Trace way.

By The Arrive Outdoors TeamUpdated October 31, 20266 min read

Good backpacking hygiene comes down to three habits: clean your hands before you eat and after the bathroom, give your body a quick wipe or sponge bath each evening, and do all washing and bathroom business at least 200 feet (60 m) from water. The first habit matters most. Most "bad water" stomach illnesses on the trail actually spread hand to mouth, often through a shared bag of trail mix. Here's how to stay reasonably clean on a backpacking trip or at a campground without hurting the places you camp.

Why hygiene matters in the backcountry

  • Stomach illness. Bacteria and viruses from unwashed hands spread easily in a group sharing food, pots and water bottles. A day or two of diarrhea in the backcountry is miserable and dehydrating.
  • Skin problems. Sweat, dirt and friction cause chafing, rashes, and fungal infections in the groin and feet.
  • Blisters and foot infections. Wet, dirty feet blister faster.
  • Wildlife. Food smells on hands, clothes and gear attract animals to camp.
  • Water sources. Soap, food scraps and human waste near streams pollute the water you and others drink.

Clean hands: the habit that matters most

Clean your hands every time after using the bathroom and every time before you touch food. In a group, make it a camp rule before meals.

  • Soap and water is best. Have a partner pour a little water from a bottle while you lather with a drop of soap for about 20 seconds and rinse, 200 ft from any water source.
  • Sanitizer is the backup. Use one with at least 60% alcohol. The CDC notes sanitizer works less well on visibly dirty or greasy hands and doesn't kill every germ — norovirus is a notable example — so wipe off dirt first and wash with soap when you can.
  • Don't reach into shared food bags. Pour snacks into your hand instead.
  • Keep nails short; dirt and bacteria collect under them.

How to stay clean without a shower

The evening wipe-down

Once camp is set, wipe down the areas that cause problems: face, neck, armpits, groin, buttocks and feet. One wipe or a damp bandana is enough. Clean the groin area front to back, and do your feet last. This takes three minutes and makes sleeping much more comfortable.

The sponge bath

For a more thorough wash, carry 1–2 liters of water in a pot, bowl or collapsible bucket at least 200 ft from the source. Wet a bandana, add a drop of biodegradable soap, scrub, then rinse with clean water. Scatter the used water widely over the ground rather than dumping it in one spot.

Swimming and rinsing

A plain-water rinse in a lake feels great, but do it without soap, sunscreen or bug spray on your skin if you can — those wash off into the water. Be cautious about cold water and currents. Some areas restrict swimming in drinking-water sources; follow local rules.

At a campground

If you're car camping, many developed campgrounds have showers or are near a town with a coin shower. A solar shower bag hung in the sun warms water for an evening rinse. Use it where gray water can drain into soil, not at the campground water spigot or into a stream.

Tip: Keep one set of clothes — a base layer, underwear and socks — that you only wear in your sleeping bag. Going to bed in clean, dry clothes keeps your bag cleaner, keeps you warmer, and makes a dirty trail day feel reset.

Bathroom hygiene in the backcountry

Use outhouses or vault toilets wherever they exist. Where they don't, follow Leave No Trace guidance from lnt.org.

Peeing

Go at least 200 ft from water, trails and camp, and choose rocks, gravel or pine needles over plants (animals dig up vegetation for the salt). A pee cloth or a single square of TP packed out cuts down on waste.

Pooping: how to dig a cathole

  1. Walk at least 200 ft (about 70 adult steps) from water, trails and camp.
  2. Pick a spot with rich, dark soil and some sun if possible; it helps waste break down.
  3. Dig a hole 6–8 in (15–20 cm) deep and 4–6 in (10–15 cm) across.
  4. Use it, then mix in some soil with a stick, fill the hole with the original soil, and disguise it with leaves or duff.
  5. Clean your hands right away.

Toilet paper rules vary. In some forests plain, unscented TP can go deep in the cathole. In many deserts, high-elevation and high-use areas, you must pack it out. Packing it out in a doubled zip bag (a little baking soda cuts odor) is always acceptable. Never burn TP; it has started wildfires.

Some places — certain river corridors, alpine areas, deserts and permit zones like parts of popular national parks — require you to pack out all human waste in a WAG bag. Check the park or forest regulations when you get your permit.

Periods

  • Pack out all used pads, tampons and applicators in an opaque, sealable bag. Don't bury them; animals dig them up and they don't decompose quickly.
  • A menstrual cup means less to carry out. Empty it into a cathole and rinse it with treated water, then clean your hands.
  • Bring extra wipes and plan for a slightly larger trash bag.
  • Store used products with food and other scented items at night in bear country.

Teeth, face and skin

  • Brush with treated water. Spit toothpaste broadly ("spray" it) 200 ft from water and camp, or swallow it — don't leave a toothpaste blob next to your tent.
  • Wash your face at night to clear sweat and sunscreen; it reduces breakouts.
  • Reapply sunscreen and lip balm. Sunburn and chapped lips are common hygiene complaints at altitude.
  • Use anti-chafe balm on thighs, underarms and under pack straps before chafing starts.

Clothes and laundry

  • Merino wool and some synthetic fabrics treated for odor go multiple days between washes. Cotton stays wet and smells faster.
  • Rinse socks and underwear daily if you can: fill a zip bag or pot with water and a drop of soap, agitate, wring, rinse, and wring again — all 200 ft from water.
  • Hang wet clothes on your pack to dry while you walk.
  • Rotate two or three pairs of socks.

Foot care

  • Take off shoes and socks at long breaks to let feet dry and cool.
  • Treat hot spots immediately with tape or blister dressing before they become blisters.
  • Wash and dry feet at night, and sleep in dry socks.
  • Keep toenails trimmed straight across to avoid bruised toes on descents.

Dishes and kitchen hygiene

A dirty pot can make you as sick as unwashed hands. Scrape food residue out and eat it or pack it out. Wash with hot water and a drop of soap, at least 200 ft from water. Strain the dishwater through a small mesh strainer or bandana, pack out the food bits, and scatter the water. Treat any water you use for cleaning dishes or brushing teeth the same way you treat drinking water. Leave No Trace hygiene rules at a glance

  • All washing, soap, and bathroom use: at least 200 ft from water, trails and camp.
  • No soap in lakes or streams, even biodegradable.
  • Catholes 6–8 in deep, filled and disguised.
  • Pack out wipes, menstrual products, and TP where required.
  • Store scented hygiene products with food at night in bear country.

Frequently asked questions

How do you stay clean while backpacking?

Wash or sanitize hands after the bathroom and before eating, take a quick wipe or sponge bath each evening focusing on armpits, groin and feet, keep a dedicated set of clean sleep clothes, and rinse socks and underwear as needed. All washing happens at least 200 feet from water.

How deep should a cathole be?

Leave No Trace recommends 6 to 8 inches deep and 4 to 6 inches across, at least 200 feet (about 70 adult steps) from water, trails and camp. Fill it with the original soil when done.

Is biodegradable soap OK in lakes and streams?

No. Even biodegradable soap harms aquatic life and needs soil to break down. Carry water at least 200 feet from the source, wash there, and scatter the strained gray water.

Does hand sanitizer work in the backcountry?

Sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol helps, but the CDC notes it does not work as well on visibly dirty or greasy hands and does not kill every germ, such as norovirus. Washing with soap and water is better when you can.

Can you bury toilet paper?

In some areas, plain unscented toilet paper can go deep in a cathole, but many parks, deserts and high-use areas require you to pack it out. Packing it out in a sealed bag is always acceptable.
