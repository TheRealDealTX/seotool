How the hike score works

The score starts at 100 and subtracts for the conditions that make hiking less pleasant or less safe: highs below 50°F or a feels-like temperature above 78°F, chance and amount of precipitation, gusts above 25 mph, thunderstorms (a big deduction — lightning is the main summer hazard in the mountains) and snow. 80+ is great, 65–79 good, 50–64 fair, under 50 poor.

It's a quick comparison between days, not a safety guarantee. Mountain weather changes fast, and the forecast is for the point you picked — conditions on a summit 3,000 ft higher can be very different. Use the freezing-level line to judge whether high terrain will have snow and ice, and check the National Weather Service forecast and any park alerts before you go.

Reading a mountain forecast

  • Afternoon thunderstorms: in summer, plan to be off summits and exposed ridges by early afternoon when storms are in the forecast.
  • Elevation: temperatures typically drop 3–5°F for every 1,000 ft you climb, and wind increases on ridgelines.
  • Heat: above about 88°F feels-like, start at dawn and carry more water — see the water calculator.
  • Cold nights: a freezing low changes your sleep system; size it with the sleeping bag guide.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the forecast come from?

Forecasts come from Open-Meteo, which blends national weather models such as NOAA's GFS and HRRR. Always cross-check with the National Weather Service for your area before a big day.

What is a good hike score?

80 or higher is a great hiking day, 65 to 79 is good, 50 to 64 is fair, and below 50 means poor or risky conditions such as storms, extreme heat or strong winds.

What does freezing level mean?

It's the elevation where the air temperature hits 32°F. Above it, precipitation falls as snow and wet rock can ice up, so trails above the freezing level need winter gear and judgment.
