How to Put Out a Campfire (and What to Do When a Fire Gets Away)

Drown, stir, feel, repeat until the ashes are cold to the touch. Plus how to handle a grease or stove fire at camp, and when to stop fighting a fire and leave.

To put out a campfire, drown it with water, stir the ashes and embers, then feel for heat — and repeat until everything is cold to the touch. That is the method the USDA Forest Service's Smokey Bear program teaches, and the "feel" step is the one people skip. If you can't safely touch the ashes with the back of your hand, the fire isn't out. This guide covers that process in detail, plus what to do about grease and stove fires at camp and when to stop fighting a fire and leave.

Why "mostly out" isn't out

Coals buried in ash can hold enough heat to reignite for many hours after the flames are gone. A breeze the next afternoon can lift an ember into dry grass long after you've driven home. Abandoned and poorly extinguished campfires are a well-documented cause of human-caused wildfires, which is why agencies repeat the same rule: if it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave.

In many places you can also be held financially responsible for the cost of suppressing a fire you started, on top of any citation.

Set up before you light it

Putting a fire out is much easier if you planned for it:

  • Check fire restrictions on the managing agency's website and at the campground entrance. During a burn ban, no campfire is legal, and some restrictions also ban charcoal or wood stoves.
  • Use an established fire ring in a cleared area, away from overhanging branches, tents and dry grass.
  • Stage water and a tool first. A full bucket of water and a shovel should sit beside the ring before the match is struck. A Freegrace collapsible bucket packs flat in the car.
  • Keep it small. A fire that stays inside the ring and below knee height is easy to drown. A bonfire takes a lot of water and a long time to cool.
  • Don't light one in wind. Gusty conditions throw embers. If it's windy, skip the fire; the trail weather forecast shows wind for your campsite.
  • Stop adding wood an hour before bed so it burns down to ash, which is far easier to extinguish than half-burned logs.

How to put out a campfire: drown, stir, feel

  1. Let it burn down. Spread out large logs so they burn to ash, and stop adding fuel well before you plan to leave.
  2. Drown it. Pour water over the whole fire, not just the glowing spots — drown all the embers, including the black ones. Pour slowly enough to avoid blasting up a cloud of hot steam and ash. Keep pouring until the hissing stops.
  3. Stir it. Use a shovel or stout stick to stir the ashes and embers, turning up the bottom layer. Scrape partly burned sticks and logs to knock off embers, and make sure both sides of every log are wet.
  4. Drown it again. Stirring exposes hot material that the first pour didn't reach. Add more water and stir again.
  5. Feel it. Hold the back of your hand just above the ashes first. If you feel no heat, carefully touch the ashes, coals and the stones of the ring. Anything warm means repeat steps 2 to 4.
  6. Check the surroundings. Look for embers that may have popped outside the ring and wet them down too.
Safety: The fire is out only when everything — ash, coals, logs and the ring itself — is cold to the touch. Steam and smoke mean heat is still there. Never leave camp or go to sleep with a fire that is "almost out."

If you don't have enough water

Water is far better, but if you truly have none, Smokey Bear guidance is to use dirt or sand mixed in with the embers: add it, stir it through the coals, and keep going until the mix is cool. Don't simply pile dirt on top and walk away. A buried fire can smolder for hours, and the heat can creep into roots and dry organic soil. Then feel for heat the same way.

How much water to carry

For a small campfire, plan on at least one full bucket — 2 to 5 gallons (8 to 19 L) — and expect to need more if the fire was large or burned a long time. A couple of water bottles is not enough. At backcountry sites, put the fire out with water carried from the source before you leave, and if fires aren't allowed or wood is scarce, use a stove instead.

Grease and stove fires at camp

Cooking fires are the other fire you are most likely to face at camp. The rules are different from a campfire.

Grease or oil fire in a pan

  • Never use water. Water hitting burning oil flashes to steam and throws flaming grease around.
  • Turn off the burner if you can reach the valve without reaching through the flames.
  • Smother it. Slide a lid, metal plate or second pan over the top and leave it on until the pan has cooled. A fire blanket also works.
  • Don't carry the pan. Moving it spills burning oil onto you, the table or the ground.
  • For a very small grease fire, baking soda or salt poured on can smother it. Flour does not work and can flare.
  • If it's growing, use a fire extinguisher rated for Class B fires (an ABC extinguisher covers wood, grease and electrical). A compact unit like the First Alert HOME1 fire extinguisher fits in a camp kitchen bin or RV. Aim at the base of the flames and sweep side to side.

Propane and canister stove fires

  • If the stove is burning outside its burner, turn the fuel off at the valve or tank if you can do so without reaching through flame.
  • If the fire is at the hose, regulator or canister connection, don't try to unscrew or carry it. Move people back, clear anything flammable you can reach safely, and use an extinguisher or call 911 if it spreads.
  • Prevent it: cook only on a stable, level surface, keep the stove away from the tent and dry grass, check O-rings and connections for damage before each trip, and never cook inside a tent or vestibule.

If clothing catches fire

Stop, drop and roll: stop where you are, drop to the ground, cover your face with your hands, and roll until the flames are out. Cool a burn with cool (not ice-cold) running water for several minutes and seek medical care for anything larger than a small surface burn.

If a fire gets away or a wildfire starts

A fire that has escaped the ring into dry grass can spread faster than you can run uphill. You get a few seconds to try to put out a small spot fire with water or by smothering it. If it isn't out almost immediately, stop and switch to getting people out.

Report it

  • Call 911 as soon as you can. Wildfire reports in the US go through 911.
  • Give your location as precisely as you can — campground and site number, road name and mile marker, trail name, or GPS coordinates from your phone's map app.
  • Describe what you see: size, what's burning (grass, brush, trees), which way the smoke is moving, and whether anyone is in danger.
  • If you lack signal, tell a camp host or ranger, or report it as soon as you reach service. Satellite messengers and phones with satellite SOS can also reach emergency services.

Leave

  • Go early. Don't wait for an official order if fire or heavy smoke is approaching. Leave gear behind if it slows you down.
  • Drive out on the main access road with headlights on. Don't drive into smoke you can't see through.
  • On foot, move away from the fire and toward areas with little fuel — roads, wide rocky areas, large bodies of water, or areas that have already burned and cooled. Avoid narrow canyons and steep slopes above the fire; fire runs uphill fast.
  • Follow instructions from rangers and fire crews, and stay out of closed areas.

Before you go, check for active fire closures with the managing agency (for example, the park's alerts page on nps.gov or the national forest's page on fs.usda.gov), and keep your car parked facing out with enough fuel to leave.

When not to fight a fire

Stop and leave if any of these are true:

  • The fire has spread beyond the ring or the pan and is bigger than you can cover with a bucket or extinguisher in seconds.
  • It's spreading toward your exit, or smoke is making it hard to breathe or see.
  • Your extinguisher is empty and the fire is still burning.
  • A tent is on fire — modern tent fabric melts and burns very quickly. Get everyone out and away first.

Gear can be replaced. Get people clear and call for help.

A simple camp fire-safety kit

  • Bucket (collapsible or hard) kept full near the ring
  • Shovel or trowel for stirring
  • ABC fire extinguisher in the car or camp kitchen bin
  • Pan lid or fire blanket for cooking fires
  • Phone with offline map, plus a satellite messenger in remote areas

Frequently asked questions

How do you put out a campfire properly?

Let it burn down to ash if you can, then drown it with water, stir the ashes and embers with a shovel, scrape any logs, add more water and stir again. Feel the ashes with the back of your hand; if anything is still warm, repeat. It is out only when everything is cold to the touch.

How much water does it take to put out a campfire?

More than most people expect. Plan on at least a full bucket, about 2 to 5 gallons for a small fire, and keep adding water until the hissing stops and the ashes are cold.

Can you put out a campfire with dirt?

Only if you have no water. Mix dirt or sand into the embers and stir, do not just pile it on top. Burying a fire can keep coals hot for hours and let them reignite later.

Should you put water on a grease fire?

No. Water makes burning oil spatter and spread. Turn off the heat if you safely can, cover the pan with a lid or metal plate, and leave it covered until it cools, or use a Class B or ABC fire extinguisher.

What should you do if you see a wildfire while camping?

Leave the area and call 911 as soon as you can, giving your location and what you see. Do not try to fight it, and do not wait to gather gear.
