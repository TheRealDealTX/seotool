Best Sun Hoodies for Hiking: How to Choose One That Actually Protects You

A sun hoodie replaces most of the sunscreen you would sweat off on an exposed trail. Here is how UPF ratings work, which fabrics and hood designs hold up, and which hoodies to shortlist.

The best sun hoodie for hiking is a thin, light-colored, quick-drying top rated UPF 50+ (which blocks about 98% of UV), with a hood that stays put over a cap and sleeves long enough to cover the backs of your hands. Synthetic hoodies dry fastest and cost less; merino blends smell better on multi-day trips. Fit and hood design matter more than brand.

Why hikers switch to sun hoodies

Sunscreen works, but on a hot trail it sweats off, rubs off under pack straps and needs reapplying every two hours or so. A sun hoodie covers your arms, shoulders, neck and the back of your head with protection that does not wear off as the day goes on. It also simplifies your kit: one top replaces a T-shirt, a sun hat's neck flap and most of your sunscreen.

UV exposure on the trail is usually higher than at home. UV intensity rises with elevation (the World Health Organization estimates roughly 10% per 1,000 m, or about 3,300 ft), fresh snow can reflect most of the UV back up at you, and water and pale rock reflect a meaningful amount too. A hike that starts at 9,000 feet in Colorado or crosses white slickrock in Utah delivers a much bigger dose than a city walk at the same hour. Check the UV index for your trip on the EPA's sun safety pages or in your forecast.

UPF ratings explained

UPF (Ultraviolet Protection Factor) measures how much UV radiation, both UVA and UVB, passes through a fabric. A UPF 50 fabric lets through 1/50th of the UV that hits it. In the US, garments are tested in a lab and labeled in three bands:

UPF labelLabel categoryUV blocked (approx.)Good for
15–24Good93–96%Short, shaded or low-elevation hikes
25–39Very good96–97%Most day hikes with sunscreen on exposed skin
40–50+Excellent97.5–98%+All-day exposure, high elevation, snow, fair skin

The jump from UPF 30 to UPF 50 looks small in percentage terms, but think of it the other way around: UPF 30 lets through about 3.3% of UV and UPF 50 lets through 2%, so the 50 passes roughly 40% less. For an eight-hour day above treeline, that difference is real.

For comparison, a typical white cotton T-shirt rates somewhere around UPF 5–7, and less when it is wet. That is why "I'll just wear a long-sleeve cotton shirt" is not the same as wearing a rated sun top.

What lowers a fabric's real-world protection

  • Stretching. A hoodie that is tight across your shoulders or back opens up the weave exactly where the sun hits hardest. Size so the fabric is not pulled taut over your pack straps.
  • Wetness. Many fabrics, especially cotton, block less UV when soaked. Most synthetic sun hoodies are designed to keep their rating wet, but check the brand's claim.
  • Wear. Thin spots and abrasion from pack straps reduce protection over time. If you can see light through the shoulders when you hold the hoodie up to a window, it is time to replace it.
  • Color. Darker and brighter dyes generally absorb more UV than pale ones. Very pale fabrics rely more on tight weave or UV-absorbing finishes to reach UPF 50+, which is why a white hoodie and a navy one from the same line can carry different ratings.
Tip: Read the label, not the product name. Some lines carry different UPF ratings by color, and "sun shirt" on a tag guarantees nothing. Look for a stated UPF number.

A simple decision rule: if you will sweat heavily or the trip is one to three days, pick synthetic. If you will wear the same top for four days or more without washing, a merino or merino-synthetic blend is worth the extra cost and slower drying.

Weight and weave

Brands rarely publish fabric weight in a consistent way, so judge by feel: thin, airy knits with a slightly open texture breathe best in heat but snag more easily on brush and granite. Denser, smoother knits survive scrambling and pack abrasion better but run warmer. If your hikes involve bushwhacking or rock, durability usually beats breathability; on a groomed desert trail, the reverse.

Hood design: the detail that decides comfort

The hood is what separates a sun hoodie from a long-sleeve sun shirt, and it is where cheap designs fail.

  • Fitted ("scuba") hoods hug your head and stay on in wind. They work well over a ball cap, whose brim then shades your face. This is the most popular design for exposed hiking.
  • Loose, oversized hoods are breezier but blow off in gusts and block your peripheral vision when you turn your head, which matters on ledges and when crossing roads.
  • Neck coverage. The back and sides of your neck burn first. A hood that sits close at the neck, or a crossover or quarter-zip collar that closes up, protects that zone without a separate gaiter.
  • Face masks and gaiters. Some hoodies have a built-in face cover. Useful in sand, snow glare or very high UV; many hikers find them hot and prefer a separate thin gaiter they can pull down.

Test it before you buy: put on a cap, pull up the hood and turn your head fully left and right. If the hood stays in place and you can still see over your shoulder, it will work on the trail.

Fit and features that matter

  • Relaxed, not baggy. A little air between fabric and skin feels cooler and keeps the weave from stretching. Too loose, and the fabric flaps and bunches under pack straps and the hip belt.
  • Thumb loops. They keep sleeves over the backs of your hands, the spot most hikers forget to sunscreen. Using trekking poles? Check that the loops do not rub the web of your thumb.
  • Sleeve length. Raise your arms overhead as if reaching for a hold. Your wrists should stay covered.
  • Hem length. A longer hem stays tucked under a hip belt and covers your lower back when you bend over. Too long, and it bunches up under the belt.
  • Zips. A quarter-zip dumps heat on climbs. Trade-off: extra weight, and a zipper that can rub your neck.
  • Flatlock seams on the shoulders. Raised seams under pack straps cause chafing over a long day.
Safety: A sun hoodie reduces sun exposure; it does not prevent heat illness. In extreme heat, the bigger risks are dehydration and heat exhaustion. Start early, carry enough water, and turn around if you feel dizzy, nauseated or stop sweating. See the Hiking Water Calculator and the Heat Index Calculator before hot hikes.

How to choose in five questions

  1. Where will you hike? Desert, canyon country or humid summer heat: lightest synthetic you can find, pale color. Alpine and snow: UPF 50+ is worth prioritizing, and a slightly denser fabric doubles as a light layer on cool mornings.
  2. How long will you wear it without washing? One to three days: synthetic. Four or more: merino or a blend.
  3. What is the terrain? Brush, granite and scrambling shred thin knits. Pick a tougher fabric for class 3 scrambles and off-trail travel.
  4. Do you wear a cap? If yes, choose a fitted hood that goes over the cap. If you prefer a wide-brim hat, a hoodless sun shirt with a high collar may suit you better.
  5. What is the UPF on the label? Aim for 30 at minimum and 50+ for long, exposed or high-elevation days.

Hoodless alternatives

If hoods feel claustrophobic, a long-sleeve sun shirt or a vented button-down plus a wide-brim hat gives similar coverage. Button-downs vent well and look fine in town, but the collar leaves more neck exposed than a good hood.

Care and when to replace

  • Wash synthetic hoodies in cool or warm water and skip fabric softener, which can clog the wicking finish.
  • Wash merino on a gentle cycle and air-dry it to avoid shrinking.
  • Replace when the fabric goes thin or see-through at the shoulders and hips (the pack-strap zones), or when the hood no longer holds its shape.

Pair your hoodie with sunglasses, a brimmed cap and sunscreen on your face and hands, and you have covered the main sun risks for summer trips to places like Zion, Grand Canyon or Death Valley (where summer hiking below the rim or on the valley floor is dangerous no matter what you wear). Frequently asked questions

What does UPF 50+ mean?

UPF 50+ means the fabric lets through no more than 1/50th of the UV that hits it, so it blocks about 98% of UV radiation. 50+ is the top category on US labels; any fabric that tests above 50 is simply labeled 50+.

Is UPF 30 enough for hiking?

UPF 30 blocks about 97% of UV, which is adequate for many hikes. If you have fair skin, hike at high elevation, on snow, or for many hours a day, UPF 50+ gives a bit more margin and is widely available at the same price.

Are sun hoodies hotter than a T-shirt?

A thin, light-colored sun hoodie usually feels similar to or cooler than a T-shirt in strong sun, because it shades your skin and wicks sweat. Thick or dark hoodies can feel warmer, so pick a lightweight fabric for desert and summer hiking.

Do I still need sunscreen with a sun hoodie?

Yes, on anything the fabric does not cover: face, ears, neck, and the backs of hands if your sleeves lack thumb loops. A hood shades the sides of your face but not the nose and cheeks in high sun.

Does UPF wash out of clothing?

Fabrics that get their UPF from tight weave and fiber type keep it for the life of the garment. Some garments use a finish that can fade over many washes; check the care label and the brand's claims.
