Honda CR-V Camper: The Complete Guide to Camping in a CR-V

A CR-V makes a capable small camper with the right bed, airflow and privacy setup. Here's how to choose between a no-build setup and a platform, measure your own cargo space, and camp legally and comfortably.

By The Arrive Outdoors TeamUpdated October 31, 20269 min read

A Honda CR-V makes a surprisingly good camper: fold the rear seats, slide the front seats forward, and most generations give one or two people a sleeping space roughly as long as a twin mattress. You can camp in it with no building at all, using an SUV air mattress or foam pad, or build a simple removable platform that adds a flat bed and storage underneath. What makes the difference between a miserable night and a good one is airflow, privacy, a level parking spot and knowing where sleeping in your car is allowed.

Start by deciding how often you'll camp and whether the CR-V also has to be your daily driver.

For most people, a no-build setup is the right first step. Do a few nights with a pad, find out what bothers you, then decide whether a platform is worth it.

Measure your own cargo floor first

The CR-V has gone through six generations since the late 1990s, and seat-folding mechanisms, cargo floor length and the angle of the folded seats all changed along the way. In general, newer models fold flatter and more easily, while older ones may fold or tumble the rear seats in a way that leaves a shorter or more uneven floor. Rather than trusting a number from a forum about someone else's model year, take five minutes with a tape measure:

  1. Length: fold the rear seats, slide the front seats all the way forward and tilt their seatbacks forward. Measure from the inside of the closed tailgate to the back of the front seats, both at floor level and at about 12 inches up. Then measure again with the front seats in your normal driving position, which is the length you'll have if you want to leave without rearranging.
  2. Width: measure between the rear wheel wells (the narrowest point) and at the widest point above them.
  3. Height: measure from the cargo floor to the headliner. You'll want to be able to sit up, so subtract the thickness of your mattress or platform.
  4. Slope and gaps: lay a straight board or level across the folded seats and note how much they rise and where any gap or step is.

Compare the length to your height plus 2 to 3 inches for a pillow and toes. If you're short on length, you can sleep diagonally, remove the rear headrests, or build a platform that extends over the folded seats and into the footwell space behind the front seats.

No-build sleeping setups

SUV air mattress

SUV-specific air mattresses are shaped to fill the cargo area and have side extensions that fill the gap behind the front seats. The Outraveler SUV air mattress is one example; check its inflated dimensions against your measurements. Air mattresses are comfortable but cold in cool weather, because the air inside draws heat from your body. Add a closed-cell foam pad or blanket on top below about 50°F (10°C).

Foam pads and camping pads

A thick self-inflating pad or a trifold memory-foam mattress is quieter than an air mattress, won't leak, and insulates better. Two backpacking pads side by side also work for two people. Fill the seat slope with folded blankets or a firm cushion under the pad.

Level your parking spot

Even a slight slope feels exaggerated when you're lying down. Park facing slightly uphill so your head ends up higher than your feet, and carry a small level or use a phone level app. Leveling blocks under the downhill wheels help in campgrounds with sloped sites.

Building a simple sleeping platform

A platform creates a flat bed over the folded seats and turns the space underneath into storage. The common designs:

  • Full-width hinged platform: two or three sections of 1/2- to 3/4-inch plywood hinged together, sitting on short legs or a 2x4 frame. The sections fold for storage or to bring a rear seat back into use.
  • Half-width solo platform: a single bed down one side, leaving the other rear seat usable and space for gear or a dog.
  • Drawer system: a box frame with slide-out drawers under the bed. More work and more weight, but very organized.

Design tips:

  • Height is the tradeoff. Every inch of platform height adds storage underneath but takes an inch of headroom. Sit on your mattress on the cargo floor and see how much space you have above your head before choosing a height.
  • Make it removable without tools if the car is also your daily driver. Pieces that bolt to the factory tie-down points stay put without permanent changes.
  • Cut a cardboard template of the cargo floor before cutting plywood. The sides of the cargo area are curved, and a template saves wasted wood.
  • Keep it light. 1/2-inch plywood with good support underneath is usually enough for the bed surface and saves weight over thicker panels.
  • Secure everything. In a sudden stop, a loose platform and the gear under it become projectiles. Strap or bolt the platform down and keep heavy items low and forward.

Ventilation and condensation

A closed car with one or two people breathing in it all night fills with moisture. By morning, the windows can be dripping and your sleeping bag damp. Ventilation is the fix:

  • Crack at least two windows, ideally at opposite corners of the car (front and rear) so air can flow through. An inch or two is usually enough.
  • Add mesh window screens that slip over the door frames, like these car window mesh screens, so you can open windows wider without mosquitoes.
  • Install rain guards (window deflectors) so you can leave windows cracked when it rains.
  • Keep wet gear out of the cabin, or seal it in a bag. Wet boots and rain jackets add a lot of moisture overnight.
  • Wipe and air out in the morning. Keep a microfiber towel handy, and open the doors for ten minutes while you make coffee.
Safety: Don't sleep with the engine running for heat or air conditioning; exhaust can seep into the cabin, especially if snow, a snowbank or a wall blocks the tailpipe. Never use a propane heater, stove or charcoal inside the vehicle. A battery-powered portable carbon monoxide alarm is cheap insurance.

Staying warm or cool

A car is barely insulated, so it cools to near the outside temperature overnight and heats up fast once the sun hits it.

  • Cold nights: use a sleeping bag rated at least 10°F below the expected low, and an insulated pad under you. The sleeping bag and pad temperature guide tells you what rating and R-value to look for, and our cold weather camping guide covers the rest. Insulated window covers help hold a little warmth.
  • Hot nights: park in the shade with the morning sun blocked, open windows with screens, and use reflective window covers during the day. A small battery-powered fan helps air move. Camping at higher elevation is the easiest way to beat summer heat; many of the ideas in how to cool a tent without electricity also work in a car.

Privacy and window covers

Window covers do three jobs: privacy, blocking early-morning light, and some insulation.

  • Custom-fit covers are cut for your exact model year and press into the window frames. They're the quickest and neatest option, and many have a reflective side and a dark side.
  • DIY reflective insulation: cut panels of foil-faced bubble insulation using paper templates of each window. Cover the outward side with black fabric for a lower profile.
  • Curtains: a tension rod or cord behind the front seats with a dark curtain hides the sleeping area from the windshield.

A reflective windshield sunshade is visible from far away and announces that someone is sleeping inside. If you want to keep a low profile, dark-faced covers are less conspicuous.

Power: charging and lights

  • Avoid draining the car battery. Running accessories off the 12V outlet with the engine off for hours can leave you with a battery too weak to start the car, and in many vehicles the outlets only work with the ignition on anyway.
  • A portable power station is the simplest solution. A compact unit like the Jackery Explorer 300 Plus charges phones, a headlamp, a camera and a small fan for a weekend, and can be recharged from the car while driving or from a solar panel.
  • Lights: a headlamp plus a USB-rechargeable lantern or string lights clipped to the grab handles. Set the car's dome lights so they don't switch on every time you open a door.

Storage and organization

The CR-V's limited interior means organization matters more than in a bigger rig.

  • Bins under the platform or behind the front seats, labeled by category: kitchen, clothes, tools, food.
  • Seatback organizers for headlamps, wipes, chargers and anything you'll want in the dark.
  • Front seats as the overnight garage: with a no-build setup, bags and bins go on the front seats at night.
  • Roof storage: a soft cargo bag on crossbars, like the Sailnovo rooftop cargo bag, holds bulky, light items like chairs and sleeping bags. Check your roof rails' load rating in the owner's manual before loading anything on the roof.
  • Kitchen outside: keep the stove, fuel, cooler and dishes in one bin you can pull out onto the tailgate or a folding table. Always cook outside the vehicle. A tailgate awning or tarp gives you a dry place to cook in rain.

Where it's legal to sleep in your CR-V

Rules depend entirely on who manages the land or parking lot. Here's how it generally works:

  • Developed campgrounds: sleeping in your car in a campsite is allowed almost everywhere. National park campgrounds and many national forest campgrounds can be reserved through recreation.gov.
  • National forests: dispersed camping (camping outside developed campgrounds, without facilities) is generally allowed on Forest Service land unless an area is signed closed. Stay limits are commonly 14 days, but each forest sets its own rules. The forest's free Motor Vehicle Use Map shows which roads are open to vehicles. Check the specific forest's page on fs.usda.gov.
  • BLM land: dispersed camping is allowed on much Bureau of Land Management land, also commonly with a 14-day limit, though some areas near cities or popular sites restrict it. Check the local BLM field office.
  • National parks: outside designated campgrounds, sleeping in your car is generally not allowed, including at trailheads and pullouts. Plan a campground or stay outside the park on neighboring public land.
  • Rest areas: rules vary by state. Some allow a few hours of rest, others prohibit overnight parking entirely. Look for posted signs.
  • Store and business parking lots: depends on the individual store's policy and local ordinances. Ask the manager, and respect "no overnight parking" signs.
  • City streets: many cities restrict or ban sleeping in vehicles. This is the riskiest option for a knock on the window.

Low-profile and dispersed camping etiquette

  • Use existing sites. In dispersed areas, park on a spot that's already been used rather than driving onto vegetation, and stay well away from water.
  • Arrive late and leave early anywhere other than a campground. Don't set up chairs, awnings or a stove in a parking lot.
  • Pack out everything, including food scraps. Use a restroom where one exists; otherwise follow Leave No Trace guidance for human waste.
  • Check fire restrictions. Many western forests and BLM areas restrict campfires in summer. See Smokey Bear for fire safety basics, and know how to put out a campfire completely.
  • Keep noise and lights low, and don't idle the engine for long periods.
  • Tell someone where you are when camping in remote dispersed spots with no cell service.

Your first night: a quick checklist

  • Measure the cargo floor and test-fit your mattress or pad at home.
  • Sleep one night in the driveway to find what's missing.
  • Pack window covers, mesh screens, a headlamp and a power bank or power station.
  • Bring a sleeping bag rated for the forecast low, checked with the trail weather forecast.
  • Know exactly where you'll sleep and whether it's allowed, plus a backup spot.

Frequently asked questions

Can you sleep in the back of a Honda CR-V?

Yes. With the rear seats folded and the front seats pushed forward, most CR-V generations have room for one or two people to sleep in the back. Taller sleepers may need to sleep diagonally or build a platform that extends over the folded seats. Measure your own vehicle before buying a mattress.

Do the Honda CR-V rear seats fold flat?

Newer generations fold much flatter than older ones, but most CR-Vs still leave a slight slope or a small gap between the seatbacks and the cargo floor. A foam pad, a few folded blankets or a simple platform evens it out.

How do you stop condensation when sleeping in a CR-V?

Crack at least two windows, ideally on opposite corners, and cover them with mesh screens to keep bugs out. Rain guards let you leave windows open in wet weather. Wipe the glass in the morning and air the car out during the day.

Is it legal to sleep in your car?

It depends on where you are. Dispersed camping on many national forest and BLM lands is generally allowed with stay limits, often 14 days. Rest areas, store parking lots and city streets each have their own state, local or store rules, and many prohibit overnight sleeping.

Is it safe to run the engine while sleeping in a car?

It is not recommended. Exhaust can enter the cabin, especially if the tailpipe is blocked by snow or the car is parked close to a wall or embankment. Use a warmer sleeping bag instead, and carry a battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm.
