Truck Camping: How to Turn Your Pickup Into a Camper

How to camp in a pickup: the four main setups compared, how to measure your bed, keeping condensation and carbon monoxide under control, powering your gear, and where you can legally park for the night.

By The Arrive Outdoors TeamPublished November 22, 20267 min read

Truck camping means sleeping in or on your pickup, and there are four ways to do it: a truck bed tent, a sleeping platform under a cap (topper), a rooftop tent on a bed rack, or a slide-in camper. A bed tent or a cap with a foam mattress gets you camping this weekend for little money; the bigger setups make sense if you camp often. Whichever you pick, measure your bed first, plan for ventilation, and never run a fuel heater or the engine where you sleep.

If the truck is your daily driver and you're not sure how often you'll camp, start with a bed tent or a cap and a foam mattress. Do a few nights, then decide whether a platform or rooftop tent solves real problems for you.

Measure your truck bed first

Pickup beds are sold by rough names (short, standard, long), but actual dimensions vary by make, model and year. Measure before you buy a tent, mattress or platform.

Bed typeTypical lengthSleeping notes
Short bed (many midsize and crew-cab trucks)About 5 to 5.5 ftTight for adults; sleep diagonally or use the tailgate with an extender or bed-tent extension
Standard bedAbout 6 to 6.5 ftFits most people lying straight
Long bedAbout 8 ftRoom to sleep plus gear storage at the foot

Measure these five things:

  1. Floor length from the bulkhead (front wall) to the inside of the closed tailgate, and again with the tailgate open.
  2. Width between the wheel wells, which limits a mattress that sits on the floor.
  3. Width between the bed walls above the wheel wells, which is the width available on a raised platform.
  4. Height from the bed floor to the top of the bed rail, and to the inside of the cap if you have one. Subtract your platform and mattress height to see how much sitting room is left.
  5. Tie-down and stake pocket locations, for securing a platform or rack.
Tip: A platform that sits just above the wheel wells uses the full bed width and leaves storage below, but every inch of platform height is an inch less headroom. With a cab-high cap, many people end up with a low platform and a thin mattress so they can sit up.

Building a comfortable bed

  • Mattress: a foam mattress cut to size is warm and doesn't deflate. Air mattresses made for truck beds work, but they're cold in cool weather, and the bed floor is metal or plastic that pulls heat away. Put a closed-cell foam pad under any air mattress below about 50°F (10°C).
  • Bed liner: ribbed bed liners are uncomfortable to sleep on directly; a foam pad or a carpet kit smooths them out.
  • Level the truck: park so your head is slightly uphill. Leveling blocks under the tires help on uneven sites.
  • Warmth: a truck bed loses heat in every direction. Bring a bag rated about 10°F below the expected low, and read cold weather camping for shoulder-season nights.

Ventilation and condensation

A sleeping person breathes out a lot of moisture overnight. In a closed cap or camper shell, that moisture condenses on the cold metal, glass and fiberglass and drips back onto you and your bedding.

  • Open two windows or vents at opposite ends to create airflow, even on cold nights. Bug screens let you do this in summer.
  • A small battery-powered fan or a roof vent fan pulls damp air out.
  • Keep wet clothes, boots and rain gear out of the sleeping area.
  • Insulate the cap's ceiling and walls (foil-faced bubble insulation or carpet liner) to reduce condensation and cold spots.
  • Put a moisture-wicking mesh mat or wool blanket under the mattress so water doesn't pool beneath it.
  • Air out the bedding during the day whenever you can.

Carbon monoxide: the rule that matters most

Safety: Never run the engine, a propane heater, a stove, a lantern or a generator inside or next to an enclosed sleeping space, and never sleep with one running. Carbon monoxide is odorless and can kill in your sleep. Exhaust can also enter a cap or cab from a running engine, especially if the tailpipe is blocked by snow or the truck is parked against a bank or wall. Carry a battery-powered CO alarm in your sleeping area. The CDC's carbon monoxide page explains the symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion.

Heaters sold as "indoor safe" usually have low-oxygen and tip-over shutoffs, but they still burn fuel and produce some carbon monoxide. The safest way to stay warm is passive: a warmer sleeping bag, an insulated pad, a wool blanket, a hat, and a hot water bottle (a sealed bottle of hot water in your bag). Cook outside, with the tailgate as your counter.

Power for lights, phones and a fridge

  • Portable power station: the simplest option. Charge it at home, from the truck's 12V outlet while driving, or with a solar panel. Size it by adding up what you'll run: phones and lights take very little; a 12V fridge running all day takes much more.
  • Dual battery or DC-DC charger: a second battery charged by the alternator keeps a fridge and lights running without touching the starting battery. A permanent setup that's worth it for frequent trips.
  • Protect the starting battery: don't run accessories off it overnight. Carry a portable jump starter anyway.
  • Lighting: stick-on LED puck lights or a string of USB lights inside the cap, plus a headlamp. A red light setting keeps your night vision and annoys neighbors less.

Where you can truck camp

Established campgrounds

Any campground site that allows tents or vehicles works for truck camping, and it's the easiest way to start: water, toilets and no questions. A pickup with a cap fits in most tent sites as long as you park on the parking pad.

National forests and BLM land

Dispersed camping (camping outside developed campgrounds, with no facilities) is generally allowed on much national forest and Bureau of Land Management land. Stay limits are commonly 14 days, but rules vary by forest, district and area, and some places restrict camping to designated spots.

  • Use the forest's free Motor Vehicle Use Map (MVUM) to see which roads are open to vehicles and where you can pull off to camp. Find it on the forest's page at fs.usda.gov.
  • For BLM land, check the field office's page at blm.gov for camping rules and closures.
  • Camp on spots that have clearly been used before, at least 200 feet from lakes and streams where possible, and don't drive off-road to create a new site.
  • Check fire restrictions, which change with conditions.

Places like the Grand Mesa in Colorado have plenty of national forest roads with established dispersed sites; see the Grand Mesa fishing guide for one way to spend the days. Around Great Sand Dunes, primitive sites along the Medano Pass road need high clearance or 4WD, so check road conditions with the park.

Towns, stores and rest areas

Overnight parking in store lots, rest areas and city streets depends on state law, local ordinances and the store's own policy. Many prohibit it. Ask the manager, look for posted signs, and have a backup plan.

Stealth camping etiquette

When you do sleep somewhere other than a campground, the goal is to leave no sign you were there.

  • Arrive late, leave early, and don't set up chairs, awnings or a stove.
  • Keep lights low and covered; window covers on the cap help.
  • Don't idle the engine for heat or charging.
  • Use real restrooms and pack out all trash.
  • If someone asks you to move, move. Being polite keeps these places open for everyone.

A quick word on slide-in campers

A slide-in camper sits in the bed and turns the truck into a small RV. Before buying one, check your truck's payload capacity, printed on a sticker in the driver's door jamb. A loaded camper, plus water, gear and passengers, can exceed what a half-ton truck is rated to carry, which is why lightweight pop-up campers are popular on smaller trucks. Match the camper to your bed length too: long-bed and short-bed campers aren't interchangeable.

Frequently asked questions

Can you sleep in the bed of a pickup truck?

Yes. With a truck bed tent or a cap (topper) over the bed, most pickups give one or two people a sleeping space. Short beds (about 5 to 5.5 feet) can be tight for adults; many people sleep diagonally or with the tailgate down and an extension. Always measure your own bed.

Is a truck bed tent or a cap better?

A bed tent is cheaper and comes off when you don't need it, but takes time to set up and stays exposed to wind. A cap is weatherproof, lockable and ready instantly, and pairs well with a sleeping platform, but costs more and stays on the truck.

How do you stop condensation when sleeping in a truck cap?

Ventilate. Open the cap's side windows or a roof vent on opposite ends, ideally with bug screens, and keep wet gear outside. Insulating the cap and using a wool blanket or moisture-absorbing pad under the mattress also helps.

Is it safe to run a heater while sleeping in a truck?

Don't run a fuel-burning heater, stove or the engine in an enclosed sleeping space. Carbon monoxide can build to deadly levels without warning. Use a warmer sleeping bag, an insulated pad and a battery-powered CO alarm instead.

Where can you legally truck camp?

Dispersed camping on many national forest and BLM lands is generally allowed, often with a 14-day stay limit, though rules vary by area. Established campgrounds welcome trucks. Rest areas, store parking lots and city streets each have their own rules, and many prohibit overnight sleeping.
