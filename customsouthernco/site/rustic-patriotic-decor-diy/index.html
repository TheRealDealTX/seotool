Rustic Patriotic Decor DIY: A Barn Wood Flag Sign

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · August 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Weathered boards, a little paint and a star stencil make a rustic flag sign that looks right from Memorial Day through Labor Day.

Come late May, we start itching to pull out the red, white and blue. A barn wood flag sign is our go-to piece of rustic patriotic decor because it looks at home on a farmhouse porch, leaned on a mantel, or hung over a buffet for a summer cookout. It's built from weathered boards, so it already has that sun-faded, been-here-forever look before you even pick up a brush.

This project is friendly for beginners. There's no fancy joinery, just boards held together with two cleats on the back, a bit of dry-brushed paint and a star stencil. If you can drive a screw and tape a straight line, you can make one this weekend.

Gather your boards and paint, then follow the supplies list and steps below.

How to make it

  1. Prep the boards

    Brush off loose dirt and splinters with a wire brush, sand rough spots just enough to handle safely, and let damp wood dry completely before painting.

  2. Assemble the panel

    Lay the boards face down side by side, then screw or nail two cleats across the back about 3 inches from each end so the panel holds together as one piece.

  3. Mark the field

    Turn the panel over and tape off the upper left corner for the blue field, roughly two-fifths of the width and a little more than half the height.

  4. Paint the stripes

    Alternate red and cream boards below and beside the field, using thin, dry-brushed coats so the wood grain shows through. Let each color dry before the next.

  5. Paint the blue field

    Fill the taped corner with navy in two thin coats and peel the tape while the last coat is still slightly tacky for a crisp edge.

  6. Add the stars

    Stencil or trace stars in cream on the dry blue field using a nearly dry brush and an up-and-down dabbing motion to avoid bleeding.

  7. Distress and seal

    Lightly sand edges and high spots to let some raw wood peek through, wipe away dust, and add a clear matte sealer if the sign will live on a covered porch.

  8. Hang it up

    Attach a sawtooth hanger or D-rings to the cleats, or simply lean the sign against a wall, porch rail or mantel.

Finding the Right Wood for Rustic Patriotic Decor

The character of the wood is what makes this sign feel rustic rather than store-bought. Good sources include:

  • Old fencing. Weathered cedar or pine pickets are thin, light and already gray.
  • Barn siding and trim. Ask farmers or folks tearing down an outbuilding; scraps are often free for the hauling. Our post on barn wood Christmas trees has more tips on gathering and cleaning old boards.
  • Pallets. Look for pallets stamped HT (heat-treated). Avoid ones marked MB, which indicates methyl bromide fumigation, and skip any with oily stains or strange odors.
  • Salvage yards and flea markets. Bundles of lath and old shelving boards turn up regularly and are usually inexpensive.

Try to choose boards that are close in thickness so the front of your sign sits fairly flat. Different widths are fine and actually add charm; the stripes don't have to match a real flag's proportions exactly.

A Word About Old Paint and Safety

Old barn wood is wonderful, but it can carry a few hazards. Wood painted before 1978 may have lead-based paint, and that's especially common on old trim, porches and outbuildings. If boards have old painted surfaces, test them with a lead test kit from the hardware store before sanding. If the test is positive, don't sand or scrape; either pick different boards or seal the surface rather than disturbing it.

Before painting, give any salvaged board a good look for bugs. Small round exit holes with fresh, powdery sawdust can be a sign of active wood-boring insects. Old holes are just character, but if you see fresh dust, leave the board outdoors or pass on it so you don't bring anything into the house. Letting boards sit in the sun on a dry day for a while also helps make sure they're thoroughly dry before you seal them, since paint applied over damp wood tends to peel.

Whatever wood you use, wear a dust mask and safety glasses when brushing and sanding, work outdoors if you can, and watch for rusty nails and staples. Pull them or hammer them flush, and make sure your tetanus shot is current if you do a lot of salvage work. Wear gloves to keep splinters out of your hands.

Tip: If your boards have rusty nail heads you love the look of, leave them in, but wire-brush off any flaking rust and seal over them so they won't stain your wall.

Design and Styling Ideas

The beauty of a rustic flag is that it doesn't need to be perfect. A few ways to give yours personality:

  • Muted colors. Barn red, antique cream and a deep navy look softer and more vintage than bright primary colors. Dry-brushing lets the grain show through.
  • Fewer stars. A single large star or a loose cluster of five or seven reads as folk art and is much easier than fifty.
  • Distressed edges. A light sanding on the edges and raised grain makes the sign look aged. Our guide on how to distress furniture covers the same technique in more detail.
  • Stained instead of painted. Use thinned red and blue stains for a more transparent, weathered effect.

For styling, lean the finished sign behind a galvanized bucket of hydrangeas or zinnias on the porch, prop it on a mantel with mason jars of small flags, or hang it above a buffet for a Fourth of July gathering. If you're dressing the whole front entry, our front porch decor ideas post has plenty of pairing ideas.

Variations on the Barn Wood Flag

  • Vertical flag. Run the boards up and down and put the blue field at the top for a tall, narrow sign that fits beside a door.
  • Mini tabletop flags. Use paint stirrers or lath glued to a small cleat for flags that fit in a tiered tray.
  • Star-only sign. Cut a large wooden star from scrap plywood, paint it barn red and hang it on a rustic board.
  • Bunting. Pair the sign with fabric bunting made from old feed sacks or drop cloth with red ticking stripes.
  • Yardstick flag. Old wooden yardsticks make great stripes, too. See our yardstick decor project for that approach.

Care for Indoor and Porch Displays

Indoors, a sign like this needs nothing more than a dusting now and then. On a covered porch, a coat of clear matte exterior sealer will help the paint hold up to humidity, but keep it out of direct rain and full afternoon sun, which will fade and crack the paint over time. A Southern summer is hard on anything left outdoors, so bring it in after the holiday season.

If you hang the sign, use anchors rated for its weight; barn wood is heavier than it looks. Leaning signs should be set where they won't tip onto kids or pets. Want more seasonal ideas? Our DIY project finder can point you to another weekend project.

Frequently asked questions

Is old barn wood safe to use indoors?

Usually, yes, once it's cleaned and dried, but painted boards may contain lead if the paint is older than 1978. Test painted surfaces with a hardware-store lead test kit and avoid sanding anything that tests positive.

Which pallets are safe for DIY projects?

Look for pallets stamped HT, which means heat-treated. Avoid pallets marked MB for methyl bromide, and skip any with chemical stains, spills or unusual odors.

What paint gives the most rustic look?

Chalk-style or flat acrylic paint applied with a nearly dry brush lets the wood grain show through. Muted barn red, antique white and navy look more vintage than bright primaries.

Can I leave a barn wood flag sign outside?

A covered porch is fine if you seal it with a clear matte exterior sealer. Full exposure to rain and sun will fade the paint and weather the wood quickly, so bring it indoors when the season is over.
