Easy Ball Jar Decor DIY: A Farmhouse Jar Centerpiece

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read

A rustic wooden caddy and a few old Ball jars make a farmhouse centerpiece you can restyle with flowers, candles or cotton every season.

Few things say farmhouse like a row of old Ball jars. Whether they're the soft aqua blue jars that collectors love or the plain clear ones from grandma's canning shelf, they bring an easy, homegrown charm to any room. This Ball jar decor project is a simple wooden caddy that holds three or four jars in a row. It makes a great centerpiece for a long table, a kitchen island or a front porch, and you can change what's in the jars with every season.

The caddy is basic woodworking, just a base, two ends and a handle. If you'd rather skip the building, a vintage wooden tool tote or a soda crate from the flea market works the same way.

Gather your jars and a few scraps of wood and follow the steps below.

How to make it

  1. Measure your jars

    Line your jars up side by side and measure their total width plus about an inch, which sets the length of the base board. Ends should be about two-thirds the height of the jars.

  2. Cut the pieces

    Cut the base and two end pieces. If you like, round the top corners of the ends or trace a curve with a coffee can for a softer look.

  3. Drill for the handle

    Drill a hole near the top center of each end piece, about halfway through, sized to fit your dowel or broom handle.

  4. Assemble the caddy

    Glue and nail the end pieces to the base, slipping the dowel into the holes before the second end goes on. Check that it's square and let the glue set.

  5. Sand and finish

    Sand the edges smooth, then stain or paint the caddy. Lightly sand the edges after painting for a worn finish.

  6. Clean and fill the jars

    Wash the jars, dry them well, and fill them with water and flowers, cotton stems, greenery or battery candles.

  7. Set the table

    Place the jars in the caddy, wrap a little twine around the jar necks if you like, and set it in the center of your table.

Finding the Right Jars for Ball Jar Decor

Old jars are everywhere if you know where to look: estate sales, yard sales, flea markets, and the dusty shelves of farm sales. Thrift stores often have a few, and relatives with canning histories may have boxes in the barn. Clear jars are the easiest to find and are usually inexpensive. The aqua and blue jars typically cost more and vary widely based on age, embossing and condition.

A few things to look for:

  • Color. Ball's well-known blue jars were made for a long stretch of time, and that soft blue glass is a favorite for decor.
  • The logo. The style of the Ball script on the front changed over the years and is one of the main clues to a jar's age.
  • Bubbles and wavy glass. Small seeds or bubbles in the glass are a nice sign of older manufacturing.
  • No cracks or chips. Run a finger carefully around the rim and check the base; damaged jars can break when filled with water.

Cleaning Your Jars

Old jars often come home dusty or cloudy. Soak them in warm soapy water, scrub with a bottle brush, and rinse well. Avoid sudden temperature changes, like pouring boiling water into a cold jar, which can crack old glass. Cloudiness that won't come off may be etching in the glass itself, which is permanent but still pretty in decor. Our post on how to clean old jars covers stubborn residue and rust rings in more detail.

Tip: Leave the original zinc lids off when you fill jars with water. They rust quickly. Display the lids beside the caddy instead, or set them upside down under small plants.

Styling Ideas for Every Season

The best thing about a jar caddy is how easy it is to change. Some ideas:

  • Spring: Tulips, daffodils or lilac branches in blue jars.
  • Summer: Zinnias, sunflowers and hydrangeas, or small flags for the Fourth of July.
  • Fall: Cotton stems, wheat, dried leaves and mini gourds tucked around the base.
  • Winter: Evergreen sprigs, pinecones and battery-operated candles with a sprinkle of faux snow.

When arranging flowers in jars, keep the stems roughly one and a half to two times the height of the jar so the bouquet looks full but not top-heavy. Mixing jar sizes in the caddy, such as two quarts with a pint in the middle, adds rhythm. Blue jars glow beautifully with white, yellow and soft pink blooms, while clear jars show off the stems themselves, so trim off any leaves that would sit below the waterline to keep the water clear longer.

Add a little texture with jute twine or a strip of burlap tied around each jar neck. If you're setting a long table, run two caddies end to end with a few loose jars in between at different heights.

Variations to Try

  • Hanging lanterns. Twist galvanized wire around the jar neck to form a handle and hang jars with battery lights from porch hooks.
  • Wall sconce. Attach pint jars to a barn wood board with pipe clamps and fill them with stems.
  • Soap dispenser. Fit a pint jar with a pump lid for the kitchen or bath.
  • Pantry display. Fill clear jars with dried beans, pasta or oats and line them up on an open shelf.
  • Candle jars. Fill with sand and a pillar candle, following the safety notes below.

Care and Safety Notes

For the caddy itself, a coat of paste wax or clear sealer over the stain or paint protects it from the inevitable water drips when you change flowers. Set a felt pad under each corner so it won't scratch your table, and wipe up spills promptly so the wood doesn't swell or water-spot.

Old glass can be fragile. Handle jars carefully, never set them on a hot stove, and avoid pouring very hot or very cold liquids into them. A chipped or cracked jar should be retired from water duty. If a jar breaks, sweep the pieces up with care and wear gloves.

If you use real candles, place them so the flame sits well below the rim and has plenty of space around it, keep them away from dried flowers and greenery, and never leave a lit candle unattended. Old glass can crack from heat, so battery-operated candles are the safer choice for vintage jars, especially the collectible ones. When the caddy isn't in use, empty the water and dry the jars to prevent mineral rings. If you're hunting for jars, our blog has plenty of picking tips to help you find them.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell how old my Ball jar is?

The style of the Ball logo is one of the best clues, along with the glass color and any embossing. Our Ball jar age finder tool and dating guide walk through the logo changes so you can estimate a range.

Are blue Ball jars worth more than clear ones?

Blue jars typically sell for more than common clear ones, but value varies a lot with age, embossing, size and condition. Most blue jars are fairly common; unusual variations and excellent condition bring higher prices.

Is it safe to put candles in old Ball jars?

Old glass can crack from heat, so battery-operated candles are the safer choice. If you use a real candle, keep the flame well below the rim, away from dried decor, and never leave it unattended.

How do I remove cloudiness from old jars?

Soak in warm soapy water and scrub with a bottle brush; a soak in white vinegar can help with mineral film. If cloudiness remains, the glass may be etched, which is permanent.
