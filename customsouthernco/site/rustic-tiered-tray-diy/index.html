DIY Tiered Tray: Build a Rustic Two-Tier Stand from Thrift Finds

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · September 26, 2026 · 5 min read

Build a rustic two-tier tray from thrift-store boards, plates, or tins, then style it for every season of the year.

A DIY tiered tray is one of the most useful pieces of decor in a farmhouse kitchen. It gathers small seasonal things into one tidy display, gives a coffee table some height, and makes swapping decor every few months simple. Store-bought trays are nice, but building one from thrifted boards, plates, or tins gives you something with real age and a lot more personality.

This version uses two round wooden tiers, a center post, and a simple threaded rod to hold it all together. It is sturdier than a glued tray, and you can take it apart for storage.

Time1-2 hours (plus paint drying)
DifficultyModerate
Cost$10–$30

How to make it

  1. Gather and dry-fit your pieces

    Stack your larger tier, center post, smaller tier, and handle to check proportions. A good rule of thumb is a top tier about two-thirds the diameter of the bottom.

  2. Mark the centers

    Find the center of each tier by drawing two lines across the diameter at right angles and marking where they cross. Mark the center of each end of your post as well.

  3. Drill the holes

    Drill straight through the center of each tier and lengthwise into the post ends, sized for your rod or kit hardware. Use a scrap board underneath to prevent tear-out.

  4. Sand and finish

    Sand away rough spots, then paint, stain, or leave the wood natural. Let everything dry fully and add a coat of clear matte sealer.

  5. Assemble with hardware

    Thread the rod through the bottom tier with a washer and nut underneath, add the post, the top tier, and finally the knob. Tighten until snug and the tray does not wobble.

  6. Add felt and test

    Stick felt pads under the bottom tier, set it on a flat surface, and gently press each side to check stability before styling.

Finding the Pieces for Your DIY Tiered Tray

Half the fun is the hunt. Thrift stores, yard sales, and flea markets are full of potential tiers and posts. Our antique picking guide has more on where to look.

  • Tiers. Round cutting boards, bread boards, lazy Susans, wooden plates, enamelware plates, galvanized pie tins, and old cake pans all work. See our cutting board decor post for tips on choosing boards.
  • Center posts. Stair spindles, table legs, wooden candlesticks, and thick dowels. Spindles are a favorite for their turned shapes; our spindle snowman shows another use for them.
  • Handles. Old drawer pulls, wooden knobs, finials, or a glass door knob.

Tiered tray hardware kits are sold at craft stores and online and take much of the guesswork out of assembly. A length of 1/4-inch threaded rod from the hardware store, with washers and nuts, works just as well and costs very little.

Getting the Proportions Right

The difference between a tray that looks charming and one that looks a little off usually comes down to proportion. Aim for a top tier roughly two-thirds the diameter of the bottom tier; for example, a 12-inch bottom with an 8-inch top. The space between tiers should be tall enough for your decor to stand up comfortably, usually around 6 to 9 inches. A taller post looks elegant but holds less and tips more easily, while a short one can feel squat. If you plan to use small jars and bottle-brush trees, measure the tallest piece you want on the bottom tier and add an inch or two of breathing room.

The handle on top should be easy to grab with a few fingers. A knob around 1 to 1-1/2 inches across is comfortable, and a vintage porcelain or glass knob adds a nice finishing touch.

Rustic Variations

Galvanized tray

Use two galvanized pie tins or round baking pans with a candlestick in between. Drill metal with a sharp bit, starting with a small pilot hole, and file any sharp edges smooth. Wear gloves and safety glasses, and skip any tin with rust that has eaten through. Our guide to removing rust from vintage metal can help clean up light surface rust.

Three-tier tray

Add a third, smallest tier with a second short post. Keep the bottom tier heavy and wide so it does not tip.

No-drill tray

For a lightweight, decor-only tray, glue a candlestick between two boards with a strong craft adhesive. It will not be as sturdy, so keep it to light items and do not lift it by the top.

Enamelware and china

Mismatched vintage plates make a sweet cottage-style stand. Drilling ceramic requires a diamond or tile bit, steady water cooling, and patience; it is easy to crack a plate, so practice on one you do not love first.

Tip: Paint the center post a contrasting color, like barn red or black, and leave the tiers natural. It makes even a simple tray look intentional.

Styling Your Tiered Tray Through the Seasons

The trick to a tiered tray that looks full but not cluttered is variety in height and texture. Put one taller item on each tier, add a couple of medium pieces, and fill in with small things like greenery or berries.

  • Spring. A small potted herb, speckled eggs in a bowl, a mini cloche, and a bundle of faux tulips.
  • Summer. Mini mason jars with wildflowers, a small flag, lemons, and a seashell or two.
  • Fall. Tiny pumpkins, a funnel pumpkin, cinnamon sticks, and a battery candle.
  • Christmas. Bottle-brush trees, a vintage ornament or two, a jar of peppermints, and a sprig of cedar.
  • Valentine's Day. A stack of old love letters tied with twine, a small vase of red carnations, and a heart cut from tea-stained paper.
  • Everyday. Coffee pods, sugar, and a small plant on a coffee bar, or salt, pepper, and olive oil next to the stove.

Food Safety and Care

If you want to serve food on your tray, use unpainted wood finished with food-grade mineral oil, or set food on parchment or a plate rather than directly on painted, sealed, or old metal surfaces. Vintage painted pieces may contain lead paint, and some old metal tins and enamelware are best kept as decor only. When in doubt, treat it as decor.

Wipe wooden tiers with a barely damp cloth and dry them right away. Check the nut underneath from time to time and snug it up if the tray starts to wobble. If you use real candles, keep them on the top tier away from greenery and paper, and never leave a flame unattended. Flameless candles are the easiest choice for a busy tray.

When the season is over, it takes only a minute to unscrew the knob and lift the tiers apart, so your tray stores flat in a closet alongside your other holiday decor. A good tray earns its spot year after year, and every season gives you a fresh excuse to go picking for one more little treasure to set on it.

Frequently asked questions

What can I use as tiers for a DIY tiered tray?

Round cutting boards, wooden plates, lazy Susans, galvanized pie tins, cake pans, and vintage plates all work. Choose a top tier about two-thirds the size of the bottom tier for good balance.

Do I need a hardware kit to make a tiered tray?

No. A kit makes assembly easy, but a length of 1/4-inch threaded rod with washers and nuts from the hardware store works just as well. For a lightweight decor-only tray, strong craft glue can work too.

Can I put food on a homemade tiered tray?

Only on surfaces that are food-safe, such as unpainted wood finished with food-grade mineral oil. Painted, sealed, or old metal surfaces should not touch food directly, so use parchment or a plate.

How do I keep my tiered tray from wobbling?

Make sure the holes are drilled straight through the centers, use a wide, heavy bottom tier, and tighten the nut underneath until snug. Felt pads on the bottom also help it sit level.
