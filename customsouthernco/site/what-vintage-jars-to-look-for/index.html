Vintage Jars to Look For: A Collector's Guide

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · September 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Not every old canning jar is a treasure, but some are. Here are the vintage jars to look for, the colors and marks that matter, and how to spot fakes.

Old canning jars are one of the most common finds at estate sales, barn sales, and flea markets. Most were made in huge numbers and are worth a few dollars, but every so often a jar turns up with an unusual color, closure, or embossing that makes collectors sit up straight. If you'd like to know which vintage jars to look for, this guide covers the major makers, the colors and features that matter, what drives value, and how to spot a reproduction.

Why Collect Vintage Canning Jars?

Canning jars are affordable, beautiful, and full of history. They tell the story of how families preserved food before refrigeration and supermarkets, and the glass itself catches light in a way modern jars just don't. They're also wonderfully useful for decorating, storing pantry staples, holding flowers, and organizing craft supplies. And since so many survive, it's easy to start a collection without spending much.

Vintage Jars to Look For, Maker by Maker

Mason's Patent Nov 30th 1858

John Landis Mason patented his threaded-shoulder jar in 1858, and that patent date was embossed on jars made by many different glass companies for decades afterward. A jar reading "Mason's Patent Nov 30th 1858" was very likely not made in 1858. Most date to later in the 1800s or early 1900s. These jars are common, but variations in color, maker's marks on the base, and unusual embossing can make some more desirable.

Ball Jars

The Ball brothers began making jars in the 1880s and moved their operation to Muncie, Indiana, by the late 1880s. Ball became one of the biggest jar makers in the country, which means Ball jars are the ones you'll find most often. Some to watch for:

  • Ball Perfect Mason jars, made in large numbers in the early twentieth century, often in aqua or blue glass.
  • Ball Improved jars, designed to use a glass insert under a metal band.
  • Ball Ideal jars with wire bail closures and glass lids, made for decades.
  • Ball Special and Ball Sure Seal jars, which add variety to a collection.
  • Older Ball logos, including block letters and earlier script styles, which help date jars.

The Ball logo changed several times over the years, and those changes are one of the best tools for estimating age. Use our helper below to narrow down your jar's age by its logo.

Atlas Jars

Atlas jars were made by Hazel-Atlas Glass Company, formed in the early 1900s. Look for Atlas Strong Shoulder Mason jars, Atlas E-Z Seal jars with wire bail closures, and Atlas Special Mason jars. Many came in clear and aqua glass, and some colors are harder to find.

Kerr Jars

Kerr is known for helping popularize the two-piece lid system, with a flat metal lid and a screw band, in the early twentieth century. Kerr Self Sealing Mason jars and Kerr Economy jars are common finds. Older Kerr jars with unusual embossing or colors may interest collectors.

Lightning Jars

Lightning-style jars use a glass lid held down by a wire bail and lever, a closure that dates back to the 1880s. Original Lightning jars and similar bail-type jars from various makers are popular with collectors and look lovely on a pantry shelf.

Other Names to Know

Keep an eye out for jars marked Drey, Root, Crown, Globe, Woodbury, Hero, and other names, along with jars made by smaller regional glass houses. Some of these are scarce and can be quite collectible, especially in unusual colors.

Colors That Collectors Love

Color is one of the biggest factors in a jar's appeal and value.

  • Clear. Most common, especially on later jars. Usually the least valuable.
  • Aqua. The pale blue-green color of many older jars, caused by natural iron in the sand used to make glass. Very common on older jars and widely loved for decorating.
  • Ball blue. Ball's distinctive light blue glass, associated with older Ball jars. Very popular with decorators.
  • Green, olive, and emerald. Less common and often more desirable.
  • Amber. Uncommon on many jar types and sought after by collectors.
  • Cobalt and other deep blues. Rare on original canning jars. Be cautious, because many cobalt jars are modern.
  • Sun-colored amethyst. Some older glass contains manganese, added to make glass clear, which turns a soft purple after long exposure to sunlight. Natural amethyst coloring is a sign of age, though some glass has been artificially irradiated to create the color.

Features That Make a Jar Special

  • Unusual sizes. Very small "midget" pint jars and very large half-gallon jars can be more desirable than standard quarts.
  • Embossing errors. Misspellings, backward letters, and double strikes interest serious collectors.
  • Original closures. Jars with their original glass lids, wire bails, or period zinc lids are more complete and appealing.
  • Bubbles and imperfections. Seed bubbles, whittle marks, and slightly uneven glass suggest older production methods.
  • Base markings. Numbers, letters, and symbols on the base can indicate the mold or factory.
  • Ground lips. Some older jars have a rough, ground rim from older manufacturing methods.

What Affects Vintage Jar Value?

Most vintage canning jars are common and typically sell for a few dollars to around $20, especially clear and aqua jars from the major makers. Uncommon colors, rare makers, unusual sizes, early closures, and embossing errors can push values much higher, sometimes into the hundreds for truly rare pieces. As with anything, condition drives value:

  • Chips and cracks on the rim or body lower value significantly.
  • Cloudiness, often called "sick glass," is caused by etching from the contents or minerals and can lower value. It usually can't be cleaned away at home.
  • Bright, clean glass with crisp embossing is most desirable.
  • Original lids and closures add appeal.

Prices change with trends and regional demand, so compare similar jars in similar condition before buying or selling.

How to Spot Reproduction Jars

Because old jars are popular, plenty of reproductions and modern decorative jars are out there. There's nothing wrong with modern jars, but if you're paying a collector's price, take a close look.

  • Modern colored jars. In recent years, jar makers have released colored jars in shades like blue, green, and purple. These are modern and widely available. Check the base and side markings for modern details.
  • Too-perfect glass. Uniform thickness, no bubbles, and very crisp seams suggest modern production.
  • Odd weight. Some reproductions are lighter or heavier than originals.
  • Logo details. Compare the logo to known originals. Small differences in lettering style can be telling.
  • Fake aging. Artificial dirt, staining, or rust on lids can be used to make a jar look old.

For more ways to tell old from new, read our guide on how to spot reproduction antiques.

Where to Find Vintage Jars

  • Estate sales and farm sales, especially in basements, pantries, root cellars, and barns.
  • Flea markets and antique malls, where you'll often find jars in bulk.
  • Yard sales and church sales, which sometimes have whole boxes of jars for a few dollars.
  • Online marketplaces, which are good for specific jars but require careful attention to condition and shipping.
Tip: Bring a small flashlight to check for cracks and chips, and run your finger gently around the rim before buying. A small chip on the rim is easy to miss in a dim barn.

Using and Displaying Your Jars

Vintage jars look beautiful grouped by color on a windowsill, lined up on open kitchen shelves, or filled with dried beans, buttons, or fresh flowers. Many people love using them for pantry storage and decor. For canning, though, we stick to modern jars and new lids, since old jars may have hidden weaknesses and old closures aren't designed for today's recommended methods.

Before displaying a dusty find, give it a gentle cleaning with our guide on how to clean old jars. Happy jar hunting!

Frequently asked questions

What vintage canning jars are worth money?

Jars in unusual colors like amber, green, or cobalt, jars from rare makers, unusual sizes, early closures, and jars with embossing errors tend to be most valuable. Common clear and aqua jars from major makers typically sell for a few dollars to around $20.

Is a jar marked Mason's Patent Nov 30th 1858 from 1858?

Almost certainly not. That patent date was embossed on jars made by many companies for decades afterward, so most of these jars date to the later 1800s or early 1900s.

How can I tell how old my Ball jar is?

The Ball logo changed several times over the years, and the logo style is one of the best ways to estimate age. Our Ball jar age finder walks you through the logo styles to narrow down a date range.

Are colored Ball jars rare?

Original older Ball jars in their typical light blue or aqua glass are common. Truly unusual colors are scarce, but many modern colored jars have been released in recent years, so check carefully for modern markings before paying a premium.

Can I use vintage jars for canning?

We recommend modern jars and new lids for canning. Older jars may have hidden flaws, and older closures aren't designed for current recommended canning methods. Vintage jars are great for dry storage and decor.
