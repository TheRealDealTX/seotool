How to Date Ball Mason Jars by the Logo, Color and Clues

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · August 6, 2026 · 7 min read

That script logo on your Ball jar is a built-in timeline. Here's how to read it, plus the color, seam and lid clues that narrow down its age.

If you've ever turned a pretty blue canning jar over in your hands at a flea market and wondered just how old it is, you're in good company. Learning how to date Ball Mason jars is one of the easiest ways to get started in collecting, because Ball changed its logo several times over the decades, and those changes act like a rough calendar embossed right into the glass. Add in a few other clues, such as glass color, mold seams and the style of lid, and you can usually place a jar within a window of ten to thirty years.

We'll walk through the logo eras first, then the supporting clues, and finish with the common myths that trip people up. One honest note before we start: the dates below are widely used collector guidelines, not hard factory records. Ball ran many molds at once, molds stayed in service for years, and logo styles overlapped, so treat every range as approximate.

A Quick Bit of Ball History

The Ball brothers got their start in Buffalo, New York, in the 1880s making containers, and by the late 1880s they had moved their glass operations to Muncie, Indiana, where natural gas made glassmaking cheaper. Home canning was booming, and the "Mason" style jar, with its threaded neck and screw-on lid, had been patented by John Landis Mason back in 1858. Once Mason's patent protections lapsed, many glassworks made Mason-style jars, and Ball became the best-known name of them all.

Because Ball produced enormous numbers of jars for well over a century, most Ball jars you'll find are common. That's good news for decorators and new collectors: you can learn on affordable jars and still keep an eye out for the scarce ones.

How to Date Ball Mason Jars by the Logo

The logo is the single most useful clue. Hold the jar up to the light and look closely at the word "Ball" on the front. Here are the commonly cited eras, from oldest to newest:

Logo styleWhat to look forApproximate era
Block letters"BALL" in plain capital letters, sometimes with "Mason" or "Improved"Late 1880s to 1890s
Triple-L ("3-L") scriptScript "Ball" where the underline loops back so it looks like a third LRoughly 1890s to about 1910
Dropped-A scriptThe lowercase "a" sits lower than the other letters and connects to the underlineAbout 1910 to 1923
Script with underscoreNeat script "Ball" with a separate underline beneath the wordAbout 1923 to 1933
Script, no underscoreSame general script, but the underline is goneAbout 1933 to around 1960
Modern "Ball"Cleaner, more uniform script, often with modern wording and markingsAfter about 1960

A few notes on reading these. The block-letter jars are the oldest and are generally the least common of the Ball jars you'll see in the wild. The triple-L and dropped-A jars are what many folks picture when they think "old blue Ball jar." The underscore and no-underscore jars are very common and were made in huge quantities through the Depression and World War II years, when home canning was a household necessity.

Tip: Run your fingertip over the logo while you look at it. Worn or faintly embossed logos can be easier to feel than to see, especially on aqua glass in dim light.

Glass Color Clues

Color won't give you an exact year, but it helps confirm what the logo is telling you.

  • Aqua and "Ball blue": The soft blue-green glass most people love was standard for many decades. Most blue Ball jars date from roughly the 1900s through the 1930s, with production of blue jars tapering off as clear glass took over.
  • Clear (flint) glass: Clear jars appear early in small numbers and become the norm from around the 1930s onward. A clear jar with a no-underscore logo is about as common as old jars get.
  • Amber, green and other colors: Older amber, olive and deep green Ball jars exist but are much scarcer, and they are worth a closer look. Be careful, though: modern colored jars are also out there (more on that below).
  • Sun-colored amethyst: Some older clear glass contained manganese, which turns a light lavender after years in the sun. That tint suggests age, but it's a clue about the glass formula, not proof of a specific date.

Seams, Bubbles and Other Manufacturing Clues

How a jar was made leaves fingerprints. Early jars were often made with semi-automatic or hand-finished methods, which can leave a lip that looks slightly uneven or a seam that stops below the rim. By the early 1900s, Ball and other makers were shifting to fully automatic machines, and on those jars the mold seam typically runs all the way up the side and through the threads to the lip.

Other details to note:

  • Bubbles and ripples: Small seed bubbles and a wavy, rippled surface are more common on older glass. They're charming, but they aren't a dating tool on their own.
  • Base markings: Numbers and letters on the bottom are usually mold or plant identifiers. They are not years. A jar with a big "8" on the bottom was not made in 1908.
  • Wording: "Perfect Mason," "Improved," "Special," "Sure Seal," "Ideal" and "Eclipse" are Ball product lines. Each line ran for a stretch of years, so the wording plus the logo narrows things down better than either alone.

Using Lids and Closures to Narrow the Date

The lid is a helpful clue, with one big caveat: lids get swapped constantly. A jar from 1915 might be wearing a lid from 1975. Use closures to support your read of the glass, not to override it.

  • Zinc caps with milk-glass liners: Common from the late 1800s into the mid-1900s. The white or opal glass insert inside the cap is a nice detail to keep with the jar.
  • Glass lids with wire bails: "Lightning-style" closures like those on Ball Ideal jars clamp a glass lid down with a wire. These ran for decades in the 20th century.
  • Two-piece lids: The flat lid plus screw band most of us use today became widespread in the 20th century and is not, by itself, a sign of age.

Myths That Lead People Astray

"The patent date is the year it was made"

Many Mason-type jars carry an embossed patent date from 1858. That date refers to Mason's patent, not the jar's manufacture. Jars bearing it were produced for many years afterward, so it tells you almost nothing about a specific jar's age.

"Every blue jar is valuable"

Blue Ball jars were made by the millions. Most typically sell for modest amounts, and condition, size, color shade and unusual embossing are what push value up. A chipped rim or heavy haze will pull value down quickly.

"If it looks old, it is old"

Ball and other companies have released colored commemorative and heritage-style jars in recent decades, and some decorative jars are made to look antique. Modern colored jars tend to have very uniform color, crisp sharp embossing and modern markings. A Simple Jar-Dating Checklist

  1. Identify the logo style first and note its approximate era.
  2. Check the glass color and see whether it fits that era.
  3. Look for the mold seam and whether it runs through the lip.
  4. Read any product-line wording and note base markings (as mold numbers, not dates).
  5. Consider the lid last, remembering it may not be original.
  6. Look over condition: chips, cracks, haze and stains all affect value.

Caring for the Jars You Find

Once you've dated a jar, you'll probably want to clean it up. Go gently: avoid sudden temperature changes, skip the dishwasher for older glass, and never use harsh abrasives on the embossing. Our step-by-step guide on how to clean old jars covers soaks, haze and rust-stained rims. Keep in mind that old jars are best treated as display pieces. For actual home canning, current canning guidance recommends modern jars and new lids, since older glass may have hidden stress or tiny chips that compromise a seal.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell how old my Ball jar is?

Start with the logo on the front. Block letters, the triple-L script, the dropped-A script, the script with an underscore and the script without one each line up with a rough era. Then check glass color, the mold seam and any product wording to confirm the estimate.

Does the 1858 date on my jar mean it was made in 1858?

No. The 1858 date refers to John Landis Mason's patent for the threaded jar. Jars carrying that embossing were made for many years afterward, so it doesn't date the individual jar.

What do the numbers on the bottom of a Ball jar mean?

Numbers and letters on the base are usually mold or plant identifiers used in production. They are not the year the jar was made, so a number like 8 or 12 shouldn't be read as a date.

Are blue Ball jars worth money?

Most blue Ball jars are common and typically sell for modest amounts. Scarce colors, unusual embossing, early logos, odd sizes and excellent condition can raise value, while chips, cracks and haze lower it.

Can I still can food in an old Ball jar?

Old jars are best kept for display, dry storage or decor. Current home-canning guidance recommends modern jars and new lids, because older glass can have hidden stress, tiny chips or worn rims that prevent a reliable seal.
