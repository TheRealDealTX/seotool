DIY Spindle Snowman Ornament From Old Stair Balusters

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · October 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Give an old stair baluster or chair spindle new life as a cheerful little snowman for your tree, mantel, or gift basket.

A spindle snowman is one of our favorite winter projects because it turns a castoff piece of wood, like an old stair baluster, a broken chair spindle, or a leftover table leg, into a cheerful little character with real personality. The turned curves on a spindle already look like a snowman's stacked body, so half the work is done before you ever pick up a brush.

These make sweet ornaments for the tree, shelf sitters for a mantel, or package toppers for neighbor gifts. One salvaged baluster can easily yield three or four snowmen, which makes this a budget-friendly way to decorate for the whole winter, not just December.

Time2 hours plus drying time
DifficultyEasy
Cost$5–$15

How to make it

  1. Test and prep the spindle

    If the spindle has old paint, test it for lead before doing anything else. Pull any nails or staples and wipe the wood clean.

  2. Mark and cut segments

    Look for the beads and curves that suggest a head and body, then mark cuts 4 to 7 inches apart. Cut straight so each snowman stands level.

  3. Sand lightly

    Smooth cut ends and splinters with 120-grit, then 220-grit sandpaper. Skip sanding on any surface that tested positive for lead.

  4. Paint the body white

    Apply two coats of white paint, letting each coat dry. Leave the top inch or so unpainted if a hat will cover it.

  5. Add the face and buttons

    Dot eyes and buttons with the end of a pencil or brush handle dipped in black paint. Paint a small orange carrot nose with a fine brush.

  6. Dress your snowman

    Cut the cuff off a child's sock, tie or glue the top closed, and slip it on as a hat. Tie a thin strip of flannel around the neck for a scarf.

  7. Add arms and a hanger

    Drill small holes and glue in twig arms if you like. For an ornament, twist a screw eye into the top and loop on twine.

Finding the Right Spindles for a Spindle Snowman

The best spindles have a few distinct "beads" or rounded sections, because those become the snowman's head and middle. Here's where we like to look:

  • Architectural salvage shops and building reuse stores. Balusters from old staircases and porches often turn up in bundles for very little.
  • Broken chairs. A wobbly chair at a yard sale or curbside can give you a set of matching spindles. Chair rungs work too, though they're usually slimmer.
  • Estate and barn sales. Look in garages and outbuildings, where leftover lumber and furniture parts get stashed. Our antique picking tips can help you spot the good stuff.
  • Craft and hardware stores. If you can't find salvage, new unfinished spindles and table legs work beautifully and skip the lead paint question altogether.

Choose wood that's solid and dry. Avoid pieces with soft, crumbly spots, deep rot, or signs of active insects such as fresh sawdust or tiny exit holes.

Lead Paint and Old Wood: Read This First

Many homes built before 1978 have lead-based paint somewhere, and old balusters and porch spindles are common culprits. Before you cut or sand a painted spindle, use an inexpensive lead test swab from the hardware store. If it tests positive, don't sand, scrape, or cut it indoors; the dust is the real hazard. The simplest choice is to set that piece aside and use bare or new wood instead.

Even with unpainted wood, wear safety glasses when cutting, keep fingers well clear of the blade, and clamp small pieces rather than holding them by hand. Short segments are tricky to cut safely, so cut your longest pieces first and finish the small ones while you still have something to hold onto.

Choosing Paint and Finishes

Chalk-style paint is our go-to for this project because it grips raw wood without primer, dries fast, and has a soft, matte look that reads like fresh snow. Acrylic craft paint works well too, though bright white craft paints can take an extra coat to cover darker woods. If your spindle has an old shiny varnish, scuff it with sandpaper first so the new paint has something to hold onto.

For the face, a tiny bottle of black acrylic and a toothpick or pencil eraser are all you need. Practice your dots on a scrap of cardboard first; it's easier than you'd think to get them evenly sized once you get the feel for how much paint to pick up. Finish with a clear matte sealer if the snowman will be handled a lot or displayed somewhere humid.

Design Ideas for Your Snowman

Every spindle has its own shape, so every snowman ends up a little different. That's the charm of it. A few ideas to make yours special:

  • Faces with personality. Tilt the eyes slightly or place them close together for a sweet, curious look. A few tiny dots in a curve make a classic coal smile.
  • Rosy cheeks. Dab a dry brush in pink paint or powder blush and swirl it lightly on the cheeks.
  • Hats from anything. Sock cuffs make stocking caps, felt circles make berets, and a small wooden spool painted black becomes a top hat.
  • Scarves in Southern plaid. Thin strips torn from an old flannel shirt look cozy and give the snowman a farmhouse feel.
  • A distressed finish. Lightly sand the edges after painting to let a little wood peek through. Our post on how to distress furniture explains the technique.

Variations to Try

Once you've made one, it's hard to stop. Cut segments of different lengths to make a whole snowman family and line them up on a mantel or windowsill. Make a snow lady with felt earmuffs and a tiny pom-pom. Brush on a little glue and sprinkle with clear glitter or coarse salt for a frosty, sparkly texture.

For gifts, write the year or a family name on the bottom with a paint pen. Taller pieces cut from table legs make wonderful porch-step greeters if you seal them with an exterior clear coat. And if you have leftover spindle scraps, turn them into our spindle ornaments for a matching set.

Turning a Spindle Snowman Into an Ornament

To hang your snowman on the tree, keep the pieces short, around 3 to 4 inches, and choose slimmer spindles so they aren't too heavy for a branch. Drill a tiny pilot hole in the top center, twist in a small screw eye, and tie on a loop of twine or ribbon before you glue the hat in place. The hat will hide the hardware, and the twine pokes out the top like a tassel.

Heavier snowmen are better as shelf sitters. If one wobbles, sand the bottom flat on a sheet of sandpaper laid on a table, or glue on a small wooden disc as a base.

Care and Storage

Painted wood holds up well year after year. Store your snowmen wrapped in tissue in a shoebox so the paint doesn't rub and the hats stay in shape. Keep them out of damp basements and hot attics, which can loosen glue and make paint crack. A quick dusting with a soft brush each season is all they need. Pair them with a barn wood Christmas tree for a rustic winter vignette.

Frequently asked questions

What kind of spindle works best for a spindle snowman?

Spindles with two or three distinct rounded beads work best, because those sections read as the snowman's head and body. Stair balusters, chair spindles, and small table legs are all good options. New unfinished spindles from a craft or hardware store work just as well.

Is it safe to use old painted balusters?

Old painted wood may contain lead, especially from homes built before 1978. Test it with a lead test swab before cutting or sanding. If it tests positive, it is safest to choose bare or new wood instead.

What paint should I use on a spindle snowman?

Chalk-style paint or acrylic craft paint both work well on raw or lightly sanded wood. Use two thin coats of white for good coverage. Seal with a clear coat if the snowman will sit outdoors.

How do I hang a spindle snowman on a tree?

Keep the piece short and light, then twist a small screw eye into the top and add a twine loop. Glue the hat on afterward so it hides the hardware.
