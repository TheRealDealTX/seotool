How to Distress Furniture with Chalk Paint for a Farmhouse Look

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · September 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Paint, sand the edges, seal and you're done. Our step-by-step for a believable farmhouse finish on a thrifted piece of furniture.

There's a reason chalk-painted furniture shows up in so many farmhouse homes: it's forgiving, it covers almost anything with minimal prep, and it sands back beautifully to that gently worn look. If you've been wondering how to distress furniture without making it look like a cartoon of "shabby chic," chalk paint is the easiest place to start. A thrifted nightstand or side table can go from dated to charming in an afternoon.

The secret to a believable finish is restraint. Real wear happens where hands, feet and bumps would naturally rub through the paint over decades of use. When you distress only those spots, the piece looks like it has a story instead of looking sanded at random.

Time4–6 hours plus drying
DifficultyEasy
Cost$40–$80

How to make it

  1. Remove hardware and clean

    Take off knobs and pulls, then scrub the whole piece with a mild degreaser and rinse with a damp rag. Let it dry completely, since chalk paint grips best on a clean surface.

  2. Repair and scuff

    Fill gouges or loose veneer spots with wood filler and let it cure. Lightly scuff glossy finishes with 120-grit sandpaper so the paint has something to hold onto, then wipe away the dust.

  3. Apply the first coat

    Brush on a thin, even coat of chalk paint, following the wood grain or using loose crisscross strokes for texture. If brown or pink stains bleed through, let it dry and spot-prime with stain-blocking primer.

  4. Add a second coat

    Once the first coat is dry to the touch, usually within an hour or so, apply a second coat for full coverage. If layering colors, use your base color first and your top color second.

  5. Distress the edges

    When the paint is fully dry, lightly sand edges, corners, raised details and around handles with 220-grit sandpaper. Work a little at a time and step back often so the wear looks natural.

  6. Wipe off dust

    Remove all sanding dust with a dry cloth or slightly damp rag. Any dust left behind will get trapped in your finish.

  7. Seal the finish

    Apply clear wax in thin layers with a wax brush or cloth, wipe off the excess and buff after it sets. For tabletops and high-use pieces, a water-based polyurethane gives tougher protection.

  8. Reattach hardware

    Clean or replace the hardware and screw it back on once the finish has dried. Treat the piece gently for the first few weeks while the finish cures.

Finding the Right Piece

Solid wood furniture is ideal because sanding reveals real wood underneath. Look for sturdy construction, drawers that slide, and simple lines with a few details like turned legs, raised panels or beveled edges, since those are the spots that distress best. Estate sales, thrift stores and yard sales are full of good candidates. Our estate sale tips can help you find the best pieces before someone else does.

A few things to watch out for:

  • Laminate and veneer: Chalk paint can work on laminate with proper scuffing and primer, but distressing will expose particleboard or plastic, not wood. Veneer is thin, so sand it lightly.
  • Valuable antiques: Painting a genuine antique can greatly reduce its value. If a piece looks old and well made, research it before you paint. Our guide on how to spot reproduction antiques covers some of the construction clues.
  • Smells and pests: Musty odors and signs of insects are hard to fix, so pass on those.

A Word on Lead Paint and Safety

If a piece already has old paint on it, especially paint from before 1978, assume it could contain lead until you've tested it. Inexpensive lead test kits are available at hardware stores. If it tests positive, don't sand it: paint over it without distressing, or set the piece aside. For any sanding, wear a dust mask, work outdoors or in a well-ventilated space, and clean up dust with a damp cloth rather than sweeping. Keep children and pets away from the work area, and let used wax rags dry flat outdoors before throwing them away, since some finishing products can be a fire risk when rags are bunched up.

How to Distress Furniture: Colors and Styles to Try

Classic farmhouse white

A warm creamy white over a dark wood stain is the most popular look. The contrast at the edges makes the distressing pop.

Two-color layering

Paint a base coat in one color, such as a muted blue, sage green or barn red, then a top coat in white or cream. When you sand, the base color peeks through along with bits of wood, which looks like a piece repainted over generations.

Wet distressing

Instead of sandpaper, use a damp cloth or sponge to rub away paint while it's dry but not fully cured. It creates softer edges and almost no dust, which makes it a nice option indoors.

Resist technique

Rub a little petroleum jelly or wax on edges before painting. The paint won't stick well there, so it wipes or sands off easily afterward to reveal the layer beneath.

Dark wax accents

After a coat of clear wax, a little dark wax worked into crevices and wiped back adds an aged look. Use it sparingly and always over clear wax so it doesn't stain unevenly.

Variations and Project Ideas

  • Small accessories first: Not ready for a dresser? Practice on a picture frame, a wooden tray or a step stool. Our DIY tiered tray is a great small project to try the technique on.
  • Mismatched chairs: Paint a set of thrifted chairs in the same color and distress them alike to pull them together around a table.
  • Painted drawer interiors: A contrasting color inside drawers is a fun surprise.
  • Stenciling: Add a simple stencil or number after painting, then lightly sand over it so it looks aged along with the rest.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Distressing everywhere. Sanding flat panels and random spots looks forced. Stick to edges, corners, raised details and the areas around knobs and pulls, where real wear happens.
  • Painting too thick. Heavy coats can crack, show brush globs and take longer to dry. Two thin coats look better and sand more evenly.
  • Sanding before the paint is dry. Wet or tacky paint gums up the sandpaper and tears instead of wearing back cleanly. Give each coat plenty of drying time.
  • Skipping the dust cleanup. Sanding dust trapped under wax or topcoat leaves a gritty, cloudy finish.
  • Too much wax. Wax goes on thin, like lotion. Thick wax stays tacky, attracts dust and looks blotchy.
  • Ignoring bleed-through. Some old finishes, especially on mahogany and certain stains, bleed pink or brown through light paint. Spot-prime as soon as you see it rather than adding more coats.

Caring for Your Distressed Furniture

Chalk paint is porous until it's sealed, so don't skip the topcoat. Wax gives a soft, hand-rubbed look but needs time to cure fully, often a few weeks, so use coasters and treat the piece gently at first. Wipe it with a soft, dry or slightly damp cloth, and avoid harsh cleaners. Refresh waxed pieces with a thin new coat every so often, especially on tabletops. For kitchen tables, dressers that see daily use or anything near water, water-based polyurethane is usually the more durable choice.

Once you've done one piece, you'll start seeing possibilities in every thrift store. Take your time with the sanding, step back often, and stop a little before you think you're done. That's usually right where it looks best.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to sand furniture before using chalk paint?

Usually not much. Chalk paint sticks well to most clean surfaces, but a light scuff with 120-grit sandpaper helps on glossy finishes. Always clean the piece with a degreaser first.

When should I distress chalk paint, before or after waxing?

Most people distress after the paint is dry and before sealing, then wipe away dust and apply wax or topcoat. Some prefer to wax first and then distress, which can produce smoother edges with less dust, followed by a final light coat of wax.

What grit sandpaper is best for distressing furniture?

A fine 220-grit sandpaper or sanding sponge gives controlled, natural-looking wear. Coarser 120-grit removes paint faster but can leave scratches, so use it only where you want heavier wear.

Should I use wax or polyurethane over chalk paint?

Wax gives a soft, hand-rubbed look and suits decorative or lightly used pieces. Water-based polyurethane is more durable and better for tabletops and furniture that sees daily use or moisture.

Is it safe to sand old painted furniture?

Paint from before 1978 may contain lead, so test it with a lead test kit before sanding. If it tests positive, don't sand or distress it. Paint over it or choose a different piece.
