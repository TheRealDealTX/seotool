DIY Tinsel Tree: How to Make a Faux Vintage Tinsel Christmas Tree

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · October 14, 2026 · 6 min read

Turn a thrift-store artificial tree into a shimmering retro showpiece with metallic paint and tinsel garland.

There's something about a shimmering silver Christmas tree that makes folks smile. It looks like a holiday card from 1962 come to life. A real vintage tinsel tree can be hard to find (and harder to afford), so this DIY tinsel tree project shows you how to turn a plain thrift-store artificial tree into a retro showpiece with a few cans of metallic paint and a box of tinsel garland.

We love this project because it rescues a tree somebody else gave up on. Those lopsided, slightly balding artificial trees that turn up at thrift stores and yard sales every January are perfect candidates. Once they're silver and sparkling, nobody notices a thin spot or two.

What Is a Tinsel Tree?

If you're wondering what is a tinsel tree, the term usually refers to the shiny aluminum Christmas trees that were a sensation in American living rooms from around the late 1950s through the mid-1960s. The best-known brand, Evergleam, was made by the Aluminum Specialty Company in Manitowoc, Wisconsin, and its foil-like needles caught light like nothing else in the house. Because you should never string electric lights on a metal tree, families lit them with a rotating color wheel that bathed the branches in red, blue, green, and amber.

Their popularity faded by the late 1960s. Many people point to A Charlie Brown Christmas (1965), which gently poked fun at shiny aluminum trees, but collectors eventually brought them roaring back. Today an original in its box with intact branch sleeves can bring a surprising sum, and condition drives the value almost entirely. That's exactly why a DIY tinsel Christmas tree makes sense: you get the look without hunting down an original or worrying about crushing a collectible.

How to make it

  1. Fluff and clean the tree

    Shape every branch so the tips point outward and wipe away dust with a dry cloth. Paint will not stick well to a dusty tree.

  2. Set up an outdoor paint station

    Lay down a drop cloth outside on a calm, dry day. Hang removable branches from a clothesline or lay them flat on cardboard so you can reach both sides.

  3. Prime if needed

    Mist the branches with a plastic-bonding primer, holding the can 10 to 12 inches away. Let it dry according to the can directions.

  4. Spray the silver in light coats

    Apply metallic silver in several light passes, turning the branches to coat the undersides. Light coats keep needles from clumping or dripping.

  5. Let the paint cure fully

    Leave the tree outdoors or in a ventilated garage for at least 24 to 48 hours, until there is no paint odor and nothing transfers to your fingers.

  6. Assemble and wrap with tinsel

    Put the tree back together and wrap thin tinsel garland in a loose spiral from the trunk out along each branch to add that foil-like shimmer.

  7. Decorate and light

    Add single-color ornaments and set a color wheel or spotlight nearby. If you want lights on the tree itself, use only cool-running LEDs.

Finding the Right Tree for Your DIY Tinsel Tree

Almost any artificial tree can work, but some take paint better than others. Here's what we look for:

  • Flat, papery PVC tips. The thin, flat needles on older, inexpensive trees grab paint and look the most like foil once they're silver.
  • Straight, evenly spaced branches. A retro artificial Christmas tree with a simple bottle-brush silhouette gives you that classic mid-century shape.
  • A manageable size. Tabletop trees in the 2- to 4-foot range are the easiest to paint evenly and look charming on a sideboard or in a window.
  • No pre-lit wiring. Skip pre-lit trees. Paint can gum up sockets, and you don't want to risk damaging wire insulation you can't see.

Thrift stores, church rummage sales, and after-Christmas clearance bins are your best bets. If you enjoy the hunt, our antique picking tips and guide to the Christmas barn sale trail will help you know where to look.

Can You Spray Paint a Christmas Tree?

Can you spray paint a Christmas tree? It's one of the most common questions we hear, and the answer is yes, with a few precautions. Most artificial trees are made from PVC or PE plastic, and a spray paint labeled for plastic (or a plastic primer followed by metallic paint) bonds well. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated garage, wear a mask rated for paint fumes, and apply several light coats rather than one heavy one. Heavy coats clump the needles together and can drip.

Two honest caveats: painted needles may shed a little metallic dust during the first season, and a coat of paint changes the surface of the tree in ways the manufacturer never tested. Let the paint cure completely before bringing the tree inside, use only cool-running LED lights if you add lights at all, and keep it well away from candles, space heaters, and fireplaces.

Tip: Spend extra time on the undersides of the branches. That's the part you see from the sofa, and bare green patches are what give a painted tree away.

Styling Ideas for a Retro Look

Once your tree is silver and draped in tinsel, the fun begins. For a true mid-century feel, keep the decorations simple and bold:

  • One-color ornaments. All-red, all-blue, or all-pink glass balls were a classic look on aluminum trees. Mixed colors can look busy against all that shine.
  • A color wheel or spotlight. Reproduction rotating color wheels are easy to find. Aim one at the tree for that authentic glow.
  • Vintage glass ornaments. Look for old striped and indented glass ornaments in holiday boxes at estate sales; a little wear adds charm. Our estate sale tips cover how to shop those boxes.
  • A felt or tinsel tree skirt. White felt or a ring of extra tinsel garland hides the stand and reflects even more light.

Variations to Try

Silver is the classic, but aluminum trees were also sold in colors like pink, gold, blue, and green, so feel free to play. A pale pink tree with white ornaments is pure 1960s whimsy. Gold paint with copper ornaments feels warmer and pairs nicely with wood paneling and brass. For a frosty look, lightly mist the tips with white over the silver.

You can also skip paint entirely and wrap a green tree densely with silver tinsel garland, working from the trunk out along each branch. It takes more garland and patience, but there's nothing to cure. Small bottle-brush trees can be sprayed the same way to make a mini silver forest for a mantel or a cake stand.

Care, Storage, and Safety Notes

  • Never put electric lights on a real metal tree. If you ever find an original aluminum tree, light it only with a color wheel or spotlight. The old boxes warned about this for good reason.
  • Keep it away from flames and heat. Painted plastic and tinsel are both flammable. No candles nearby, and keep the tree several feet from heaters.
  • Store it gently. Wrap branches in tissue or slip them into paper sleeves, then pack them in a sturdy box. Crushed tinsel rarely fluffs back up.
  • Mind little ones and pets. Tinsel can be dangerous if swallowed, so hang it high or skip loose strands if you have cats or toddlers.

Frequently asked questions

What is a tinsel tree?

A tinsel tree usually means the shiny aluminum Christmas trees popular in the US from around the late 1950s to the mid-1960s. Their foil-like branches were lit with a rotating color wheel instead of string lights. Today the term also covers any silver, tinsel-covered artificial tree.

Can you spray paint a Christmas tree?

Yes. Most artificial trees are PVC or PE plastic, and spray paint made for plastic bonds well, especially over a plastic primer. Paint outdoors in light coats, let it cure fully before bringing it inside, and keep the tree away from heat and open flames.

Can I put lights on a DIY tinsel tree?

On a painted plastic tree, cool-running LED lights are the safest choice. Never put electric lights on a genuine metal aluminum tree, because the branches can conduct electricity. Use a color wheel or spotlight instead.

How much paint does a DIY tinsel Christmas tree need?

A tabletop tree usually takes one to two cans, and a 4-foot tree often needs three or four. Fuller trees with more tips will need more. Buy an extra can so you are not left with green patches.

Where can I find a retro artificial Christmas tree to paint?

Thrift stores, church rummage sales, yard sales, and after-Christmas clearance bins are the best sources. Look for older trees with flat, papery needles and simple, evenly spaced branches.
