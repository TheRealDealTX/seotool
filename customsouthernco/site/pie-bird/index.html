Pie Bird: History, How to Use One & Collecting Tips

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · August 28, 2026 · 6 min read

That little ceramic bird poking out of Grandma's pie had a real job. Here's the history of the pie bird, how to bake with one, and how to collect them.

If you've ever spotted a little ceramic bird with its beak tipped up to the sky in a flea market kitchen booth, you've met a pie bird. These charming little figures look like pure decoration, but they were made to work. Tucked into the center of a double-crust pie, a pie bird lets steam escape and keeps the top crust from sagging into the filling. Today they're also one of the sweetest small collectibles for anyone who loves a vintage kitchen.

Here's what a pie bird is, where it came from, how to use one in your own baking, and what to look for if you'd like to start collecting.

What Is a Pie Bird?

A pie bird is a hollow ceramic or porcelain funnel, usually shaped like a bird with an open beak, that stands upright in a pie dish. The base has one or more arched openings so steam from the filling can flow in, travel up through the hollow body, and vent out the beak. The crust is cut around it so only the head and beak poke through.

Pie birds are also called pie funnels, pie vents, pie whistles, pie chimneys, and pie crust supports. Not all of them are birds. Over the years you'll see elephants, chefs, dragons, ducks, and plain cone shapes, but the bird is by far the most common and the most loved.

What a Pie Bird Does

  • Vents steam. Juicy fruit fillings release a lot of steam. Without an escape, it can push the top crust up, crack it, or force filling out the edges.
  • Prevents boil-over. Letting steam out through the center keeps bubbling juices from overflowing onto the oven floor (mostly; no pie is perfectly polite).
  • Holds up the crust. In deep pies, particularly savory meat pies baked in deep dishes, the funnel props up the center of the crust so it doesn't sink into the filling and get soggy.

The History of the Pie Bird

From Plain Pie Funnels

The pie bird grew out of the plain pie funnel, a simple hollow ceramic cone or chimney used in British kitchens. By the late 1800s these funnels were common in Victorian England, where deep savory pies of meat, game, and vegetables were everyday fare. The funnel was a practical tool: a sturdy ceramic support that kept a thick pastry lid lifted and vented.

The Bird Takes Flight

Over time, makers began shaping funnels into fun figures, and the bird became the favorite. Figural pie funnels became especially popular in the early to mid-twentieth century in both Britain and the United States. Many people link the blackbird design to the old nursery rhyme "Sing a Song of Sixpence," with its "four and twenty blackbirds baked in a pie." Whether or not that rhyme inspired the first one, it's hard to look at a little blackbird peeking out of a crust and not think of it.

In the mid-1900s, American potteries and hobby ceramicists made pie birds in all sorts of colors and styles, from glossy black birds with yellow beaks to pastel birds, roosters, and songbirds. They were sold in kitchen shops and dime stores and given as gifts, which is why so many turn up today in estate sales and church rummage sales.

How to Use a Pie Bird

Using a pie bird is easy, and it works with both sweet and savory pies.

  1. Prepare the bottom crust. Line your pie dish with pastry as usual.
  2. Place the bird. Set the pie bird in the center of the dish, standing upright on the bottom crust with the openings at its base clear.
  3. Add the filling. Spoon the filling around the bird. Keep it from clogging the vent holes at the bottom.
  4. Cut a hole in the top crust. Cut a small X or circle in the center of your top crust and gently ease it down over the bird's head so the beak pokes through.
  5. Seal and bake. Crimp the edges, brush with egg wash if you like, and bake as your recipe directs. Steam will escape through the beak.
  6. Remove carefully. The bird will be very hot. Let the pie cool before lifting it out, or serve around it.
Tip: Warm your pie bird to room temperature before using it, and never move a ceramic bird straight from a cold kitchen into a very hot oven. Sudden temperature changes can crack older pieces.

A pie bird is especially handy for juicy fruit pies like blackberry, cherry, and peach, and for deep-dish chicken or beef pot pies. For a shallow single-crust pie, you can skip it. If you enjoy the old-fashioned kitchen look, a pie bird sits nicely on an open shelf alongside other finds like vintage canning jars. See our guide to vintage jars to look for for ideas.

Collecting Vintage Pie Birds

Pie birds are small, colorful, and still affordable in many cases, which makes them a wonderful starting collection. Here's what we look for.

Where to Find Them

  • Estate sales, in the kitchen drawers and the back of the cupboard. Check our estate sale tips for getting in early.
  • Antique malls and flea markets, in kitchenware booths alongside measuring cups and cookie cutters.
  • Church and charity rummage sales, where small kitchen items are often priced low.
  • Online marketplaces, which offer a big selection but call for careful attention to photos and descriptions.

What Affects Value

Most common vintage pie birds typically sell for modest amounts, often in the range of a few dollars to around $30. Older, marked, unusual, or figural pieces in excellent condition can bring more. As always, condition drives value. Look for:

  • No chips or cracks. Check the beak and the edges of the base openings, which take the most wear.
  • Clean glaze. Crazing (fine cracks in the glaze) is common on older pieces and may lower value slightly; heavy staining lowers it more.
  • Maker's marks. A stamped or impressed mark on the base can help with identification and adds interest.
  • Unusual forms. Animals other than birds, multi-figure pieces, and well-made figurals often stand out.

How to Spot a Reproduction Pie Bird

Pie birds have been popular enough that many modern versions exist, including new birds made to look old. There's nothing wrong with a new pie bird for baking, but if you're paying a collector's price, you'll want to know what you're buying. These clues help:

  • Check the base. Older funnels often have an unglazed base showing honest wear and kitchen grime. A perfectly clean, uniform base can suggest a newer piece.
  • Look at the vent openings. On a working pie bird, the arched openings at the base are clean and functional. Some decorative copies have shallow or poorly formed openings that wouldn't vent properly.
  • Feel the weight. Many older pieces are made of heavier stoneware or earthenware. Very light, thin pieces deserve a second look.
  • Examine the paint. Cold paint (painted on after firing) that looks too fresh, or "aged" finishes that rub off, can point to a newer piece.
  • Read the marks carefully. Some modern pieces carry marks meant to look old. Compare marks to reference guides when you can.

For more on spotting fakes across all kinds of antiques, see our guide on how to spot reproduction antiques.

Caring for a Pie Bird

If you plan to bake with a vintage pie bird, wash it by hand in warm, soapy water and use a bottle brush or pipe cleaner to clean the hollow inside. Let it dry completely, upright, before storing. Skip the dishwasher for older pieces, since harsh detergent and heat can dull glaze and paint. And if a bird has hairline cracks, deep crazing, or chips, we'd retire it to the display shelf rather than the oven. Some older ceramic glazes may also contain lead, so if you're unsure about a piece's age or origin, it's wise to keep it decorative and use a newer, food-safe pie bird for baking.

Displaying Your Pie Bird Collection

A flock of pie birds makes a cheerful display. Line them up on a narrow kitchen shelf, gather them on a cake stand, or group them by color in a glass-front cabinet. A small collection on a vintage tiered tray looks especially sweet. Mixing pie birds with old pie tins, rolling pins, and enamelware gives a farmhouse kitchen that lived-in, well-loved feel.

However you use them, pie birds are a little reminder that the most charming kitchen tools were often the hardest-working ones. Happy baking and happy hunting. Find more vintage kitchen finds on the blog.

Frequently asked questions

What is a pie bird used for?

A pie bird is a hollow ceramic funnel placed in the center of a double-crust pie. It vents steam through its beak so the top crust doesn't crack or sag, and helps keep juicy fillings from bubbling over. In deep pies it also props up the center of the crust.

Do you put a pie bird in before or after the filling?

Place the pie bird on the bottom crust first, then spoon the filling around it. Cut a small hole in the top crust and ease it over the bird's head so the beak pokes through before baking.

Why are pie birds shaped like blackbirds?

Pie funnels were originally plain cones, but figural shapes became popular in the twentieth century. The blackbird design is commonly linked to the nursery rhyme "Sing a Song of Sixpence," which mentions four and twenty blackbirds baked in a pie.

How much is a vintage pie bird worth?

Many common vintage pie birds typically sell for a few dollars to around $30. Marked, older, or unusual figural pieces in excellent condition can bring more. Chips, cracks, and heavy staining lower value.

Is it safe to bake with an old pie bird?

A sound, uncracked pie bird can usually be used, but older ceramic glazes may contain lead. If you're unsure of a piece's age or origin, keep it for display and bake with a newer, food-safe pie bird.
