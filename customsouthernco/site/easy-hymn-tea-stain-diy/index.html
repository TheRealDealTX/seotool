How to Make Tea Stained Hymn Pages for Vintage Decor

By The Custom Southern Co. Team · August 15, 2026 · 5 min read

A few tea bags turn plain hymnal pages or printed copies into warm, aged paper for frames, banners, wreaths, and gift wrap.

There is something about old hymnal pages that feels right at home in a Southern house. The notes, the familiar titles, and the soft paper bring back Sunday mornings for a lot of us. Making tea stained hymn pages deepens that warm, aged look in about an hour, using nothing fancier than a few tea bags from the pantry.

Tea-stained pages are a building block for dozens of projects: framed art, garlands, wreaths, gift wrap, and decoupage. We will cover where to find pages, how to stain them, and our favorite ways to use them.

Time1 hour (plus drying)
DifficultyEasy
Cost$0–$10

How to make it

  1. Brew a strong tea bath

    Steep 4-6 black tea bags in 2-3 cups of hot water for at least 10 minutes. The longer it steeps, the darker the stain.

  2. Prep the pages

    Gently remove pages along the spine or print copies. For a more aged look, crumple each page into a ball and then smooth it back out.

  3. Stain the paper

    Pour the tea into a rimmed tray and dip each page for a few seconds, or brush the tea on with a sponge. Dab a used tea bag on the edges and corners for darker spots.

  4. Blot the excess

    Lift each page carefully, since wet paper tears easily, and blot it between paper towels.

  5. Dry the pages

    Air dry flat on a rack or hang from a line. For speed, oven dry on parchment at the lowest setting, about 200°F, for a few minutes while you watch it the entire time.

  6. Flatten and finish

    Once completely dry, press pages under heavy books overnight. Optionally burn or sand the edges lightly for extra age, following the safety notes.

Finding Hymnals to Use

Damaged and incomplete hymnals turn up regularly at thrift stores, library sales, estate sales, and church rummage sales, often for very little. Look for books with loose bindings, water damage, or missing covers, which are perfect for crafting and not much use for anything else. Our estate sale tips can help you spot them in a crowded book room.

We do encourage a little respect here. If a hymnal is in good shape, has a family inscription, or looks old and unusual, consider keeping it intact or passing it on to someone who will sing from it. For those, simply scan or photocopy favorite pages and stain the copies. Many older hymns are in the public domain, and printed copies stain beautifully on plain printer paper.

Getting the Best Tea Stained Hymn Pages

  • Black tea is the standard. Basic black tea bags give a warm golden-brown tone. Rooibos leans more reddish.
  • Coffee goes darker. A strong mix of instant coffee and water gives a deeper, more dramatic antique look.
  • Layer for depth. Let one coat dry, then dab on a second in spots for a mottled, uneven finish that looks naturally aged.
  • Crumple first. Creases catch more tea and create fine, darker lines like an old letter.
  • Sprinkle for spots. Sprinkle a few loose tea leaves or coffee grounds onto a wet page and brush them off when dry for speckles.
Tip: Old book paper is often thin and soft. Brush the tea on instead of dunking, and lift wet pages with a spatula slid underneath so they do not tear.

Ways to Use Tea Stained Hymn Pages

Framed hymn art

Frame a single page of a favorite hymn in a thrifted frame with the glass removed or a simple wooden frame. Group three for a gallery wall above a piano or a guest bed.

Banners and garlands

Cut pages into pennants, punch two holes, and thread them on twine. Our vintage valentine banner uses a similar technique you can adapt to any holiday.

Paper wreaths

Roll pages into cones or fold them into fans and glue them to a cardboard ring or a foam wreath form for a soft, neutral wreath.

Decoupage

Tea-stained pages are lovely on frames, trays, and boxes. They are exactly what we used on the frame of our old window mirror.

Gift wrap and tags

Wrap small gifts in a page and tie with jute and a sprig of rosemary, or cut tags and write the recipient's name in ink.

Seasonal touches

Fold pages into paper stars at Christmas, cut hearts in February, or tuck rolled pages into a crock with dried flowers. They pair nicely with book page decor and other paper projects.

Choosing a Shade: Light, Medium, or Dark

Before you stain a whole stack, test a scrap page. A quick dip in weak tea gives a light, honey-colored wash that keeps the notes crisp and readable, which is best for framed art where you want the words to show. A longer soak in strong tea gives a medium, parchment look that suits garlands and wreaths. Strong coffee or a second coat produces a dark, rich brown with deep edges, great for gift wrap and rustic decoupage where the paper is more texture than text. Keep in mind that pages look darker while wet and dry a shade or two lighter, so go a bit deeper than you think you need.

It also helps to stain more pages than your project calls for. Every page takes the tea a little differently, and having extras lets you pick the best-looking ones and save the rest for another day.

Variations to Try

  • Sheet music. The same method works on any vintage sheet music or old book pages.
  • Stamped pages. After staining, stamp a simple botanical or a word in brown ink.
  • Two-tone. Dip only the bottom half of a page for an ombre look.
  • Spiced stain. Add a cinnamon stick or a pinch of ground cloves to the tea bath. The color shifts slightly warmer and the pages carry a faint, cozy scent for a while.
  • Waxed pages. Rub a little beeswax over a dried page for a soft sheen and slight water resistance.

Safety and Care Notes

Paper and heat need attention. If you oven dry your pages, use the lowest setting, keep pages on parchment, and stay right there the whole time. Paper can scorch quickly, so take it out as soon as it is dry. Never leave the oven on and walk away.

Some crafters singe page edges for an aged look. If you try it, work over a sink or a metal tray with water nearby, hold only a small section of edge to a candle for a split second, and blow it out immediately. Brushing a little extra tea or coffee along the edges gives a similar look with no flame at all, and it is what we usually recommend.

Tea-stained paper is for decor, and like most paper it will keep aging. Keep finished pieces out of direct sun to slow fading, and frame anything you want to last behind glass.

Frequently asked questions

What kind of tea is best for staining paper?

Plain black tea is the most popular choice and gives a warm golden-brown tone. Strong instant coffee makes a darker antique finish, and rooibos adds a slightly reddish cast. Steeping longer makes any of them darker.

How long do tea stained pages take to dry?

Air drying usually takes a few hours depending on humidity. Oven drying at the lowest setting takes only a few minutes, but you must watch the paper the entire time so it does not scorch.

Is it okay to cut up an old hymnal for crafts?

Using a damaged or incomplete hymnal is common and practical. If a hymnal is in good condition, has family history, or seems rare, consider keeping it whole and staining photocopies of your favorite pages instead.

Will tea stained paper attract bugs or mold?

Once it is fully dry, tea-stained paper behaves much like any other paper decor. Make sure pages are completely dry before stacking or framing them, and keep them out of damp areas to avoid mildew.
