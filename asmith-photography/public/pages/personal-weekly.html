The Weekly Photo Project: A Practice for Working Photographers

Client work pays the bills but rarely moves your eye forward. A weekly photo project with a few simple rules is the most reliable way to keep seeing.

Personal & Documentary3 min readFrame 46A

Why working photographers need one

A weekly photo project is one of the oldest habits in photography, and it matters most to people who already shoot for a living. Commercial work is shaped by briefs, budgets and approvals. Over time it trains you to deliver what is expected. A personal practice is where you get to find out what you would do if no one were asking.

It is also where new skills get tested at low stakes. A lighting idea you want to try on a campaign, a film stock you have not used, a lens you are thinking about buying. Try it on a Tuesday walk through Silver Lake before you bet a client's shoot on it.

And it keeps a portfolio alive. Art directors notice when a book shows only past clients. Personal work shows how you see, which is what they are actually hiring.

Setting the rules

The best projects have constraints. One day a week, one camera, one lens, one neighborhood or one subject. Constraints remove decisions and push you to look harder at what is in front of you. A 35mm prime and the six blocks around your apartment can carry a year.

Pick a frequency you can actually keep. Weekly works because it is frequent enough to build a habit and loose enough to survive busy production weeks. Daily projects burn out fast; monthly ones drift. Put it on the calendar like a client booking.

Decide in advance what counts as done. One finished image a week, a contact sheet or a sequence of five. Without a finish line, it is easy to shoot, never edit and lose the thread.

Client work teaches you to deliver what is expected. Personal work shows you what you would do if no one asked.

Ideas that hold up for a year

Some prompts sustain a weekly photo project better than others. A single place visited every week, like the LA River in Frogtown or one stretch of Broadway Downtown, shows change over seasons. A single subject, like strangers at bus stops or swimming pools, builds a typology.

Others are about method. Shoot only at night, only on film, only at 1/15s, only with a flash on camera. A technical rule is a fast way to break habits you did not know you had, and the results often feed straight back into client work.

Collaborative versions work too. Two photographers swap a prompt every Monday and share results on Sunday. The accountability alone keeps both going through the slow weeks.

Editing, sharing and keeping going

Edit every week, even briefly. Pick your best frame, write the date and place and file it somewhere consistent. A simple folder structure by year and week number is enough. At the end of a quarter, look back across the whole run; patterns appear that you could not see week to week.

Share it somewhere. A private account, a newsletter to a few friends, a printed zine at the end of the year. Sharing creates a small, steady pressure to keep going and gives the work a life outside your hard drive.

Expect gaps. A busy month or a bad week happens. The point of a weekly photo project is not a perfect streak. It is to keep coming back to looking for its own sake, and to let that seep into everything else you shoot.

Turning the practice into work

A year of weekly frames is a body of work waiting to be edited. Lay them out, look for threads and pull a tight sequence of 20 to 30. That sequence can become a zine, a small show or a new section in your portfolio.

Personal projects also give you something real to talk about in meetings. Telling a creative director why you photographed the same bus stop for a year says more about how you think than a list of past clients ever could.

How to start

  1. Pick one day a week and block it on the calendar
  2. Limit yourself to one camera and one prime lens
  3. Choose a place, subject or technical rule for the year
  4. Edit to one finished frame each week, dated and captioned
  5. Review the whole run every quarter for patterns
  6. Share it, even privately, to keep yourself accountable
