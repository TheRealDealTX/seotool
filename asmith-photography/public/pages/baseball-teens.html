Youth Baseball Photography and the In-Between Moments

Baseball is mostly waiting, and that is the gift. A documentary approach to youth baseball photography that finds the story in dugouts, warmups and long summer evenings.

Sport3 min readFrame 41A

A game made of pauses

Youth baseball photography is a different job from shooting a big-league game. There is no photo well, no credentialed position, no scoreboard crew. There is a chain-link fence, a dugout full of teenagers and long stretches of nothing happening. For a documentary photographer, those stretches are the point.

A nine-inning game might have a few dozen moments of real action. The rest is players spitting sunflower seeds, adjusting batting gloves, leaning on the rail, sitting on buckets, watching the scoreboard. Those gestures say more about being sixteen and playing ball in summer than any swing.

Treat the game like an assignment about people, not plays. Make a list of the things you notice in the first inning and use it to guide the rest of the day.

Light at the ballfield

Summer league games in Los Angeles often start late in the afternoon, which means low, warm light raking across the infield and long shadows from the light poles. Position yourself so the sun is behind or to the side of the players. Backlit dust off a slide at first base can make a frame.

Midday weekend tournaments are harder. Hard overhead sun puts faces under cap brims in shadow. Shoot the dugout, where the roof makes open shade, or wait for a player to look up. A touch of positive exposure compensation keeps faces readable without blowing out white uniforms.

Evening games under field lights at a city park, from Griffith Park to the San Fernando Valley, bring color shifts and dim light. Raise ISO, set white balance manually and look for the glow around the lights as a background element.

Baseball is mostly waiting, and the waiting is where the pictures are.

Shooting through fences and dugouts

At most youth fields the best view is through a fence. Put a long lens, 70-200mm or longer, right up against the chain link and open the aperture wide. At f/2.8 the wire disappears into a soft haze. Back up a few inches and it becomes a pattern you can use deliberately.

The dugout is where the documentary happens. A 35mm or 28mm lens and a quiet camera with a silent shutter let you work close without breaking the mood. Shoot from the end of the bench or from outside the opening, never in the way of a coach or the bat rack.

Bullpens and warmups are good too. Pitchers loosening up, catchers trading jokes, a parent tossing a final ball before first pitch. Arrive an hour early and these scenes come to you.

Working with teenagers and families

Photographing minors calls for clear permission. Talk to the coach or league organizer before you shoot, explain what the pictures are for and ask how they handle consent. For anything that will be published, get signed releases from parents or guardians.

Teenagers are self-conscious. Spend time around the team before raising a camera, and the players stop performing for it. Share a few frames on the back of the camera; once they see the work is about them and not at their expense, the doors open.

Give back. Send the team a selection of good frames at the end of the season. In youth baseball photography, that small act of trust is what lets you keep coming back for another summer.

Kit for a long summer day

Keep it light. One body with a 70-200mm, a second with a 35mm, a spare battery and a hat. Tournament days can run eight hours in the sun, and a heavy bag gets left in the car by noon, along with the pictures you would have made with it.

Bring sunscreen, water and a folding stool. Shooting from a low seat behind the backstop or down the line puts you at the eye level of the players and makes a crowded ballpark feel intimate. Patience is the real equipment; the moments come to those who are still there in the seventh inning.

Shot list

  2. Long lens through chain link at f/2.8 so the fence disappears
  3. Backlit dust on a slide or a play at the plate
  4. Hands: gloves, seeds, tape, a bat knob being twisted
  5. Postgame walk to the parking lot under the field lights
  6. Signed releases from parents before publishing anything
