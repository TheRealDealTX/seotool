Craft Portraits: A Workshop in the Spirit of George Esquivel Shoes

Photographing a bespoke shoemaking workshop, the kind behind George Esquivel shoes, is a lesson in hands, tools, texture and patience, made for a California magazine story.

Editorial & Portrait3 min readFrame 34A

The workshop as subject

George Esquivel is a Southern California shoemaker, and a workshop like the one behind George Esquivel shoes is the kind of place a California magazine loves to profile: a room full of lasts, hides, hand tools and half-finished pairs. Photographing it well means treating the space and the process as subjects alongside the maker.

Spend the first hour watching, not shooting. Ask the shoemaker to walk you through how a pair is made, from pattern to cutting to lasting to finishing. Note which steps are the most visual and when they happen, so you can be in position rather than asking for a staged repeat.

Hands, tools and leather

Craft stories are told through hands. Get close with a 50mm macro or an 85mm at f/4, enough depth of field to hold fingers and the tool in focus. Shoot the awl going through leather, thread pulled tight, a hammer tapping a sole onto a last. Burst a few frames at each step; the decisive moment is often a fraction of a second.

Leather shows its quality through texture, and texture comes from side light. Place a small softbox or use a window at a low, raking angle across a hide or a finished toe cap. Polished leather reflects everything, so watch for your own silhouette and the ceiling lights in the shine.

Shoot the tools at rest too. Racks of lasts, a bench of knives worn smooth, spools of waxed thread. These make strong still lifes for openers and spreads.

Good craft photography moves at the speed of the craft. Wait for the hands, and the story arrives.

The maker's portrait

Once the process is covered, make a deliberate portrait. Put the shoemaker at the bench with the work in front of them and the shelves of lasts behind. Window light from one side with a white card fill keeps the room honest. A medium-wide 35mm captures face and workshop together.

Shoot a second, quieter frame: the maker looking at a finished pair, or holding a shoe up to the light. Those moments show the care involved better than a direct stare into the lens.

Kit and etiquette in a working shop

A small shoemaking workshop is crowded, so the kit should be compact: one body, a 35mm, a 50mm macro or 85mm, a reflector, a small battery LED with a soft dome and a fast card. Large strobes and stands get in the way of benches and machines, and the shop's own windows usually look better anyway.

Raise ISO before adding light. Workshop lighting is often a mix of skylights, task lamps and fluorescents, and a slightly noisy frame that keeps the real atmosphere beats a clean frame that looks lit for television. Set a custom white balance from a gray card at the bench and keep it for the whole session.

Remember that people are working. Ask before stepping behind a sewing machine, stay out of the line of a knife and keep bags off benches. Craftspeople notice a crew that respects their space, and they relax accordingly. In a profile of a shop like the one behind George Esquivel shoes, that relaxed trust shows up in the final portraits.

Making it a story for a California magazine

A good craft feature has a beginning and an end. Sequence the edit as the shoe is made: raw hide, pattern pieces, stitching, lasting, finishing, the finished pair on a customer's feet. California readers like the connection between a small local workshop and the larger culture of design and footwear in the state.

Keep color natural and warm, close to how leather looks in daylight. If the workshop is busy, agree times in advance so production isn't interrupted. A feature about a place like the one behind George Esquivel shoes is a portrait of patience, and the photography should feel just as unhurried.
