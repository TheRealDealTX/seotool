Where the money goes missing

Six places an Oregon claim usually comes up short

Most underpaid claims aren’t denied. They’re just incomplete. These are the gaps an Oregon Insurance Appraiser finds again and again in estimates written after Oregon storms and fires. The dollar ranges are illustrative only, not results from any client.

01

Matching left out

One slope of roof or one wall of siding approved, when the rest can’t be matched.

Illustrative gap: $6,000–$15,000
02

Old-house finishes priced as new

Plaster walls, old-growth fir trim and original hardwood priced as builder-grade drywall and laminate.

Illustrative gap: $4,000–$12,000
03

Tree impact priced as a patch

A fallen fir scoped as a roof repair with no framing, decking or interior damage.

Illustrative gap: $8,000–$30,000
04

Drying stopped too early

Water losses closed out before crawlspaces, insulation and subfloor were dry.

Illustrative gap: $3,000–$10,000
05

Code items missing

Underlayment, flashing and other work a building department can require during repairs.

Illustrative gap: $2,000–$8,000
06

Post-disaster pricing ignored

Rebuild costs estimated at normal prices after a regional wildfire or ice storm.

Illustrative gap: varies widely

The appraisal trail

From a short check to a signed award

The appraisal clause turns an open-ended argument into a defined process with a binding result. This is the trail an Oregon Insurance Appraiser walks with you.

1

A disagreement on the number

The insurer accepts the claim but its estimate is lower than yours. You have asked for a re-inspection or sent a supplement, and the gap remains.

2

The written demand

Either side sends a written demand for appraisal that identifies the claim, the disagreement and the clause being invoked.

3

Each side names an appraiser

Within the time the policy sets, often 20 days, each side names a competent, disinterested appraiser and tells the other.

4

The appraisers pick an umpire

The two appraisers agree on a neutral umpire. If they can’t, the policy usually lets either side ask a court to appoint one.

5

Inspection and estimates

Your appraiser inspects, measures and writes an independent, line-item estimate. The other appraiser does the same.

6

Working the differences

The appraisers compare estimates item by item and agree what they can. Most of the gap usually closes here.

7

Umpire review

Items still open go to the umpire, who reviews both appraisers’ support and decides each one.

8

The signed award

An award signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss. The insurer then pays under the policy’s terms.

The storm record

Events that still shape Oregon claim files

Every Oregon Insurance Appraiser estimate is written with this history in mind: what each kind of event does to a building, and what adjusters tend to miss afterwards.

1962

Columbus Day Storm

A remnant typhoon brought extreme winds to western Oregon, toppling trees and damaging buildings across the Willamette Valley. It remains the benchmark Pacific Northwest windstorm.

2007

Great Coastal Gale

In early December, hurricane-force gusts hit the Oregon coast, knocking out power for days and damaging roofs, siding and trees from Astoria south.

2010

Aumsville tornado

An EF2 tornado struck the small Marion County town of Aumsville in December, a reminder that Oregon tornadoes are rare but real.

2019

Willamette Valley snowstorm

Heavy, wet February snow brought down thousands of trees and limbs on homes in Eugene, Springfield and the southern valley.

2020

Labor Day fires

Wind-driven wildfires, including Almeda, Beachie Creek, Lionshead, Holiday Farm and Archie Creek, destroyed thousands of Oregon homes in a few days.

2021

February ice storm

Freezing rain coated the Portland and Salem areas, bringing down trees and power lines and leaving many homes without power for days.

Questions

Asked often of an Oregon Insurance Appraiser

Plain answers about how appraisal works, what it costs and when it’s the wrong move.

All questions Claim dictionary
What does an Oregon Insurance Appraiser do?

We value property damage when you and your insurer agree the loss is covered but disagree on what it costs. As the appraiser you appoint, we inspect, write an independent line-item estimate, work with the insurer’s appraiser and, if needed, present open items to a neutral umpire.

How is an appraiser different from a public adjuster?

A public adjuster handles and negotiates the whole claim. An appraiser works inside the policy’s appraisal clause on one question: the amount of loss. We don’t negotiate outside appraisal and we don’t give legal advice.

When is it worth calling an Oregon Insurance Appraiser?

When coverage is accepted, you have the insurer’s estimate, and the gap to your contractor’s number is meaningful, roughly more than 10% or several thousand dollars.

Is the appraisal award binding?

On the amount of loss, yes, once two of the three panel members sign. That is why the file you bring matters.

What does it cost?

Each side pays its own appraiser and usually half the umpire. We quote our fee in writing before work begins, and the first conversation is free.

Do you cover my part of Oregon?

Yes. We work in all 36 counties, from the Portland metro and Willamette Valley to the coast, Southern, Central and Eastern Oregon.
