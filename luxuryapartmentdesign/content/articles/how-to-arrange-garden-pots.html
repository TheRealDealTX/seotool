To arrange potted plants so they look designed rather than scattered, group them in odd numbers (three or five), vary the heights from tall at the back to low at the front, repeat one pot material or color, and cluster plants that share the same sun and water needs. Inside each larger container, use the thriller-filler-spiller formula: one tall focal plant, mid-height fillers around it and trailing plants spilling over the rim.

Those few rules work whether you are styling a 3 x 8 ft apartment balcony, a small patio, a front step or a terrace. They also make plant care easier, because pots that sit together and share conditions can be watered together.

Below you will find a step-by-step method for how to arrange garden pots, a pot size and material comparison, grouping layouts for different spaces, drainage essentials and a guide to matching plants with sun exposure.

The Thriller-Filler-Spiller Method

This is the classic container design formula, and it works for any pot about 14 in. (35 cm) wide or larger.

  • Thriller: one tall, eye-catching plant placed in the center (for a pot seen from all sides) or at the back (for a pot against a wall). Examples: ornamental grass, cordyline, dwarf conifer, canna, upright rosemary.
  • Filler: mounded mid-height plants that surround the thriller and hide the soil. Examples: geraniums, coleus, heuchera, begonias, petunias, parsley.
  • Spiller: trailing plants placed near the rim so they tumble over the edge. Examples: sweet potato vine, creeping Jenny, trailing ivy, bacopa, trailing verbena.

A good ratio for a 16 to 20 in. pot is one thriller, three fillers and two or three spillers. Choose all three for the same light and watering conditions. For a polished look, stick to two flower colors plus foliage, or go monochrome with greens and white.

Designer tip: For a high-end look, skip the mixed planting in your largest pots and use one plant per container instead, such as a clipped boxwood ball, an olive tree or a single ornamental grass. Save thriller-filler-spiller for one or two medium pots where you want color.

How to Arrange Potted Plants in a Group

Grouping is where most arrangements succeed or fail. Follow these steps.

  • Pick an anchor. Start with your largest pot and tallest plant. Place it at the back of the group or in a corner.
  • Use odd numbers. Groups of three or five look natural and balanced. Even numbers tend to look stiff unless you want symmetry.
  • Layer heights. Arrange tall, medium and short. A good rule of thumb is to make each step roughly one-half to two-thirds the height of the one behind it.
  • Form a triangle. Seen from the front, the tops of the plants should form a loose triangle or slope, not a flat line.
  • Vary width and texture. Combine a spiky plant, a rounded one and a trailing one so each stands out.
  • Repeat something. Repeat a pot color, a material or one plant variety across the group to tie it together.
  • Overlap slightly. Let foliage touch or overlap so the group reads as one mass rather than separate pots.
  • Step back and edit. View it from the doorway or indoors. Remove anything that looks like an afterthought.

Use risers to fake height

If all your plants are a similar height, set the back pots on plant stands, upturned pots, sturdy crates or a bench. Raising one pot 12 to 18 in. instantly creates layers without buying a new plant.

Container Garden Arrangement Ideas by Space

Balcony plant arrangement

Keep the floor area clear by putting one tall group in a corner and a long trough along the railing or wall. If your building allows, add railing planters at the same height as the top of the trough to create a continuous green line. Keep heavy pots close to the building wall, and use saucers so water does not drip on neighbors.

Small patio

Frame the seating area with groupings at two corners, or flank a doorway with a matching pair of tall pots. Pairs are the exception to the odd-number rule because symmetry suits entrances. For furniture and rug layout around your pots, see our patio decorating ideas.

Front steps

Place one pot per step, staggered from side to side, or put the largest pot at the bottom and smaller ones on the steps above. Keep at least two-thirds of each step clear for safe walking.

Terraces and rooftops

Larger spaces can use planters to divide zones, such as a row of tall grasses between a dining table and a lounge corner. Choose wind-tolerant plants and lightweight pots that are wide at the base so they do not tip.

Drainage: The Step Most People Skip

Poor drainage kills more container plants than almost anything else.

  • Choose pots with holes. If a decorative pot has none, use it as a cachepot: plant in a plastic nursery pot with holes and set it inside.
  • Skip the gravel layer. A layer of gravel at the bottom does not improve drainage and can raise the wet zone in the soil. Use quality potting mix all the way down, with a piece of mesh over the hole to keep soil in.
  • Use potting mix, not garden soil. Garden soil compacts in containers and is much heavier.
  • Raise pots slightly. Pot feet or small risers help water escape and keep balcony and deck surfaces from staining.
  • Empty saucers. Standing water in saucers can rot roots; empty them after watering or heavy rain.

Renter note: Water stains on balconies and decks can count against your deposit. Use saucers or self-watering planters with reservoirs, add pot feet for airflow, and move pots occasionally to prevent marks.

Watering and Seasonal Care for Grouped Pots

A beautiful arrangement only stays beautiful if the plants thrive. Grouping helps here: pots placed close together shade each other's soil and raise the humidity around the leaves, so they dry out a little more slowly than isolated pots.

  • Check soil, not the calendar. Push a finger about 1 to 2 in. into the mix. If it is dry at that depth, water slowly until it runs from the drainage hole.
  • Expect small pots to dry fastest. In summer heat, an 8 in. terracotta pot in full sun may need water daily, while a 24 in. fiberglass planter may go several days.
  • Use self-watering planters or a simple drip kit on a timer if you travel often.
  • Feed regularly. Container plants run through nutrients faster than plants in the ground; a slow-release fertilizer at planting time plus a liquid feed during the growing season keeps them full.
  • Rotate pots every few weeks so plants grow evenly instead of leaning toward the light.
  • Refresh seasonally. Keep the structural plants (trees, evergreens, grasses) in place year-round and swap only the fillers and spillers for spring, summer, fall and winter color.

In cold climates, move frost-tender pots indoors or against a sheltered wall before the first freeze, and raise terracotta and ceramic off the ground with pot feet so trapped water does not freeze and crack them.

Arranging Potted Plants Indoors

The same principles apply inside. Group three plants of different heights in a corner, use a plant stand to lift a smaller one, and repeat a pot color that ties to your decor. Place sun-loving plants within a few feet of a bright window and shade-tolerant ones farther away. Putting It All Together

Begin with the light and the space, then choose a few large pots in one or two materials. Anchor each group with a tall plant, add medium and low layers in odd numbers, and repeat a color or material to unify everything. Use thriller-filler-spiller in mixed containers, make sure every pot drains, and group plants with the same sun and water needs. Step back, edit, and your containers will look like a planned garden rather than a collection of pots.
