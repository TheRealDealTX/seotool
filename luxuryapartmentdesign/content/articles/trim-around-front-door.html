The best front door trim ideas start with matching the casing style to your home's architecture, then getting the proportions right: casing that's wide enough to frame the door, a head piece that's slightly heavier than the sides, and a paint color that either blends in or makes a confident statement. Done well, trim turns a plain door into an entrance that looks custom.

This guide covers exterior door trim and interior door casing styles, with a comparison table of craftsman, colonial, modern flat, Greek revival and other profiles, plus materials, sizes and paint ideas. If you rent, skip to the apartment front door upgrade section for changes you can make without replacing any trim.

What Is Door Trim (and What's the Difference From Casing)?

Door casing is the molding that covers the gap between the door frame (the jamb) and the wall. Trim is the broader term for all decorative molding, including casing, a head (the top piece), plinth blocks at the bottom, and on exteriors, pilasters and a pediment. On an exterior door, there's also brick molding, a thicker profile used where the frame meets siding or masonry.

Trim does two jobs. Functionally, it covers the rough gap around the frame and, outside, helps shed water. Visually, it frames the door like a picture frame, which is why upgrading it has such a big impact for relatively little material.

Exterior Door Trim Ideas

The trim around an exterior front door has to look good and stand up to rain, sun and temperature swings. A few ideas that suit different homes:

Pilasters and a pediment

Flat or fluted pilasters on either side of the door, topped with a triangular or curved pediment, give a classic colonial or Georgian entrance. Pre-made pediment kits in PVC or polyurethane make this achievable without carpentry skills.

Bold craftsman header

Wide flat casings with a thick head piece that overhangs both sides by about 1 in. look substantial and pair well with a wood-stained door.

Minimal flat trim

On modern homes, slim square trim painted the same color as the siding makes the door itself (often black, natural wood or a saturated color) the focal point.

Transom and sidelight framing

If your door has a transom window above or sidelights beside it, run the trim around the whole unit so it reads as one grand opening.

Whatever you choose, prime all sides and cut ends, caulk the top joints with exterior-grade sealant, and make sure there's flashing above the head trim so water doesn't get behind it.

Interior Door Trim Ideas

Inside, door trim sets the tone for every room it opens into. Some interior door trim ideas to consider:

  • Upgrade the head. Add a simple craftsman-style head and cap above existing casing to make doors look taller.
  • Add plinth blocks. Small blocks at the base of each casing (slightly wider and thicker than the casing) are a traditional detail that also gives baseboards a clean place to stop.
  • Layer a back band. A small molding nailed around the outer edge of plain casing adds depth and shadow, making it look more substantial.
  • Paint the trim and door the same color. Tone-on-tone trim in a deep color (navy, olive, charcoal) looks rich and modern.
  • Match the casing to your baseboards. Consistent profiles and colors throughout an apartment look intentional.

How to Size and Install New Door Casing

If you own your home, replacing casing is a manageable DIY project with a miter saw, a nail gun (or hammer and finishing nails), wood filler and caulk.

  1. Remove old casing. Score the caulk line with a utility knife, then pry gently with a flat bar against a scrap block to protect the wall.
  2. Mark the reveal. Draw a line about 3/16 to 1/4 in. back from the inside edge of the jamb on both sides and the top. The casing sits on this line, leaving a small step called the reveal.
  3. Cut the side pieces. Measure from the floor to the reveal line at the top, then add the reveal. For mitered styles, cut 45-degree angles; for craftsman or flat styles, cut square.
  4. Cut the head. For mitered casing, measure between the outer edges of the sides. For craftsman, cut the head wider so it overhangs the sides evenly.
  5. Nail it up. Use 1.25 in. nails into the jamb and 2 in. nails into the wall framing, about every 12 to 16 in.
  6. Finish. Fill nail holes, caulk the seam between casing and wall, sand lightly and paint.

For semi-gloss trim paint, a quart usually covers several doors' worth of casing. If you're painting walls at the same time, the paint calculator will estimate gallons and primer for the whole room.

Paint Color Ideas for Front Door Trim

Trim color changes how the whole entrance reads:

  • Crisp white trim with a dark door: classic and high contrast. Works with nearly any architecture.
  • Trim matched to the walls or siding: modern and quiet. The door becomes the focus.
  • Trim and door in one deep color: enveloping and luxurious, especially in navy, deep green or charcoal.
  • Soft color trim: a pale sage, blush or warm greige casing against white walls feels fresh. See our guide to pastel colors for home for shades that work on trim.

Use a semi-gloss or satin finish on trim: it wipes clean and the slight sheen shows off the profile. Test palette combinations with the room color palette generator before buying.

Apartment Front Door Upgrade Ideas for Renters

If you rent, the exterior trim is the landlord's, and the hallway side of your front door usually has to match the rest of the building. But the inside of your apartment front door is a big, visible surface you can improve.

Renter note: Always get written permission before painting a door or trim, and keep everything you remove. Most building fire codes don't allow changes to the closer, latch or fire rating of an apartment entry door, so leave those alone.

  • Paint the inside, with permission. A deep color on the interior face of the door (and its casing) turns it into a feature. Ask whether you'll need to repaint it before you move out.
  • Use peel-and-stick molding. Self-adhesive panel molding can create a raised-panel look on a flat door. Test removal on a hidden spot first, since some adhesives pull paint.
  • Swap the hardware. Change the interior lever, the deadbolt thumb-turn trim (not the lock mechanism itself) and door stop to a finish like brushed brass or matte black. Store the originals.
  • Add a full-length mirror. An over-door or leaning mirror near the entry bounces light and is handy on your way out.
  • Upgrade the door mat. A thick coir or flatweave mat inside the door, sized slightly narrower than the door, makes the entry feel finished.
  • Light the entry. A plug-in wall sconce or a small lamp on a console beside the door creates a warm welcome. The room lighting calculator helps you plan.
  • Hang hooks or a slim shelf. Adhesive hooks or an over-door rack keep coats and keys organized; see our hidden key cabinet idea for a tidy alternative.

Bringing Your Front Door Trim Together

Start with the style of your home and pick a casing profile from the table that suits it. Size up from builder-grade widths if you can, keep the head slightly heavier than the sides, and use paint to decide whether the trim blends in or stands out. Renters can get a similar effect inside the front door with permission-based paint, peel-and-stick molding, new hardware, a good mat and warm lighting.
