To repair drywall after removing wallpaper, you need to do four things in order: wash off every trace of old adhesive, cut away loose drywall paper, seal any torn brown paper with an oil-based or shellac-based primer (or a dedicated gypsum sealer), and only then fill and skim coat with joint compound before priming and painting. The sealing step is the one most people skip, and it's why patched walls end up with bubbles and blisters.

The reason is simple. The brown kraft paper under drywall's smooth face layer soaks up water like a sponge. Joint compound and latex primers are water-based, so when they hit exposed brown paper, the paper swells and lifts into bubbles. A solvent-based or shellac-based primer soaks in, dries hard and locks the fibers down so the compound has a stable surface to grab.

Below is the full process, with the tools, products and drying times, plus how to tell if you need a full skim coat or just spot repairs.

Why Drywall Gets Damaged When You Remove Wallpaper

Drywall is a gypsum core wrapped in paper. The outer face paper is thin and smooth; under it is a coarser brown layer. When wallpaper paste bonds tightly to the face paper (common when walls weren't primed or sized before the paper went up), stripping the wallpaper takes some of that face layer with it.

The most common types of damage are:

  • Torn face paper exposing fuzzy brown paper underneath.
  • Adhesive residue, a slick or tacky film that causes paint to crack, wrinkle or peel later.
  • Gouges and scrapes from putty knives or scoring tools.
  • Bubbled or delaminated paper where the drywall got too wet during removal.
  • Exposed seams and nail pops that wallpaper had been hiding.

Step 1: Remove All Wallpaper Adhesive Residue

Paint applied over old paste can crack in a pattern that looks like dried mud, sometimes weeks later. So the first job is cleaning.

  1. Mix warm water with a small amount of dish soap or a wallpaper-removal solution.
  2. Wipe a section with a well-wrung sponge, let it sit a minute to soften the paste, then wipe again with clean water.
  3. Rinse the sponge often and change the water when it gets cloudy.
  4. Let the wall dry, then run your hand over it. If it feels slick or tacky, or a drop of water beads instead of soaking in, there's still residue. Repeat.

Work gently around areas that are already torn, since water will make the brown paper worse. Let the wall dry completely, at least overnight, before the next step.

Renter note: If your apartment came with wallpaper you didn't install, get written permission before stripping it and repairing walls. Some older buildings also have paint or adhesive layers that may contain lead or asbestos; ask your landlord or building manager before sanding anything in a pre-1980s building.

Step 2: Cut Away Loose Drywall Paper

Any paper that is lifting, bubbled or flapping has to go, because compound applied over it will crack or peel off along with the loose paper.

  • Use a sharp utility knife to slice around the loose area at a shallow angle, then peel or cut it away until you reach paper that is firmly attached.
  • Don't dig into the gypsum core. You only want to remove what's loose.
  • Lightly knock down fuzzy fibers with a dry sanding sponge. Don't sand aggressively; you'll just raise more fuzz.

Step 3: Seal Torn Drywall Paper Before Joint Compound

This is how to fix torn drywall paper so it stays fixed. Exposed brown paper must be sealed with a primer that won't re-wet it.

Which primer to use

  • Shellac-based primer: dries in under an hour, seals extremely well and also blocks stains and leftover adhesive. It has a strong alcohol smell, so ventilate well.
  • Oil-based (alkyd) primer: seals torn paper reliably and is easy to find. It takes longer to dry (often several hours or overnight; follow the can) and needs mineral spirits for cleanup.
  • Dedicated gypsum or drywall sealer: a penetrating, water-thin sealer made specifically for damaged drywall paper. It soaks in and hardens the surface, and many versions are low odor.

What not to use here: standard latex primer, PVA drywall primer or joint compound straight on the brown paper. They're water-based and are exactly what causes the bubbling you're trying to avoid.

How to apply it

  • Brush or roll the primer over the torn areas, extending 1 to 2 in. onto the sound paper around them.
  • For walls with lots of patchy damage or doubtful residue, prime the whole wall.
  • Let it dry fully according to the label.
  • Check for bubbles. If any appear, cut them out, lightly sand and spot-prime again.

Designer tip: Shellac primer in a spray can is the quickest fix for a handful of small tears. Spray a light coat, wait about 30 to 45 minutes, and you can usually start on compound in the same afternoon.

Step 4: Fill Gouges and Spot Repairs

With the paper sealed, fill the deeper damage first.

  1. Use a 6 in. knife to press joint compound into gouges and torn areas, pulling the knife flat to leave a thin, smooth layer.
  2. Re-set any popped screws or nails just below the surface and cover them.
  3. Let it dry completely. Lightweight compound usually takes about 24 hours; deep fills take longer. Setting-type compound ("hot mud") hardens in a set time, such as 20 or 45 minutes, and is good for deeper holes.
  4. Apply a second, wider coat if the first one shrank or left a dip.

Do You Need a Skim Coat After Wallpaper Removal?

A skim coat is a thin layer of joint compound over the whole wall that creates a fresh, uniform surface. You need one when:

  • More than about a quarter of the wall has torn paper or patches.
  • Patches would show through paint as dull or shiny spots (called flashing), especially with eggshell or satin paint.
  • The wall will be lit from the side by a window or sconce, which exaggerates every ridge.

If damage is limited to a few spots, careful spot repairs and a good primer are usually enough.

How to skim coat a wall

  1. Thin the compound. Mix lightweight or topping compound with a little water until it's about the consistency of thick yogurt.
  2. Apply. Spread it with a 10–12 in. knife or a paint roller with a thick nap, working in sections of about 3 x 3 ft.
  3. Smooth. Pull a wide knife across the wall at a low angle to leave a very thin coat, about the thickness of a credit card.
  4. Dry and sand. Let it dry overnight, then sand lightly with 120–150 grit.
  5. Repeat. Apply a second coat in the opposite direction if needed, then do a final light sand with 180–220 grit.
  6. Check with a light. Hold a work light flat against the wall to spot ridges and low spots, mark them with a pencil and touch up.

Step 5: Prime Damaged Drywall Before Painting

Fresh joint compound is very porous, and paint applied directly over it will look patchy. Once all compound is dry and sanded, wipe the dust off with a barely damp cloth or vacuum brush, then prime the whole wall.

Choosing paint and sheen for repaired walls

Sheen affects how forgiving the wall will be. Flat and matte finishes hide small imperfections best, which makes them a smart choice after wallpaper removal. Eggshell is a good middle ground for living rooms and bedrooms, while satin and semi-gloss show every ridge but are easier to wipe in kitchens and baths.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Putting joint compound straight on torn paper. It bubbles. Seal first.
  • Skipping residue cleanup. Paint can crack or wrinkle over old paste.
  • Oversoaking the wall during removal. Too much water delaminates the face paper.
  • Thick coats of compound. Several thin coats dry faster, crack less and sand easier.
  • Painting before compound is dry. Darker, cool spots on the wall mean it's still wet inside.
  • Skipping primer over compound. Patches will flash through the paint.
