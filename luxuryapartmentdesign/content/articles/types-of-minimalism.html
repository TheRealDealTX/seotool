There are several types of minimalism, and they look very different. In interiors, the main minimalist interior design styles are classic modern minimalism, warm minimalism, Scandinavian minimalism, Japanese minimalism, Japandi, organic minimalism, industrial minimalism and maximal minimalism. All of them share a love of clear surfaces and fewer, better things. Where they differ is in color, materials and how much warmth and personality they let in.

Minimalism is also a way of living, so this guide briefly covers the lifestyle types too: aesthetic, intentional, digital and more. Mostly, though, it's about how each style looks in an apartment, how to tell them apart (including the frequently confused Japandi vs. Scandinavian minimalism), and how to choose and create the one that suits you.

What All Types of Minimalism Have in Common

Whatever the flavor, minimalist rooms tend to share a few principles:

  • Function first: every piece earns its place by being useful, beautiful or both.
  • Negative space: empty walls, clear floors and bare surfaces are part of the design, not leftover gaps.
  • A restrained palette: usually three to five colors, often neutral.
  • Clean lines: simple silhouettes with little ornament.
  • Hidden storage: clutter goes behind closed doors so the room stays calm.
  • Quality over quantity: fewer objects, chosen carefully.

The differences come from what each style adds back in: how much wood, texture, color, pattern and personality it allows.

Minimalist Interior Design Styles Explained

1. Modern (classic) minimalism

This is the gallery-like version: white walls, hard-edged furniture, glossy or matte black accents, and almost no decor. It looks striking in photos but can feel cold in practice. To live with it, add one soft textile layer, like a wool rug or linen curtains, and warm 2700K bulbs.

2. Warm minimalism

Warm minimalism keeps the restraint and adds comfort. Picture cream walls, a low bouclé or linen sofa, oak or walnut wood, plaster-look lamps and a soft wool rug. It's the most livable version for most people and works especially well in rentals with beige carpet or warm-toned floors. It shares a lot with quiet luxury interior design, which adds richer materials and a few heirloom-feeling pieces.

3. Scandinavian minimalism

Developed in countries with long, dark winters, Scandinavian design maximizes light with white walls, pale woods like birch and ash, and simple, practical furniture. It allows a bit more coziness and color than pure modern minimalism: sheepskins, knitted throws, candles and the odd soft pastel or muted blue.

4. Japanese minimalism

Rooted in Zen principles and the idea of ma, the meaningful use of empty space, Japanese minimalism uses low furniture, natural materials such as cedar, bamboo, paper and stone, sliding screens and very few objects. Wabi-sabi, the appreciation of imperfection and age, means handmade ceramics and natural wear are welcome.

5. Japandi

Japandi blends Japanese and Scandinavian minimalism: Scandinavian comfort and function with Japanese craft, darker accents and serenity. Expect low wooden furniture, a mix of light and dark woods, linen, rattan, handmade ceramics, a few large plants, and a natural-fiber rug. Our guide to choosing a Japandi rug covers the materials and sizes that suit the style.

6. Organic minimalism

Organic minimalism leans into nature: curved, sculptural shapes, travertine, raw wood, clay and jute, sun-washed earth tones, and lots of plants. It feels relaxed and slightly Mediterranean.

7. Industrial minimalism

Raw architecture does the decorating: exposed brick, concrete, ductwork and steel windows, balanced with leather, wood and minimal furniture. It suits loft apartments but can be faked in standard rentals with black metal frames, a leather sofa and concrete-look accessories.

8. Maximal minimalism

A newer approach: the same uncluttered surfaces and few objects, but each one is bold, like a saturated sofa, a large colorful artwork or a sculptural chair. It's minimal in quantity, not in personality.

Japandi vs. Scandinavian Minimalism: What's the Difference?

These two get confused more than any other pair, because Japandi borrows so much from Scandinavian design. The differences are mostly in color, wood tone and mood.

FeatureScandinavian minimalismJapandi
Color paletteBright whites, pale grays, soft pastelsWarmer neutrals, plus charcoal, olive, clay and black
Wood tonesMostly light (birch, ash, pine)Light and dark mixed (oak with walnut or stained wood)
Furniture heightStandard heightOften lower, closer to the floor
Decor quantityCozy layers: throws, candles, cushionsFewer, more sculptural pieces
Craft influenceMid-century Nordic design, hygge comfortJapanese craft, wabi-sabi imperfection
Overall moodBright, homey, airyCalm, grounded, contemplative

In short, if you love bright, airy rooms and lots of cozy textiles, Scandinavian minimalism will suit you. If you prefer a moodier, more grounded and pared-back room with darker accents, choose Japandi.

Designer tip: The quickest way to turn a Scandinavian room into a Japandi one is to add a dark element (a walnut side table, a charcoal throw or a black-framed print), lower the furniture height, and remove half the accessories.

What Is Warm Minimalism, and Why Is It So Popular?

Warm minimalism has become one of the most popular minimalist interior design styles because it fixes classic minimalism's biggest problem: rooms that feel cold and unlivable. It keeps clean lines and clear surfaces but adds:

  • A warm white or cream base instead of stark white.
  • Plenty of wood, from light oak to walnut.
  • Soft, tactile fabrics: bouclé, linen, wool, sheepskin.
  • Rounded edges and curved silhouettes.
  • Layered warm light from lamps rather than overhead fixtures.

For apartment dwellers, it's also forgiving. Warm minimalism works with beige carpet, honey-toned floors and standard white walls. You can add it with textiles and lamps alone. Use our room color palette generator with a cream or camel base to build a warm minimalist scheme.

Lifestyle Types of Minimalism

Beyond decor, people describe minimalism as a way of living. These types often overlap with how your home looks:

  • Aesthetic minimalism: focused on a clean, uncluttered visual style. This is what the interior styles above describe.
  • Intentional minimalism: owning only what adds value, without strict counts or rules. The most common and sustainable approach.
  • Essentialism or extreme minimalism: paring belongings down to the bare minimum, sometimes counting every item.
  • Nomadic minimalism: living out of a suitcase or small space while traveling or moving often.
  • Digital minimalism: reducing apps, notifications and screen time.
  • Sustainable or eco minimalism: buying less, buying secondhand and choosing durable, natural materials.
  • Cozy minimalism: a simple home that still prioritizes comfort, candles, books and soft textiles, close to warm minimalism and hygge.

You don't need to adopt a whole lifestyle to decorate minimally, but a round of decluttering makes any minimalist style easier to keep up. Our declutter checklist walks you through it room by room.

How to Choose the Right Type of Minimalism for You

  • Look at your light: dim, north-facing apartments suit Scandinavian or warm minimalism. Bright, sunny spaces can handle organic or Japandi's darker accents.
  • Look at your fixed finishes: warm wood floors and beige carpet pair with warm minimalism or Japandi. Concrete and exposed brick suit industrial.
  • Be honest about your stuff: if you own lots of books and keepsakes, warm or cozy minimalism with closed storage is more realistic than modern minimalism.
  • Think about comfort: if you want to curl up on the sofa, choose a softer style over a gallery-like one.
  • Gather images: save 10 to 15 rooms you love, then look for the common palette and materials.

How to Create a Minimalist Apartment, Step by Step

  1. Declutter first. Remove anything you don't use or love before you buy anything.
  2. Set a palette. Pick a base color, a secondary color and one accent, following roughly 60-30-10.
  3. Invest in a few key pieces. A good sofa, bed and rug matter more than accessories.
  4. Add closed storage. Cabinets, lidded baskets and under-bed drawers keep surfaces clear.
  5. Layer warm light. Use 2700K bulbs and at least three light sources per room.
  6. Add texture. In a pared-back room, linen, wool, wood and stone supply the interest that pattern would elsewhere.
  7. Edit again. Leave about a third of each surface empty and resist filling every wall.

Renter note: Minimalism suits renters well, because most of it comes down to what you remove. Hide dated fixtures with large natural rugs, plain full-length curtains and a few large leaned artworks rather than many small nail holes.

Finding Your Version of Minimalism

None of these types of minimalism is the correct one. Classic modern suits people who love crisp architecture; warm minimalism and Japandi suit people who want calm without coldness; Scandinavian suits bright, cozy homes; and maximal minimalism suits people who love color but hate clutter. Pick the version that fits your light, your belongings and the way you live, and let the rest go.
