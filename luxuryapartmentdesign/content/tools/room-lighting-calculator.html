How the Room Lighting Calculator Works

The calculator uses one simple formula: lumens = room square footage × foot-candle target. A foot-candle is one lumen spread across one square foot, so multiplying area by the target brightness gives the total light output your fixtures should produce. Different rooms need different targets because the activities in them differ: you need far more light to shave or chop vegetables than to watch a movie.

Once it has a lumen total, the tool divides by 800 lumens, the output of a standard 60-watt-equivalent LED bulb, and rounds up to give a bulb count. It then splits that light into layers so you know how much should come from ceiling fixtures, lamps, and accent lights.

Example: a 12 by 15 foot living room is 180 square feet. At 10 to 20 foot-candles, it needs 1,800 to 3,600 lumens. At the top of that range, 3,600 ÷ 800 = 4.5, so plan on about five 800-lumen bulbs spread across the room.

Layering: Ambient, Task and Accent

A single overhead fixture providing all the light is what makes many apartments feel flat and institutional. The calculator divides your total into three layers, starting from about 50 percent ambient, 30 percent task, and 20 percent accent.

  • Ambient (about 50%): general light from ceiling fixtures, flush mounts, pendants, or bright floor lamps that bounce light off the ceiling.
  • Task (about 30%): focused light where you do things, such as reading lamps beside the sofa, under-cabinet strips in the kitchen, a desk lamp, or vanity lights.
  • Accent (about 20%): picture lights, uplights behind plants, LED strips in shelving, or candles and string lights for glow. Our guide to fairy lights in the living room shows how to use soft accent light without looking dorm-like, and fairy lights for the bedroom does the same for sleeping spaces.

Task light should sit close to where you need it: a reading lamp's shade bottom roughly at shoulder height when you are seated, or under-cabinet strips toward the front edge of the cabinet so light falls on the counter rather than the backsplash. In kitchens and bathrooms, task areas need more light than the room average, so the calculator's task share matters most there.

In a 3,600-lumen living room, that works out to roughly 1,800 lumens of ambient, 1,080 of task, and 720 of accent light.

Choosing the Right Color Temperature

Color temperature, measured in kelvins (K), describes how warm or cool white light looks. Lower numbers are warmer and more golden; higher numbers are cooler and bluer.

  • 2700K warm white: living rooms and bedrooms. It is flattering, relaxing, and closest to the incandescent glow that makes rooms feel expensive.
  • 3000K soft white: kitchens and bathrooms, where you want clarity without a clinical feel.
  • 3500–4000K neutral white: home offices and workspaces for alert, focused light.

Keep color temperature consistent within a room. Mixing 2700K lamps with a 5000K ceiling light makes both look wrong. Look for bulbs with a high color rendering index (CRI 90 or above) so fabrics, wood, and skin tones look natural.

Designer tip: Put as many lights as possible on dimmers. Sizing for the top of the lumen range and then dimming down gives you bright light for cleaning and a warm, low glow for evenings. Plug-in dimmers and smart bulbs work in rentals with no rewiring.

Lighting a Rental Without Rewiring

Most apartments come with a single builder-grade ceiling fixture, and many have none in the living room at all. You can still hit your lumen targets with plug-in pieces:

  • Use two or three table and floor lamps per living room, placed in different corners.
  • Add plug-in wall sconces with cord covers painted to match the wall.
  • Stick battery or plug-in LED strips under kitchen cabinets for task light.
  • Use picture lights or a small uplight to highlight art and plants.
  • Swap the existing ceiling fixture for a nicer one, keeping the original to reinstall when you move.
