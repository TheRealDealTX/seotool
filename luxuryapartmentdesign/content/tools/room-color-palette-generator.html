How the 60-30-10 Rule Shapes Your Palette

The generator always returns three main colors in a 60-30-10 split, because that proportion is the simplest reliable way to make a room feel balanced. The 60 percent color covers walls and the largest surfaces, so it is usually the quietest shade. The 30 percent color goes on furniture, rugs, curtains, and bedding, adding contrast without competing with the walls. The 10 percent accent shows up in pillows, art, vases, and small decor, giving the eye somewhere to land.

The tool also suggests a trim white that suits your base color. A warm palette gets a creamy white with a touch of yellow; a cool palette gets a cleaner, crisper white. The wrong white is one of the most common reasons a palette looks off, so do not skip it.

The Six Color Harmonies Explained

A harmony is a rule for choosing colors based on their positions on the color wheel. Each one produces a different mood.

  • Monochromatic: tints and shades of one hue. Serene and sophisticated, ideal for bedrooms and quiet luxury looks.
  • Analogous: neighbors on the wheel, such as blue, blue-green, and green. Relaxed and natural.
  • Complementary: opposites, such as navy and cognac orange. High contrast and energetic, best with the bold color kept to the 10 percent slot.
  • Split-complementary: a base plus the two hues beside its opposite. Vivid but easier to balance than pure complementary.
  • Triadic: three hues evenly spaced around the wheel. Playful; works best when the generator softens them toward muted versions.
  • Warm neutral: your base color paired with greige, cream, and camel. The safest choice for open-plan apartments and rentals with beige carpet or honey floors.

Undertones and LRV: Why Swatches Lie

Two colors with the same name can look completely different on your wall because of undertone, the subtle hue beneath the main shade. Greige can lean green, pink, or violet; white can lean yellow, blue, or gray. Hold samples against your floor, countertops, and largest fabrics, and choose colors whose undertones agree.

LRV (Light Reflectance Value) runs from 0 for black to 100 for pure white and tells you how much light a paint reflects. As a rule of thumb, colors above about 60 keep a room bright, the 30s to 50s read as true midtones, and anything below about 20 feels dramatic. Dim, north-facing rooms usually benefit from a higher-LRV 60 percent color, while a sunny room can carry something deeper. Designer tip: Screen colors are a starting point only. Buy peel-and-stick samples or paint a poster board at least 2 by 2 feet (60 by 60 cm), and move it around the room. Check it in morning light, late afternoon, and at night under your own bulbs before you buy a gallon.

Testing Your Palette in Real Light

Light direction changes everything. North-facing rooms get cool, even light that pushes colors toward gray and blue, so warm undertones help. South-facing rooms get warm, strong light that intensifies yellows and can wash out pale colors. East-facing rooms are bright in the morning and cool later; west-facing rooms are the reverse. Your bulbs matter too: warm 2700K bulbs make cream and blush glow but can muddy cool grays.

Test the 30 and 10 percent colors as fabric swatches or decor, not just paint. If you rent and cannot paint, apply the palette entirely through textiles, art, and furniture, using the existing wall color as your 60 percent.
