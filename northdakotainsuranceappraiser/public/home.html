01Claim calendar

Four seasons, four kinds of claims

A North Dakota Insurance Appraiser sees the year in claim types. Knowing which season produced the damage shapes what to look for — and what the carrier may have missed.

Spring Mar–May

Snowmelt, ice jams and saturated ground. Sump failures, seepage and roof leaks from late-season ice dams.

Summer Jun–Aug

Peak hail and severe-storm season: shingles, siding, windows, metal buildings — plus straight-line winds and tornadoes.

Fall Sep–Nov

Dry, windy days raise grass-fire risk out west, and a race to finish repairs before freeze-up.

Winter Dec–Feb

Blizzards, frozen pipes, ice dams and heavy snow load on roofs and outbuildings.

04The route

From disagreement to signed award

The appraisal clause turns an open-ended argument into a defined process with a binding result. Here’s the route North Dakota Insurance Appraiser follows on a typical file.

  1. 1

    Define exactly what’s disputed

    Confirm that the dispute is about the amount of loss, not coverage, and list exactly which items and dollar figures are contested.

  2. 2

    Written appraisal demand

    Send a written demand that invokes the appraisal clause, cites the policy and claim number, and follows any notice rules in the policy.

  3. 3

    Both appraisers are appointed

    The policyholder and the insurer each select a competent, disinterested appraiser within the window the policy sets.

  4. 4

    Umpire selection

    The two appraisers try to agree on a neutral umpire, and if they cannot, either side may ask a court of record to appoint one.

  5. 5

    Inspection & independent estimates

    Each appraiser inspects the property, reviews the claim file, and builds an independent, line-by-line estimate of the loss.

  6. 6

    Appraiser negotiation

    The appraisers compare estimates item by item, resolve what they can, and narrow the remaining differences to a short list.

  7. 7

    Umpire review

    Any items the appraisers cannot settle go to the umpire, who reviews both positions and decides each disputed amount.

  8. 8

    Signed award & payment

    An award signed by any two of the three panel members sets the amount of loss, and the insurer then pays under the policy terms.

06Regions

Five claim climates in one state

Hail in the valley, river ice in the capital, wind in the oil patch, grass fire in the badlands. A North Dakota Insurance Appraiser has to know how each region’s weather shows up on a building — and in an estimate.

Red River Valley

Fargo · West Fargo · Grand Forks · Wahpeton

  • Large hail
  • Spring flooding
  • Straight-line wind
  • Basement water

The flattest, most populated corner of the state. Severe summer storms track straight across the valley, and snowmelt years bring sump, foundation and sewer-backup questions — many of them coverage issues, not appraisal issues.

Local pages: Fargo · West Fargo · Grand Forks · Wahpeton

Lakes & Drift Prairie

Devils Lake · Jamestown · Valley City

  • Hail on siding
  • Ice dams
  • Blizzards
  • Wind

Small cities, farmsteads and lake homes spread across the prairie. Claims here often mix a house, a detached garage and an outbuilding or two — and adjusters can be days away after a big storm.

Local pages: Devils Lake · Jamestown · Valley City

Capital Corridor

Bismarck · Mandan

  • Hail
  • River ice & high water
  • Snow load
  • Fire

The capital region sits where the Heart River meets the Missouri River. Older homes near downtown, fast-growing subdivisions on the edges and a commercial core produce a wide mix of roof, siding and interior claims.

Local pages: Bismarck · Mandan

Northwest & Bakken

Minot · Williston

  • Wind
  • Frozen pipes
  • Hail
  • Flood aftermath

From the Souris River valley to the oil patch. Rapid growth added apartments, workforce housing and steel commercial buildings — construction types that each price differently in an estimate.

Local pages: Minot · Williston

Southwest & Badlands

Dickinson · Medora

  • Grass fire
  • Wind
  • Hail
  • Extreme cold

Open rangeland, ranch buildings and oil-country towns. Dry, windy autumns raise grass-fire exposure, and long distances make careful documentation more important than ever.

Local pages: Dickinson

09Right help

Which kind of help fits your claim?

Each solves a different problem, and picking the right one first saves time and fees. North Dakota Insurance Appraiser will point you elsewhere when appraisal isn’t the right tool.

Party-appointed appraiser

Values the amount of loss

  • Coverage accepted, number disputed
  • Works inside the appraisal clause
  • Binding award with two of three signatures
  • Doesn’t decide coverage or bad faith

Licensed public adjuster

Manages and negotiates the claim

  • Most useful early, from first notice
  • Prepares and presents the claim
  • Negotiated settlement
  • Isn’t a lawyer

Insurance coverage lawyer

Handles legal disputes

  • Denials and coverage fights
  • Bad-faith and deadline questions
  • Settlement or judgment
  • Usually the costliest route

11Questions

Straight answers from a North Dakota Insurance Appraiser

Straight answers about how appraisal works, what it costs and when it’s the wrong move.

All questions Terms A–Z
What does a North Dakota Insurance Appraiser do?

We value property damage when you and your insurer agree the loss is covered but disagree on what it costs. As your appointed appraiser we inspect, build an independent line-item estimate, work with the carrier’s appraiser and, if needed, present unresolved items to a neutral umpire.

How is that different from a public adjuster?

A public adjuster handles and negotiates the whole claim. An appraiser works inside the policy’s appraisal clause on one question — the amount of loss. We don’t negotiate outside appraisal and we don’t give legal advice.

When should I call a North Dakota Insurance Appraiser?

When coverage is accepted, you have the carrier’s itemized estimate, and the gap between it and a contractor’s estimate is meaningful — generally more than about 10% or several thousand dollars.

Is the award binding?

On the amount of loss, generally yes, once two of the three panel members sign. That’s why the file should be complete before anyone demands appraisal.

What does it cost?

Each side usually pays its own appraiser and half of the umpire. Our fee is put in writing before work begins, and the first conversation is free.

Can winter damage wait until spring?

Report it now and protect the property; policy clocks keep running. Snow-covered roofs can be re-inspected after thaw, and that follow-up often matters for the scope.
