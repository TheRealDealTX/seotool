SBA loan eligibility: who qualifies in 2026

The SBA's basic eligibility rules, the businesses it won't finance, and what lenders add on top.

To get an SBA loan, a business generally has to be a for-profit company operating in the U.S., meet the SBA's size standard for its industry, show it can repay from cash flow, and have owners who invest their own money and have reasonable credit. Some business types are ineligible no matter how strong they are.

The core SBA eligibility requirements

RequirementWhat it means in practice
For-profit businessNonprofits can't get 7(a) or 504 loans. (Some nonprofit child-care centers are a narrow exception for microloans.)
Operates in the U.S.The business is located in and operates mainly in the United States or its territories.
Small by SBA standardsUnder the size standard for your NAICS code, measured by revenue or employees, including affiliates. An alternative test (tangible net worth under $20 million and net income under $6.5 million) also works for 7(a) and 504.
Ability to repayCash flow covers the new payment with room to spare. Most lenders want a debt service coverage ratio of at least 1.15 to 1.25.
Owner equityOwners have money in the business. Start-ups and business purchases generally need at least 10% equity injection.
Owner character and creditOwners of 20% or more give a personal guarantee. Lenders review credit history, and owners can't be delinquent on federal debt.
Sound business purposeThe money goes to an eligible use: working capital, equipment, real estate, a business purchase or refinancing.

Businesses that can't get an SBA loan

The SBA bars certain business types regardless of size or credit. The most common ones borrowers run into:

  • Passive businesses that only hold real estate or investments to collect rent or returns, such as landlords leasing to unrelated tenants. (An eligible operating company that occupies its own building is fine.)
  • Lending and investment businesses: lenders, pawn shops that mainly lend, investment funds.
  • Speculative businesses such as oil wildcatting or buying property to flip.
  • Gambling businesses that get more than a third of revenue from gambling.
  • Businesses that are illegal under federal law, which includes cannabis businesses even where state law allows them.
  • Pyramid sales plans, private clubs that restrict membership for reasons other than capacity, and businesses primarily engaged in political or lobbying activities.
  • Government-owned entities and most businesses owned by people on parole or incarcerated (rules here were relaxed in recent years, so ask).

Eligibility vs. approval

Eligible doesn't mean approved. The SBA sets the floor, and the lender decides. A lender will look at the "five Cs": character, capacity (cash flow), capital (your equity), collateral and conditions (your industry and the economy). The SBA doesn't require full collateral, and a loan of $50,000 or less needs none, but a lender will take what is available on larger loans.

Start-ups and business buyers

Start-ups are eligible for 7(a), 504 and microloans. Expect to show direct industry or management experience, a business plan with monthly projections for the first year, and at least 10% equity. Buying an existing business is one of the most common uses of a 7(a) loan; the seller can carry part of the equity on a standby note in some cases.

Questions

What credit score do I need for an SBA loan?

The SBA doesn't set a minimum personal credit score. Most banks want to see scores in the high 600s or above, and many 7(a) loans under $350,000 are also screened with a FICO SBSS business score. Mission lenders and microlenders work with lower scores.

Can a new business get an SBA loan?

Yes. Start-ups are eligible for 7(a), 504 and microloans, but lenders want relevant experience, a solid plan with projections, and at least 10% equity. A microloan or SBA Express loan is often the realistic first step.

Are nonprofits eligible for SBA loans?

Not for 7(a) or 504 loans, which require a for-profit business. Nonprofit child-care centers can use SBA microloans, and nonprofits can apply for SBA disaster loans.

Can I get an SBA loan with a past bankruptcy?

Often, yes. A bankruptcy isn't an automatic bar, but lenders will want it discharged, an explanation, and clean credit since. Several years of distance helps a lot.

Do I need collateral to qualify?

No. The SBA won't decline a loan only for lack of collateral, and loans of $50,000 or less don't require any. On larger loans the lender must take available collateral, which can include business assets and a lien on your home.
