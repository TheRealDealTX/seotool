That sharp, stabbing pain in your heel with your first steps out of bed is a classic sign of plantar fasciitis. It happens when the thick band of tissue running along the bottom of your foot, the plantar fascia, becomes irritated. The good news is that most cases get better with simple home care, and plantar fasciitis stretches are one of the most useful tools you have. This guide walks you through seven stretches, a quick morning routine, and the footwear habits that help your heel recover.

Key takeaways

  • Stretch the plantar fascia and calves two to three times a day.
  • Do a short routine before your first steps in the morning.
  • Stretch to a gentle pull, never to sharp pain.
  • Supportive shoes and avoiding barefoot walking on hard floors help a lot.
  • See a doctor if pain lasts more than a few weeks or is severe.

What Causes Plantar Fasciitis?

The plantar fascia connects your heel bone to the base of your toes and supports your arch. Repeated stress can irritate it, especially where it attaches at the heel. Common contributors include:

  • Tight calf muscles and Achilles tendon
  • Sudden increases in walking, running, or standing time
  • Flat feet or very high arches
  • Worn-out or unsupportive shoes
  • Extra body weight
  • Jobs that keep you on your feet on hard surfaces

Pain is usually worst with the first steps in the morning or after sitting, because the fascia tightens while you rest. That is why stretching before you stand up can make such a difference.

7 Plantar Fasciitis Stretches

Move slowly, breathe, and aim for a gentle stretch. A timer helps you hold each stretch long enough without counting.

1. Seated Plantar Fascia Stretch

Targets: plantar fascia, arch3 x 30 seconds per foot

Sit and cross your affected foot over the opposite knee. Grab your toes and gently pull them back toward your shin until you feel a stretch along the bottom of your foot. With your other hand, you can feel the fascia tighten like a band under your arch. Hold, release, and repeat.

2. Towel Stretch

Targets: calf, plantar fascia3 x 30 seconds per foot

Sit on the floor or bed with your legs straight. Loop a towel or strap around the ball of your foot and hold both ends. Keep your knee straight and gently pull the towel toward you until you feel a stretch in your calf and foot. This one is ideal before getting out of bed.

3. Wall Calf Stretch (Straight Knee)

Targets: gastrocnemius (upper calf)3 x 30 seconds per side

Face a wall with your hands at shoulder height. Step your affected foot back, keep the heel flat and the knee straight, and lean forward until you feel a stretch in your upper calf. Keep your back toes pointing straight ahead.

4. Wall Calf Stretch (Bent Knee)

Targets: soleus (lower calf), Achilles3 x 30 seconds per side

Set up like the previous stretch, but bring the back foot a little closer and bend both knees slightly while keeping the back heel down. You should feel the stretch lower in your calf, near the Achilles tendon. This deeper muscle is often tight in people with heel pain.

5. Step Stretch

Targets: calves, Achilles, plantar fascia3 x 20-30 seconds

Stand on the edge of a stair with the balls of your feet on the step and your heels hanging off. Hold the railing. Slowly lower your heels below the step until you feel a stretch, hold, then rise back to level. Keep it gentle and controlled, and skip it if it causes sharp heel pain.

6. Frozen Bottle Roll

Targets: plantar fascia, pain relief1-2 minutes per foot

Freeze a water bottle. While seated, roll it slowly under your arch from heel to toes with light to moderate pressure. The combination of massage and cold can ease soreness, especially after activity. A tennis ball or massage ball works when you do not need the cold.

7. Toe Extension Stretch

Targets: plantar fascia, toe flexors3 x 20 seconds per foot

Kneel or stand and place your toes against a wall with your heel on the floor, so your big toe bends upward. Gently lean forward until you feel a stretch under your arch. You can also do this seated by pressing your toes back against the floor.

A 5-Minute Morning Routine

Do this before your feet ever touch the floor. It warms up the fascia so your first steps hurt less.

  1. Ankle pumps (30 seconds). Lying in bed, point and flex your feet to get blood moving.
  2. Towel stretch (1 minute per foot). Use a towel or belt you keep by the bed.
  3. Seated plantar fascia stretch (1 minute per foot). Sit on the edge of the bed and pull your toes back.
  4. Foot roll (30 seconds per foot). Roll a massage ball under each arch while still seated.
  5. Stand up in supportive shoes or sandals. Avoid walking barefoot on hard floors.
Tip: Repeat a quick version of this routine after long periods of sitting, like a work meeting or a drive.

Footwear Tips for Heel Pain

What you wear on your feet matters as much as stretching. A few changes can take pressure off the fascia while it heals:

  • Choose arch support and cushioning. Look for a supportive midsole and a firm heel counter.
  • Avoid flat, flimsy shoes. Flip-flops, worn-out sneakers, and completely flat shoes offer little support.
  • Wear shoes at home. Supportive house shoes or recovery sandals beat going barefoot on tile or hardwood.
  • Replace worn shoes. Running shoes lose support as their foam breaks down.
  • Consider inserts. Over-the-counter arch supports or heel cups help many people.

If you have flat feet, which can strain the plantar fascia, check out our guide on how to choose running shoes for flat feet and add a few flat feet exercises to strengthen your arches.

Other Ways to Ease Plantar Fasciitis

  • Reduce high-impact activity for a while. Swap some running for cycling or swimming.
  • Ice after activity. The frozen bottle roll does double duty here.
  • Build back gradually. When you return to running or long walks, increase volume slowly. Our beginner's running guide shows how to progress with run/walk intervals.
  • Keep moving gently. Low-impact options like walking on softer surfaces in good shoes can keep you active once pain eases.

When to See a Doctor

Most heel pain improves with home care, but you should see a doctor or podiatrist if:

  • Pain has not improved after a few weeks of stretching and rest
  • The pain is severe or keeps you from walking normally
  • You have numbness, tingling, or swelling
  • You had a sudden injury or felt a pop in your heel or arch
  • You have redness, warmth, or fever along with heel pain

A doctor can confirm the diagnosis and rule out other causes like a stress fracture or nerve problem. Treatment options may include physical therapy, night splints, custom orthotics, or other therapies. The Mayo Clinic has a helpful overview of plantar fasciitis symptoms and causes.

The Bottom Line

Plantar fasciitis can be frustrating, but it usually responds well to consistent care. Do these plantar fasciitis stretches two to three times a day, never skip the morning routine, wear supportive shoes, and ease back into activity slowly. If your heel is not getting better after a few weeks, get it checked so you can get back on your feet comfortably.
