If you have low or fallen arches, tennis can be tough on your feet. Every split step, wide forehand, and hard stop asks your foot to stay stable while it moves sideways, and flat feet tend to roll inward when they're tired. The good news: the right court shoe makes a big difference. This guide rounds up the best tennis shoes for flat feet, shows you how to check your arch at home, and explains exactly which features matter so you can buy with confidence.

Key takeaways

  • Look for a firm midfoot, a stiff heel counter, and strong lateral support rather than plush cushioning alone.
  • A removable insole lets you add an orthotic or arch support.
  • Match the outsole to your court: durable patterns for hard courts, full herringbone for clay.
  • Use running shoes for running and walking, not for tennis.
  • Persistent arch, heel, or ankle pain is a reason to see a podiatrist.

Why Flat Feet Need a Different Tennis Shoe

Flat feet usually mean the arch collapses more than average when you put weight on it. That inward roll, called overpronation, is fine for some people and painful for others. In tennis, the problem gets amplified because you are constantly cutting, sliding, and pushing off the inside edge of your foot.

A good court shoe for flat feet does three jobs. It limits excess inward roll through the midfoot, it holds your heel in place so your foot does not slide on lateral moves, and it gives you a wide, stable base to land on. Cushioning matters too, but stability comes first.

How to Tell If You Have Flat Feet (The Wet Test)

You do not need special equipment to get a rough idea of your arch type. Try the wet test:

  1. Wet your feet. Step into a shallow pan of water or wet the soles of your feet.
  2. Stand naturally. Step onto a piece of dark paper, a paper bag, or dry concrete with your full weight.
  3. Step off and look. Check the shape your footprint leaves behind.
  4. Read the print. If you see almost the whole sole with little or no inward curve, you likely have flat or low arches. A clear curve with a narrow band means a normal arch, and a very thin band means a high arch.

It also helps to look at an old pair of shoes. Heavy wear along the inside edge of the sole and uppers that lean inward are common signs of overpronation. The Mayo Clinic has a helpful overview of flatfeet symptoms and causes if you want to learn more.

Buying Guide: What to Look For in Shoes for Flat Feet

Lateral support

Tennis is a side-to-side sport. A supportive upper, reinforced sidewalls, and a wide base keep your foot on the platform when you change direction. Press on the side of the shoe: it should feel firm, not floppy.

Midfoot support and medial posts

Many stability shoes use firmer foam or a supportive structure on the inner (medial) side of the midsole. This slows the inward roll that comes with flat feet. Twist the shoe gently: a good stability shoe resists twisting through the middle.

Heel counter

The heel counter is the stiff cup around the back of the shoe. Squeeze it. It should barely flex. A firm heel counter keeps your heel aligned and reduces wobble on landings.

Wide toe box and width options

Flat feet often spread more under load. A roomy toe box and wide sizing prevent pinching, blisters, and that feeling of your foot spilling over the edge.

Removable insoles for orthotics

If you use custom or over-the-counter orthotics, choose a shoe with a removable insole. Take the stock insole out and put your orthotic in so the shoe does not become too shallow.

Outsole for your court surface

  • Hard courts: a thick, durable rubber outsole, usually with a modified herringbone pattern.
  • Clay courts: a full herringbone tread that grips loose surfaces and sheds clay.
  • Grass courts: nubbed outsoles designed for grass, where allowed.
  • Mixed play: an all-court outsole is a reasonable compromise.
Tip: Shop later in the day when your feet are slightly swollen, wear your tennis socks, and bring your orthotics. Leave about a thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe.

Running Shoes vs. Tennis Shoes for Flat Feet

It is tempting to use one pair for everything, but running and tennis shoes are built for different movements. Running shoes are designed for forward motion, with soft foam, a raised heel, and flexible forefoot. That works well for running, but it can feel unstable when you cut sideways, and a running outsole wears down fast on a hard court.

Tennis shoes are lower, flatter, and more structured, with reinforced sides for lateral moves. If you also run, look for a dedicated stability running shoe. Our guide on how to choose running shoes for flat feet walks through stability, motion control, and gait analysis in detail.

Heads up: Playing tennis in running shoes is a common cause of rolled ankles. Keep a separate pair for the court.

When to See a Podiatrist

Flat feet are common and often painless. But some signs mean it is time to get a professional opinion:

  • Arch, heel, or ankle pain that lasts more than a couple of weeks
  • Pain that gets worse during or after play despite supportive shoes
  • One foot that has recently become flatter than the other
  • Swelling along the inside of the ankle
  • Numbness, tingling, or stiffness in the foot

A podiatrist can assess your gait, rule out conditions like posterior tibial tendon problems, and recommend custom orthotics if you need them.

The Bottom Line

The best tennis shoes for flat feet combine a stable midfoot, a firm heel counter, strong lateral support, and enough room for an orthotic. For most players, a stability-focused model like the ASICS Gel-Resolution is a safe starting point, while wide-footed players should look at brands with real width options like New Balance. Match the outsole to your court, replace shoes when they lose their structure, and see a podiatrist if pain sticks around. With the right pair, flat feet do not have to hold your game back.
