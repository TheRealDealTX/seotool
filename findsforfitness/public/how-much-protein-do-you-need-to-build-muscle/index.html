How Much Protein Do You Need to Build Muscle?

Protein targets for muscle gain explained: the RDA vs. 1.6-2.2 g/kg, how to spread protein across meals, top foods, plant vs. animal sources, and safety.

If you are lifting weights to get stronger or build muscle, protein is the nutrient everyone talks about. But how much protein do you need to build muscle, really? The answer is more than the minimum most people hear about, but probably less than supplement ads suggest. This guide explains the science-backed ranges, how to spread protein across your day, which foods deliver the most, and who should be careful about eating more.

Key takeaways

  • The RDA of 0.8 g/kg is a minimum to prevent deficiency, not a target for muscle growth.
  • Around 1.6 to 2.2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day supports muscle gain for most active people.
  • Spreading protein over 3 to 5 meals with roughly 20 to 40 g each works well.
  • Both plant and animal proteins can build muscle when total intake is adequate.
  • People with kidney disease should talk to a doctor before increasing protein.

Why Protein Matters for Muscle

Every time you strength train, you create small amounts of stress in your muscle fibers. Your body repairs and rebuilds those fibers, making them slightly bigger and stronger over time. That rebuilding process, called muscle protein synthesis, relies on amino acids, the building blocks of protein.

Training is the signal, and protein is the raw material. Without enough of either, progress stalls. Leucine, an amino acid found in high amounts in dairy, meat, eggs, and soy, plays a particularly important role in switching on muscle protein synthesis.

Protein also helps you feel full, which is useful if you are trying to lose fat while keeping muscle.

How Much Protein Do You Need to Build Muscle? RDA vs. Muscle-Gain Targets

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day for healthy adults. That number is designed to prevent deficiency in most people. It was not designed to maximize muscle growth for people who train hard.

For those lifting weights with the goal of building muscle, sports nutrition research commonly points to a range of about 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram per day. Harvard Health has a helpful overview of how much protein you need every day.

Where in the range should you aim?

  • Around 1.6 g/kg: A solid target for most people building muscle at a calorie surplus or maintenance.
  • Toward 2.2 g/kg: Useful when you are in a calorie deficit and want to preserve muscle while losing fat, or if you are very lean and training hard.

If fat loss is your goal, pairing adequate protein with a modest deficit helps you keep muscle. Our guide on how to calculate a calorie deficit walks through the numbers.

How to Spread Protein Across Your Meals

Total daily intake matters most, but distribution helps too. Rather than eating a small breakfast and a huge dinner, aim to include a solid serving of protein at each meal.

  1. Divide your target. Split your daily goal across 3 to 5 meals and snacks. For example, 140 g a day could be four meals of about 35 g.
  2. Aim for 20 to 40 g per meal. This range, or roughly 0.4 g/kg per meal, is commonly suggested to stimulate muscle protein synthesis effectively.
  3. Do not skip breakfast protein. Many people eat little protein in the morning. Eggs, Greek yogurt, or cottage cheese can fix that. See our list of healthy breakfast foods for ideas.
  4. Eat a protein-rich meal around training. A meal or snack within a few hours before or after your workout is a sensible habit.
  5. Consider a pre-bed option. A serving of slow-digesting protein like cottage cheese or Greek yogurt before sleep can help some people reach their total.
Tip: Build each meal around a protein first, then add vegetables, carbs, and healthy fats. It is the simplest way to hit your target without counting every gram.

Plant vs. Animal Protein

Animal proteins such as meat, fish, eggs, and dairy are "complete," meaning they contain all nine essential amino acids in good amounts. They also tend to be rich in leucine and are easily digested.

Many plant proteins are lower in one or more essential amino acids, and some are slightly less digestible. That does not mean you cannot build muscle on plants. It just means a few strategies help:

  • Eat a variety of plant proteins throughout the day, such as beans, lentils, soy, grains, nuts, and seeds
  • Lean on soy foods like tofu, tempeh, and edamame, which are complete proteins
  • Aim for the higher end of your protein range to make up for lower digestibility
  • Use a pea, soy, or blended plant protein powder if needed to hit your target

Protein Needs for Older Adults

As we age, muscles become less responsive to protein, a change sometimes called anabolic resistance. Combined with less activity, this contributes to gradual muscle loss, known as sarcopenia, which affects strength, balance, and independence.

Many experts suggest older adults aim for more than the RDA, often in the range of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg per day, or more when strength training or recovering from illness. Getting about 25 to 40 g of protein per meal and doing regular resistance exercise are two of the best ways to protect muscle with age. Safety: Who Should Be Careful

For healthy adults, eating within the muscle-building range appears to be safe. Still, some people should get personalized advice first.

Heads up: If you have chronic kidney disease or reduced kidney function, a high-protein diet may put extra strain on your kidneys. Talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before increasing your protein intake.
  • People with kidney or liver conditions may need to limit protein.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding people have different needs and should check with their care team.
  • Anyone relying heavily on supplements should remember powders are not as tightly regulated as medicines. Look for third-party tested products.

Also, keep the rest of your diet in balance. Protein works best alongside enough total calories, carbohydrates to fuel training, healthy fats, fiber, and fluids.

The Bottom Line

So, how much protein do you need to build muscle? For most people who strength train, about 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight a day, spread across several meals, is a well-supported target. Pair it with consistent training, adequate sleep, and enough overall calories, and your muscles will have what they need to grow. Frequently asked questions

Is 1 gram of protein per pound too much?

One gram per pound of body weight is about 2.2 grams per kilogram, which sits at the upper end of the range commonly suggested for building muscle. It is generally safe for healthy people but more than many need to see results.

Do I need protein shakes to build muscle?

No. Shakes are a convenient way to hit your target, but whole foods like eggs, dairy, meat, fish, beans, and tofu work just as well when total daily protein is adequate.

Should I eat protein right after a workout?

Having a protein-rich meal or snack within a few hours of training is helpful, but total daily protein and even distribution across meals matter more than precise timing.

Can you build muscle on a vegan diet?

Yes. Combining a variety of plant proteins such as soy, lentils, beans, seitan, and whole grains, and aiming for the higher end of protein targets, supports muscle growth on a vegan diet.

Is a high-protein diet bad for your kidneys?

For people with healthy kidneys, higher protein intakes appear to be safe. If you have kidney disease or reduced kidney function, talk to your doctor before increasing protein, since you may need to limit it.
