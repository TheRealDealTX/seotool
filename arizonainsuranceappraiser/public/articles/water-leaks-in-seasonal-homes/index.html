Water Leaks in Seasonal Homes: What Arizona Snowbirds Should Know

A slow leak in an empty house can run for weeks. Vacancy clauses and seepage exclusions then turn a water claim into a timing argument.

Arizona is full of homes that sit empty for part of the year: winter homes in Mesa, Yuma and Lake Havasu City, cabins in Pinetop and Payson, vacation rentals in Sedona and Flagstaff. Water losses in those homes raise two questions full-time residents rarely face.

Question one: vacancy and unoccupancy

Many policies restrict coverage for certain losses if the home has been vacant or unoccupied beyond a set period, and some require you to keep heat on or drain the plumbing. The exact wording matters: vacant (empty of furnishings) and unoccupied (furnished but no one living there) can mean different things in a policy.

Question two: sudden or long-term?

Policies typically cover sudden and accidental discharges and exclude repeated seepage or leakage over weeks or longer. When no one is home, a carrier may argue that damage built up slowly. Evidence of when the leak started becomes central.

Prevention that also helps proof

  • Shut off the main when you leave, if your irrigation and other systems allow it.
  • Install a smart water shutoff or leak sensors. Their logs can show exactly when a leak began.
  • Arrange regular property checks and keep a dated log or photos from each visit.
  • Keep utility bills. Water usage spikes can help date a leak.

If a loss happens

Stop the water, start drying, document everything and report promptly. Gather the check logs, sensor data and utility records before the carrier asks. If coverage is denied over vacancy or seepage, that is a legal question for an attorney. If coverage is accepted and the dispute is about cost, appraisal can help.
