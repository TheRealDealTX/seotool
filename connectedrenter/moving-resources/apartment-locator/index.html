Apartment Locator: Find Your Next Place Without the Legwork

An apartment locator does the searching, calling and comparing for you, and in most cities the service is free to renters. Here's how locating works and how to get the most out of it.

3 min read Updated October 2026 Free resource
Box 01 · contentsApartment Locator

What an apartment locator actually does

An apartment locator (sometimes called an apartment finder or rental agent) is a licensed real-estate professional who matches renters with apartment communities. You share your budget, preferred neighborhoods, move-in date, must-haves and deal-breakers. The locator then pulls current availability, specials and pricing from dozens of properties, narrows the list, and often schedules tours or sends video walkthroughs.

Because locators talk to leasing offices every day, they tend to know things a listing site won't show you: which communities are running look-and-lease specials, which ones are strict about credit or rental history, and where the "available now" units actually are.

Is an apartment locator free?

For renters, usually yes. In large rental markets — Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Austin, San Antonio, Atlanta, Phoenix and many others — apartment communities pay the locator a referral commission when you sign a lease. That fee is typically a percentage of one month's rent and is built into the property's marketing budget, not added to your rent.

Important: list the locator on your rental application (most applications have a "referred by" or "locator" field). If you tour a property on your own first and then use a locator, the property may not honor the referral.

In some markets (parts of New York City and Boston, for example) renters may be asked to pay a broker fee. Always ask up front: "Will I owe you anything, now or at signing?"

How the process works, step by step

  1. Set your budget. Decide on a total monthly number that includes rent, utilities, parking and pet rent. Use the affordability calculator below if you're not sure.
  2. Share your criteria. Bedrooms, move-in date, commute, school zone, pets, in-unit laundry, garage, credit or rental-history concerns.
  3. Get a shortlist. A good locator sends 5–10 options with current prices, specials and floor plans rather than a list of 40.
  4. Tour. In person or by video. Ask the leasing agent about total monthly cost, not just base rent.
  5. Apply and name your locator. Submit one application at a time when you can — each one usually carries a non-refundable fee.
  6. Sign and plan the move. Lock in your move-in date, then set up utilities, renters insurance and movers.

Questions to ask any locator

  • Are you licensed, and in which state? (In Texas, locators must hold a real-estate license through TREC.)
  • Do I pay anything — now, at signing, or if I break the lease?
  • Which properties do you not work with, and why?
  • Can you work with my credit score, past eviction or broken lease?
  • Do you offer a rebate or move-in gift? (Some locators share part of their commission where state rules allow.)

Red flags to avoid

Walk away from anyone who asks you to wire a deposit before you've seen the unit, pressures you to sign the same day, or can't show a license number. Rental scams often copy real listings and undercut the price to rush you.

Frequently asked questions

Do apartment locators cost money?

In most U.S. rental markets, no. The apartment community pays the locator a referral fee when you sign a lease. Some high-cost cities use renter-paid broker fees instead, so always ask before you start.

Will using a locator raise my rent?

No. The referral fee comes from the property's marketing budget and the rent is the same whether you use a locator or walk in yourself.

Can an apartment locator help with bad credit or an eviction?

Often, yes. Locators know which properties use more flexible screening, accept guarantors or offer second-chance leasing, which can save you from paying application fees at places that will decline you.

Do I need to name the locator on my application?

Yes. If the locator isn't listed on your application or guest card, the property may not pay them — and some locators can't help you with that property afterward.
