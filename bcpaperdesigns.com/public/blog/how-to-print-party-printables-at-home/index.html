How to Print Party Printables at Home (Paper, Settings, Cutting)

Which cardstock to buy, what printer settings to change, how to avoid shrunken or cropped prints and how to cut clean edges: a practical guide to printing party decor at home.

Printable party decorations are one of the best deals in party planning: one file, a pack of cardstock and an evening, and you have a banner, signs, cupcake toppers and favor tags that all match. But if you have ever printed a sign that came out washed out, shrunk by half an inch or with a white stripe on one edge, you know there is a little more to it. Here is how to print party printables at home so they look like they came from a print shop.

For years we printed every piece in our party sets on a photo-quality inkjet onto 110 lb cardstock, so this guide comes from a lot of trial and error.

Choosing the right cardstock

Paper weight is confusing because US paper is labeled two ways: "text" weight (thin, like copy paper) and "cover" weight (cardstock). Always compare cover to cover. Here is what each common weight is good for:

WeightApprox. gsmFeelBest for
20 to 24 lb text75 to 90Copy paperTest prints, game sheets
65 lb cover176Light cardstockInvitations in a pinch, flat labels
80 lb cover216Medium cardstockFavor tags, place cards, food labels
100 lb cover270Heavy cardstockBanners, cupcake toppers, invitations
110 lb cover300Heavy, rigidCake toppers, centerpieces, signs, banners

We used 110 lb cardstock for everything because it stands up on a skewer without bending, does not curl in humid rooms and feels substantial in guests' hands. Before you buy, check your printer manual for its maximum paper weight; many home inkjets list 300 gsm or close to it through the rear tray, while some only handle around 220 gsm.

Finish matters too. Matte cardstock is the most forgiving and takes ink evenly. Bright white (look for a brightness of 96 or higher) makes colors pop. Glossy photo cardstock looks great but can smudge and shows fingerprints. Textured or linen stock is lovely for invitations but softens fine lines.

Inkjet or laser?

Inkjet printers handle heavy cardstock better because many have a straighter paper path, especially through a rear or manual feed slot. They also produce richer color on matte paper. Laser printers give crisp text and do not smear, but many struggle to feed anything over 80 to 100 lb, and toner can crack along folds. If you have a laser printer, check its spec sheet and try a single sheet first.

Printer settings that make the difference

  1. Paper type: select "Matte Photo Paper," "Cardstock" or "Heavyweight" rather than "Plain Paper." This tells the printer to lay down more ink and slow down the feed.
  2. Quality: choose "High" or "Best." Draft mode leaves visible bands on solid colors.
  3. Scale: set to "Actual size" or 100%, never "Fit to page" or "Shrink oversized pages." Fit to page shrinks a 2-inch cupcake topper to about 1.9 inches and throws off any punch you planned to use.
  4. Paper size: match the file. Most US printables are 8.5x11 Letter; files made for A4 will shift slightly on Letter paper.
  5. Feed: use the rear tray or manual feed slot for anything over 80 lb, and load one or two sheets at a time.
  6. Color: leave color management on the printer's automatic setting unless you know your color profile well.
Tip: Print page one on plain paper first and measure a known item with a ruler. If a 2-inch circle measures 2 inches, your scale is right and you can switch to cardstock.

Understanding bleed and borderless printing

Most home printers cannot print all the way to the edge; they leave a margin of about 1/8 to 1/4 inch. That is why good printable files keep important design inside a safe area and add bleed: the background color extends about 1/8 inch beyond the cut line. When you cut on the line, a little extra color gets trimmed off and you never see a white sliver.

  • If your file has crop marks, cut on the marks, not the visible edge of the color.
  • If your printer offers borderless printing, it usually enlarges the image slightly to cover the edges, which also crops a bit of the design. Use it only for full-page prints like an 8x10 sign, and leave it off for sheets of toppers.
  • If you are designing your own, extend backgrounds 1/8 inch past the trim line and keep text at least 1/4 inch inside it.

Cutting tools for clean edges

The cut is what separates homemade from handmade. You do not need all of these; pick based on what you are making.

  • Paper trimmer (guillotine or rotary): best for straight cuts on signs, invitations, tags and banner pennants. A 12-inch rotary trimmer handles 110 lb stock easily.
  • Craft knife and metal ruler: for interior cuts and tight corners. Change the blade often; a dull blade tears cardstock.
  • Self-healing cutting mat: protects the table and keeps the knife from skating.
  • Circle punches: a 2-inch punch is the standard size for cupcake toppers and favor tags; 1.5-inch and 2.5-inch are also common.
  • Small sharp scissors: for curves and shapes like animal silhouettes. Turn the paper, not the scissors, as you cut.
  • Corner rounder: a quick upgrade for place cards and tags.

For hundreds of small pieces, a home cutting machine can save time, but for a single party, a punch and a trimmer get you there.

ProblemLikely causeFix
Print is smaller than expected"Fit to page" scalingSet scale to 100% or Actual size

Plan your print run

Before you start, make a quick list of every piece and how many you need. For a typical party of 25 guests, that might be 1 welcome sign, 1 banner of 12 to 14 pennants, 1 cake topper, 3 centerpieces, 30 cupcake toppers, 25 favor tags and 6 food labels. That works out to roughly 15 to 20 sheets of cardstock, so buy a 50-sheet pack and you will have plenty for test prints and mistakes.

Assembly and next steps

Once pieces are cut, glue toppers to sticks with a low-temp glue gun or strong double-sided tape, back two-sided pieces so the stick is hidden, and let everything set overnight. Frequently asked questions

Can a regular home printer print on 110 lb cardstock?

Many inkjet printers can, usually through a rear or manual feed slot, but not all. Check your printer's maximum paper weight in gsm; 110 lb cover is about 300 gsm. Feed one sheet at a time and use the heavyweight or matte paper setting.

Why do my printables come out smaller than they should?

Your print dialog is probably set to Fit to page or Shrink oversized pages. Change scaling to Actual size or 100% and print a test page on plain paper to measure before using cardstock.

What does bleed mean on a printable?

Bleed is extra background color that extends past the cut line, usually about 1/8 inch. When you trim on the line, the extra color is cut away so no white edge shows on the finished piece.
