Definitions

What Is an Insurance Appraiser, and When Do You Need One?

The phrase "insurance appraiser" gets used for three different jobs. This page is about the one that matters when your claim is underpaid: the appraiser you appoint under your policy's appraisal clause to determine the amount of loss.

Three things people mean by "insurance appraiser"

  • A carrier's auto damage appraiser, the employee or vendor who writes the estimate on a wrecked car. Not what this site is about.
  • A real estate appraiser, who values property for lending. Also not what this site is about, although the confusion is common.
  • An appraisal-clause appraiser: the competent, independent person each side appoints when the policyholder and the insurer cannot agree on the amount of a property loss. That is the insurance claim appraiser this page describes.

What an insurance claim appraiser does

When you invoke the appraisal clause, you appoint an appraiser and the carrier appoints one. Each appraiser inspects the damage, prices the repair in an itemized estimate, and then the two compare numbers line by line. Where they agree, that figure stands. Where they disagree, the two appraisers choose an umpire and the disputed items are decided by any two of the three. The result is an appraisal award: a binding determination of the amount of loss.

The appraiser is not a negotiator, not a lawyer and not a contractor bidding for the job. The appraiser's product is a defensible number and the documentation behind it: photographs, measurements, moisture readings, test squares, material samples, code citations and pricing.

What "certified insurance appraiser" means

There is no state license called "insurance appraiser" in Texas. "Certified" generally refers to training programs such as the P.L.A.N. umpire and appraiser certification, or to industry designations, and it signals that the appraiser has studied appraisal procedure, award drafting and the ethics of the role. What Texas does regulate, beginning with residential policies issued or renewed on or after March 1, 2027, is who may serve: appraisers must be competent by training or experience, independent of the parties and disinterested in the outcome, and on dwelling losses must be a licensed adjuster or public adjuster with residential estimating experience, an engineer or architect, or a qualified tradesperson (28 TAC §5.9804).

In practice the strongest appraisers combine a Texas adjuster license, deep estimating experience in the software carriers use, hands-on construction knowledge and a track record on both sides of the table. Our appraiser's background is laid out in full on his bio page.

What an insurance appraiser costs

Each party pays its own appraiser; the umpire's fee is usually split. Appraisers charge a flat fee or an hourly rate, set by the size and complexity of the loss. For a typical residential roof or water dispute the fee is small relative to the gap between the carrier's estimate and the real cost of repair, and it is quoted in writing before you appoint anyone. Ask any prospective appraiser two questions: how are you paid, and have you had any prior role in this claim?

When you need one

  1. The carrier's estimate is materially lower than the real cost to repair, or the carrier says some or all of the damage was not caused by the storm, and negotiation has stalled.
  2. Your policy has an appraisal clause, or will have one at your next renewal after March 1, 2027.

If both are true, invoke the appraisal clause and appoint an appraiser. In Texas the panel can decide causation as well as price (State Farm Lloyds v. Johnson, 2009). Only a denial that rests purely on policy wording, rather than on what caused the damage, needs an attorney before an appraiser.
