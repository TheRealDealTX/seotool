Residential property appraisal

Homeowners Insurance Claim Appraiser

When you and your insurance company cannot agree on what a loss costs to repair, or on how much of the damage the storm actually caused, your homeowners policy gives you a way out of the stalemate: appraisal. You appoint an appraiser, the carrier appoints one, and the two of them settle the amount of loss. We serve as the appraiser homeowners appoint.

What a homeowners claim appraiser actually does

An appraiser is not a negotiator and not an advocate in the courtroom sense. Under a Texas homeowners policy the appraiser's job is narrow and specific: determine the amount of loss. That means inspecting the property, measuring and photographing the damage, pricing the repair with a defensible estimate, then meeting the carrier's appraiser to compare numbers line by line.

Where the two appraisers agree, that number becomes the award. Where they do not, the disputed items go to the umpire the appraisers selected together, and any two of the three sign an award that binds both sides as to the amount of loss.

Because the award is binding, who you appoint matters. A carrier's appraiser will arrive with a complete, software-priced estimate and a clear position on every line item. Your appraiser needs to be able to do the same, and to explain the difference in plain terms to an umpire who may be a contractor, engineer or retired adjuster.

Residential losses we appraise

  • Roof claims: hail, wind, and the recurring argument over repair versus replacement, matching, and code upgrades.
  • Water and plumbing losses: slab leaks, supply-line failures, drying, demolition and rebuild scope.
  • Hurricane and windstorm: roof, siding, windows, fences, interior water intrusion and contents.
  • Fire and smoke: structure, cleaning and deodorizing, contents, and additional living expense documentation.
  • Freeze and burst-pipe events: widespread interior damage with scope disputes across many rooms.
  • Condominium and tenant policies: unit-owner responsibility lines and contents valuation.

Qualified under the 2027 Texas appraisal rules

Beginning with residential policies issued or renewed on or after March 1, 2027, Texas rule 28 TAC §5.9804 requires any appraiser on a dwelling loss to be competent by training or experience, independent of both parties, disinterested in the outcome, and either a licensed adjuster or public adjuster with experience estimating residential losses, an engineer or architect, or someone with trade experience in the type of repair at issue.

Good to know: causation is appraisable in Texas

Appraisal decides the amount of loss, and in Texas that includes causation. Under State Farm Lloyds v. Johnson (Tex. 2009), appraisers may decide how much of the damage the storm caused and how much is wear, deterioration or a prior event, and award the covered portion. A "wear and tear, not hail" letter is therefore an appraisable dispute, not a dead end. Only pure policy-interpretation questions sit outside the panel's authority.

How a homeowners appraisal usually runs

  1. Review. We read the policy's appraisal clause, the carrier's estimate and any denial or partial-payment letters, and confirm appraisal fits the dispute.
  2. Demand. You (or we, on your instruction) send a written appraisal demand naming your appraiser. The carrier then names its own.
  3. Inspection and estimate. We inspect, document and price the loss independently.
  4. Appraiser conference. We meet the carrier's appraiser, agree what we can and isolate what remains in dispute.
  5. Umpire. If needed, the appraisers select an umpire and present the remaining differences.
  6. Award. Two signatures create a binding amount of loss. The carrier pays the award, less any deductible and prior payments, subject to the policy's terms.

FAQ

Common questions about appraisal

What is an insurance appraiser for policyholders?

An appraiser is the person you appoint under your policy's appraisal clause to determine the amount of your loss when you and the insurance company disagree on it. The carrier appoints its own appraiser, and the two appraisers (with an umpire if they cannot agree) issue a binding award. Appraisers must be competent, independent and disinterested in the outcome; they are not advocates in the way a lawyer or public adjuster is.

How is an appraiser different from a public adjuster?

A public adjuster represents you in presenting and negotiating the claim and is typically paid a percentage of the settlement. An appraiser is appointed only after a dispute over the amount of loss, participates in a formal process that ends in a binding award, and must be disinterested in the outcome, which is why we charge a flat or hourly fee quoted in writing before you appoint us.

The carrier says my damage is wear and tear, not the storm. Can appraisal still help?

Yes. In Texas, causation is appraisable. The Texas Supreme Court held in State Farm Lloyds v. Johnson (2009) that deciding the amount of loss necessarily includes deciding which damage the covered event caused and which is pre-existing or excluded, so an appraisal panel can sort hail from wear, storm from deterioration, and award the covered portion. What appraisal does not decide are pure policy questions, such as whether a type of loss is excluded by the policy's language; those remain for the carrier and, if necessary, a court.

Is the appraisal award binding?

Yes, as to the amount of loss. An award signed by both appraisers, or by one appraiser and the umpire, binds both you and the carrier. Under the 2027 Texas rules it can be set aside only if it was made without authority, not in substantial compliance with the appraisal clause, or affected by fraud, accident or material mistake.

How much does an appraiser cost?

Each party pays its own appraiser, and the umpire's fee is usually split equally. We quote a flat or hourly fee in writing before you appoint us, based on the size and type of the loss, so you know the cost before the process starts.
