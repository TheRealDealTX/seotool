Additional living expenses on a hurricane claim are paid under the part of a homeowners policy usually called “Loss of Use” or Coverage D. It exists for the weeks or months when your home cannot be lived in while it is repaired. It is easy to overlook in the rush to tarp the roof, and easy to under-claim because the expenses are scattered: a hotel here, extra gas there, takeout because there is no kitchen. This guide explains how the coverage typically works in Florida homeowners policies and how to keep the records an adjuster will ask for.

Read your own policy. Loss-of-use wording differs between insurers and policy forms (homeowners, condo unit owners, renters, dwelling fire). Everything below describes common terms, not your contract.

What the coverage is designed to pay

Most homeowners forms pay additional living expense when a covered loss makes the residence premises not fit to live in. The key word is additional: the policy usually pays any necessary increase in living expenses so your household can maintain its normal standard of living, not your whole budget. Your normal mortgage payment, for example, continues and is not reimbursed; the cost of a rental on top of it may be.

Expenses you would have had anyway, and expenses that stop because you are out of the home (for example, utilities that are shut off), are generally subtracted.

Limits and time

Loss-of-use coverage has its own dollar limit, shown on the declarations page and often expressed as a percentage of the dwelling limit. Many forms also limit the time: typically the shortest time reasonably needed to repair or replace the damage or, if you permanently relocate, the shortest time for your household to settle elsewhere. Because post-hurricane repairs can stretch out for months when contractors and materials are scarce, the dollar limit can run out. Track the running total so you know where you stand.

Many forms also include a short civil-authority provision: if a government order prohibits you from using your home because of direct damage to neighboring premises from a covered peril, loss of use may be paid for a limited period (often stated in days or weeks) even if your own home was not badly damaged. Check your policy for the exact period.

Is my home “unlivable”?

There is no single Florida statutory test. Insurers generally look at whether the house is safe and has basic functions: a sound roof over living areas, working electricity, water and sewer, a usable bathroom and kitchen, and air quality free of significant mold or sewage contamination. Document the conditions with photos and, where possible, a contractor's or the building department's written statement. Tell the insurer in writing that you have had to leave and why, and ask what it needs from you.

If you have a mortgage, your lender may also have requirements before you move out for repairs. Keep it informed.

How to document additional living expenses

  1. Establish your normal baseline

    Pull one to three months of pre-storm bank and card statements showing normal grocery, fuel and utility spending. This is what the insurer will subtract.

  2. Get approval in writing where you can

    Before signing a lease or a long hotel stay, email the adjuster with the address, nightly or monthly cost and expected length, and ask for confirmation.

  3. Keep every receipt

    Use one envelope or one folder of photos per month. Itemized receipts are better than card slips.

  4. Log mileage

    A simple daily log (date, from, to, miles, purpose) beats reconstructing it months later.

  5. Submit regularly

    Send expenses monthly with a one-page summary, rather than waiting until the end. It keeps the claim current and shows when the limit is getting close.

Common disputes

  • “The house was livable.” Answer with photos, inspection findings and, where relevant, a statement from a licensed professional about safety conditions.
  • “Repairs should have been done sooner.” Keep proof of contractor availability, permit timelines and any delay caused by the claims process itself, such as a late inspection or estimate. See our guide on a delayed hurricane claim.
  • “That rental is above your normal standard.” Show what comparable options were available at the time; after a major storm, choices are limited.
  • Meals paid in full versus increase only. Expect the insurer to subtract normal grocery spending; your baseline statements make that calculation fair.

Public adjusters and living expenses

If you hire a public adjuster, Florida law requires a separate signed agreement before the adjuster can take a fee on additional living expense payments (s. 626.854(11)(d)). Read the contract to see whether it covers these payments.

FAQ: additional living expenses

Does homeowners insurance pay for a hotel after a hurricane?

If the hurricane caused covered damage that makes your home unfit to live in, the loss-of-use coverage in most homeowners policies pays the increase in living costs, which usually includes reasonable temporary housing, up to the policy's limit.

Will my insurer pay for all my meals?

Usually only the extra cost compared with what you normally spend on food. Keep pre-storm statements to show your normal grocery spending.

How long will additional living expenses be paid?

Typically for the shortest time reasonably needed to repair the home, or for your household to settle elsewhere, and only up to the dollar limit on your declarations page. Check your policy for the exact wording.

Can I get an advance for living expenses?

Many insurers issue advances after a major storm, especially for displaced policyholders. Ask in writing and keep receipts showing how the money was spent.
