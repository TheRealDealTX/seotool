After a big landfall, insurers may be handling tens of thousands of claims at once, and some delay is expected. But a hurricane claim delayed for months without an inspection, an estimate or a decision is not just frustrating: it can push you past your own deadlines, leave the house exposed to more damage, and cost you money in temporary housing. Florida's claims-handling statute gives you specific dates to measure the insurer against.

Is the insurer actually late? Check the statute

Section 627.70131 sets the main deadlines for residential property claims (and for many smaller commercial claims). Count each from the date shown:

Acknowledge your communication, with claim forms and instructions7 calendar daysReceipt of your communication

Complete physical inspection, if needed30 daysReceipt of proof-of-loss statements

The 60-day deadline in s. 627.70131(7)(a) applies to initial, reopened and supplemental claims alike. The insurer must also give you a written explanation for any payment, denial or partial denial, and if it pays less than its own detailed estimate, it must explain why in writing.

When delay is legally excused

Not every missed date is a violation. The statute recognizes two broad categories of “factors beyond the control of the insurer”:

  • Emergency orders. After a declared state of emergency, a significant data breach or an IT problem, the Office of Insurance Regulation can issue an order extending deadlines. For the pay-or-deny deadline, the extension cannot exceed 30 additional days.
  • The policyholder's own conduct. Fraud, lack of cooperation or intentional misrepresentation by the policyholder that reasonably prevents the insurer from complying.

Separately, the deadlines are tolled (paused) during state-run mediation under s. 627.7015 or other contractual dispute resolution, and when the insurer has asked for material information and you have not provided it within 10 days. That second tolling rule only applies if the insurer made the request at least 15 days before its deadline. So a fast answer to every reasonable document request keeps the clock running against the insurer.

Interest on late payment

If the insurer pays after the 60-day deadline (or after any extended deadline set by an emergency order), the payment generally bears interest at the rate set under s. 55.03, running from the date the insurer received notice of the claim. The policy cannot waive this. A violation of the deadlines is not, by itself, a basis for a private lawsuit, but it supports regulatory complaints and can matter in any later dispute about how the claim was handled.

Why hurricane claims stall

  • Volume. Independent adjusters brought in after a storm may each carry a very large caseload.
  • Adjuster turnover. Your file may change hands several times, and each new adjuster may ask for the same documents again.
  • Waiting on an engineer. Insurers often hire engineers to decide whether damage is wind, water, wear or something else.
  • Missing documents. An unanswered request for photos, receipts or a proof of loss can toll the deadline.
  • Coverage questions. Disputes over flood versus wind, or whether the roof was already damaged, often slow the decision.

Seven steps to move a delayed claim

  1. Rebuild the timeline

    Write down the landfall date, the date you reported, every contact since, and every document you sent. This shows exactly which statutory deadlines have passed.

  2. Answer every open request

    Check your email and the insurer's portal for any unanswered request. Respond in writing, and say so if a requested item does not exist.

  3. Send a written status request

    Ask, in writing, for the status of the claim, the name and license number of the current adjuster, a copy of any estimate or engineering report, and the date of the coverage decision. Keep it factual.

  4. Protect the property in the meantime

    Delay does not excuse you from preventing further damage. Keep the tarp up, keep drying, keep receipts, and photograph any worsening.

  5. Call the state consumer helpline

    The Florida Department of Financial Services runs an insurance consumer helpline (1-877-MY-FL-CFO, 1-877-693-5236) and takes complaints online. Its staff can contact the insurer about your file.

  6. Consider free state mediation

    Once the insurer has made its pay-or-deny decision, residential claims generally become eligible for DFS mediation under s. 627.7015, with the insurer paying the mediator's costs. Mediation also pauses the insurer's deadlines, so it is a tool for a dispute about amount or coverage, not a way to force a first decision.

  7. Get a legal review

    A lawyer can assess whether the insurer has violated claims-handling statutes, whether a civil remedy notice under s. 624.155 makes sense (it gives the insurer 60 days to cure), and how the pre-suit notice process in s. 627.70152 applies once a coverage decision is made.

Watch your own deadlines while you wait

A delay by the insurer does not stop your clocks. If you find additional damage, the supplemental-claim window under s. 627.70132 (18 months from landfall for most current losses) keeps running. Use the deadline calculator to keep the dates in view, and keep working through the claim checklist.

FAQ: delayed hurricane claims

How long can an insurance company take on a hurricane claim in Florida?

Generally 60 days after notice of the claim to pay or deny it in whole or in part (s. 627.70131(7)(a)), unless factors beyond its control prevent that or the deadline is tolled. An emergency order can add up to 30 days to the pay-or-deny deadline.

Do I get interest if my hurricane claim is paid late?

Generally yes. Payments made after the 60-day deadline (or an extended deadline) bear interest from the date the insurer received notice of the claim, at the rate set under s. 55.03. If you also qualify for prejudgment interest, you choose one or the other.

Can I sue just because the insurer missed the 60-day deadline?

The statute says failure to comply is not by itself a cause of action. But the delay may support other claims and remedies, so a lawyer should look at the whole file.

The adjuster keeps changing. What should I do?

Ask each new adjuster to confirm in writing that they have your full file, and send a short summary with your key documents attached. Keep using the same claim number and keep your call log current.
