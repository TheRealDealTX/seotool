When a hurricane claim is denied, the letter usually cites a policy provision and a short factual conclusion: the damage is old, the water came from flooding, the claim was reported too late, the roof was simply worn out. Each of those rests on facts that can be checked. The goal of this guide is to help you read the denial the way a lawyer would, gather what is needed to test it, and understand the Florida procedures that come next.

What the insurer has to give you

Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), the insurer must pay or deny within 60 days after notice of the claim (unless factors beyond its control prevent it or the deadline is tolled) and must provide a written explanation for any denial or partial denial. Ask, in writing, for:

  • The specific policy language relied on, including the endorsement number
  • Copies of every estimate, engineering report and photo set the decision used
  • The name and license number of each adjuster who handled the claim
  • A certified copy of the complete policy in force on the date of loss

Common hurricane denial reasons, and how each is tested

“Late notice”

For most current losses, s. 627.70132 bars a new or reopened claim unless notice is given within one year of the date of loss (landfall for a hurricane), and a supplemental claim within 18 months. If you reported inside those windows, an insurer may still argue that a delay under the policy's “prompt notice” condition prejudiced its investigation. Check the exact dates; older losses may fall under longer, earlier versions of the statute. The deadline guide walks through how the dates are counted.

“Flood, not wind”

Standard homeowners policies exclude flood (rising surface water and storm surge), which is covered, if at all, under a separate flood policy. Wind damage and wind-driven rain entering through a storm-created opening are generally covered under the homeowners policy, subject to its terms and the hurricane deductible. When both happened, the evidence of what wind did first (roof damage, window breakage, water lines inside versus outside, timing) becomes the case. Many policies also contain “anti-concurrent causation” wording; have it reviewed.

“Wear and tear” or “pre-existing damage”

Insurers often hire engineers who conclude that roof damage is age-related. Before-storm photos, the roof's installation and permit date, prior inspection reports (such as a four-point or wind mitigation report), and an independent roofer or engineer can contradict that conclusion.

“Failure to mitigate”

Policies generally require reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Your receipts, photos of tarps and drying equipment, and records showing when you were allowed back into the area answer this argument.

“Below the deductible”

This is not a coverage denial but a valuation: the insurer says the covered loss is smaller than the hurricane deductible. Check the deductible math and the scope of the estimate. See hurricane deductible rules and the underpaid claim guide.

“Misrepresentation” or “failure to cooperate”

These allegations are serious and fact-specific. If the insurer has requested an examination under oath, or accuses you of concealment, talk to a lawyer before responding further.

How the pre-suit notice works after a denial

Before most property insurance lawsuits, the policyholder must serve a written notice of intent to initiate litigation on a Department of Financial Services form, at least 10 business days before filing, and not before the insurer has made its coverage determination (s. 627.70152). When the dispute is a denial of coverage, the notice includes an estimate of damages, if known. The insurer must respond in writing within 10 business days: it may accept coverage, continue to deny it, or assert a right to reinspect, which gives it 14 more business days to decide. A suit filed without the required notice is dismissed without prejudice. Statutes of limitation are tolled during parts of this process.

What changed about attorney fees

For many years Florida law generally required an insurer that lost a coverage suit to pay the policyholder's attorney fees. The 2022 and 2023 reforms ended that one-way fee rule for most property insurance claims under newer policies. That makes the fee arrangement with your lawyer more important. FAQ: denied hurricane claims

Can I appeal a denied hurricane claim in Florida?

There is no formal appeal board, but you can ask the insurer to reconsider with new evidence, request DFS mediation where eligible, file a consumer complaint, and, after the pre-suit notice required by s. 627.70152, file a lawsuit.

My insurer says the damage was from storm surge. Is that covered?

Storm surge is generally treated as flood and excluded from homeowners policies; it may be covered under a separate flood policy, which has its own federal rules and deadlines. Wind damage from the same storm can still be covered under the homeowners policy, so the cause of each item of damage matters.

Should I sign up with a public adjuster after a denial?

A public adjuster can help with valuation, but may not give legal advice and cannot file suit. Coverage denials usually turn on policy interpretation and facts that may need to be proven in court, which is legal work.

How long do I have to sue after a hurricane claim denial?

Florida's limitations statute, s. 95.11(2)(e), currently provides five years from the date of loss for breach of a property insurance contract, but pre-suit notice and policy conditions apply first. Have a lawyer confirm the dates for your loss rather than relying on that number.
