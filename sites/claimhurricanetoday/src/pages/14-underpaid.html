A hurricane claim underpaid by the insurer rarely looks like a dramatic refusal. It looks like a check that covers the fence and half the roof, an estimate that skips the bedroom ceiling, or a figure that would not hire a single licensed roofer in your county. Because the insurer's estimate is the starting point for everything that follows, the first job is to work out precisely where it falls short.

Start with the paperwork the insurer owes you

You should have three things in hand before you challenge a payment:

  • The detailed estimate. Under s. 627.70131(3)(e), the insurer must send you a copy of any detailed estimate its adjuster creates within 7 days of its creation.
  • The written explanation. Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), the insurer must explain in writing the reason for any payment, partial denial or denial, and, if it pays less than its own detailed estimate, why.
  • Any engineer or expert report the insurer relied on. Ask for it in writing if you did not receive it.

Florida rules that often matter on underpayment

Matching (s. 626.9744)

For homeowners policies that settle losses on a repair or replacement cost basis, unless the policy provides otherwise, when replaced items do not match the remaining items in quality, color or size, the insurer must make reasonable repairs or replacement of items in adjoining areas. Cost, how closely a match can be achieved and the remaining useful life of the undamaged portion are all considered. Read your policy for any matching limitation.

Ordinance or law coverage (s. 627.7011)

Florida insurers must offer homeowners ordinance-or-law coverage at 25% or 50% of the dwelling limit, and a policy is treated as including 25% unless the policyholder rejected it in writing. This coverage can pay for upgrades the current building code requires when you repair, which an insurer's estimate priced to the old standard may leave out.

Replacement cost holdback (s. 627.7011(3))

With dwelling replacement cost coverage, the insurer must initially pay at least the actual cash value of the loss, minus the deductible, and then pay the rest as work is performed and expenses are incurred. If your first check is actual cash value, the depreciation is not necessarily lost: keep repair contracts and invoices and submit them. After a total loss of the dwelling, replacement cost is paid without a depreciation holdback.

Five ways to close the gap

  1. Write a focused rebuttal

    Send the adjuster a short letter listing each disputed item, what is wrong, and the evidence attached. Specific line items get re-examined; general complaints rarely do.

  2. File a supplemental claim

    Additional loss from the same peril, including damage found during repairs, can be presented as a supplemental claim. For most current losses, notice must be given within 18 months of landfall under s. 627.70132. Check the date with the deadline calculator.

  3. Request DFS mediation

    Under s. 627.7015, residential policyholders can generally request free, non-binding mediation through the Department of Financial Services once the insurer has made its decision; the insurer pays the mediator. A signed settlement can be rescinded within 3 business days unless you have cashed or deposited the check.

  4. Consider appraisal

    Many policies contain an appraisal clause for disputes over the amount of loss: each side picks an appraiser and an umpire decides disagreements. Appraisal is generally limited to amount, not coverage, and each side usually pays its own appraiser plus half the umpire. Read the clause carefully before invoking it.

  5. Get a legal review

    When the gap is large, coverage is partly denied, or the insurer will not engage, a lawyer can evaluate the claim, the policy and the pre-suit notice under s. 627.70152, which for a dispute over amount generally includes a settlement demand with an itemized estimate of damages.

Do not cash the check without reading the letter

Cashing a payment for the undisputed amount usually does not end the claim, but some documents labeled “release” or “settlement” can. Read every enclosure. If the check is made out jointly to you and your mortgage company, contact the lender's loss-draft department about how it releases funds.

Public adjuster or attorney for an underpaid claim?

A licensed public adjuster can prepare an estimate and negotiate. Under s. 626.854(11), the fee cannot exceed 10% of payments on a claim from a declared state-of-emergency event during the first year after the declaration, or 20% otherwise, and no fee is allowed on amounts the insurer paid before the contract. On reopened or supplemental claims, the fee may be based only on new money obtained. Public adjusters may not give legal advice. An attorney can do the legal work (coverage analysis, pre-suit notice, litigation) and coordinate with appraisers and experts. Compare the full cost and services in writing either way.

FAQ: underpaid hurricane claims

Can I reopen my hurricane claim if I was underpaid?

Generally yes, within the statutory windows. For most current losses, a reopened claim must be noticed within one year of landfall and a supplemental claim within 18 months under s. 627.70132. Present specific new information or evidence.

Does cashing the insurer's check mean I accept it?

Usually not by itself, but read every document that comes with the check. A signed release or a mediation settlement you did not rescind in time can end the claim.

Why did the insurer deduct depreciation?

With replacement cost coverage on the dwelling, Florida law lets the insurer initially pay actual cash value, which subtracts depreciation, and pay the rest as repairs are done. Keep invoices to recover the holdback.

What is appraisal and should I use it?

Appraisal is a policy process for resolving disagreements about the amount of loss. It can be faster than litigation but has costs and generally cannot decide coverage. Have the clause reviewed before you invoke it.
