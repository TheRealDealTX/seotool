Hurricane Flood Damage Wind or Water?

After a hurricane flood, the first question your insurer asks is not how much was damaged but what water did it. Storm surge, rain flooding and wind-driven rain are treated very differently by Florida homeowners policies and flood policies, and the answer decides who pays. This site explains the rules in plain English, with the Florida statutes and federal flood-policy terms behind them.

Three kinds of hurricane flood water

People say "the house flooded" for three different events. Insurance policies do not. Before you call anyone, work out which of these reached your home, because a single storm often delivers more than one.

Coastal

Storm surge

Seawater pushed ashore by the hurricane's winds and low pressure. It arrives fast, carries sand, seaweed and debris, and leaves a clear horizontal line on walls.

  • Damage concentrated low in the building
  • Salt residue, silt, marine debris
  • Neighbors along the same elevation hit alike

Usually paid by: a flood policy (NFIP or private). Homeowners forms typically exclude it, even when wind drove the water.

Storm surge claims explained

Inland

Rain flooding

Heavy rainfall that overwhelms drainage, overflows canals, lakes and rivers, or pools across yards and streets until it enters the building from the ground up.

  • Water came in under doors or up through floors
  • Muddy, fresh-water line inside
  • Can happen far from the coast and days after landfall

Usually paid by: a flood policy. Rising surface water is a "flood" under the NFIP definition when it covers two or more acres or two or more properties.

How flood policy claims work

From above

Wind-driven rain

Rain forced into the building by hurricane winds, through a roof, window, door, soffit or wall opening that the wind created.

  • Ceiling stains, wet insulation, attic damage
  • Damage above any flood line
  • Missing shingles or tiles, broken glazing

Usually paid by: the homeowners or condo policy's windstorm (hurricane) coverage, if wind first made the opening.

Which policy pays?

One storm, two policies, two adjusters

A Florida home damaged in a hurricane flood is often insured under two separate contracts: a homeowners (or condo, or commercial) policy that covers windstorm, and a flood policy from the National Flood Insurance Program or a private carrier. Each pays only its own peril. Typical results under common policy forms:

    The fight that follows the storm

    When wind damage gets blamed on flood

    The wind-vs-water dispute is the defining hurricane claim problem on the Florida coast. After a surge event, a homeowners insurer may conclude that everything below, and sometimes above, the water line was flood, which its policy excludes. The flood carrier may conclude that the roof and interior were wind, which its policy excludes. The owner is left in the gap.

    Three legal ideas shape who wins that argument:

    • Burden of proof. In general, the policyholder must show a direct physical loss the policy covers, and the insurer bears the burden of proving that an exclusion such as flood applies. Good evidence on both sides of the line matters.
    • Anti-concurrent causation clauses. Many homeowners policies exclude water damage "regardless of any other cause or event contributing concurrently or in any sequence." The Florida Supreme Court applied the concurrent cause doctrine in Sebo v. American Home Assurance Co. (2016) where a policy lacked such language, and Florida appellate courts have enforced clear anti-concurrent wording, so the exact clause in your policy matters.
    • Allocation in total losses. Florida's valued policy law, s. 627.702(1)(b), says that when a total loss is caused partly by a covered peril and partly by an uncovered one, the insurer owes only the part caused by the covered peril, unless the covered peril alone would have caused the total loss.

    Read the full wind vs flood damage guide or see what to do when a claim is denied as flood.

    Do not accept a "flood" label on the phone. Ask for the coverage position in writing. Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), a residential property insurer must give a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy for a payment, denial or partial denial.

    Open both claims. If you carry flood insurance, notify both carriers promptly. Notice to one is not notice to the other, and the two policies run on different clocks.

    Two clocks

    Deadlines for a hurricane flood insurance claim

    The homeowners side runs on Florida statutes. The NFIP side runs on federal regulations. Miss one deadline and you can lose that half of the claim even if the other half is on track. Exact dates depend on your policy and date of loss.

    Homeowners / windstorm

    1. LandfallFor hurricanes, the "date of loss" is the date the hurricane made landfall (s. 627.70132(3)).
    2. 7 daysInsurer must acknowledge your communications about the claim (s. 627.70131(1)).
    3. 30 daysPhysical inspection, if one is needed, within 30 days after the insurer receives proof-of-loss statements (s. 627.70131(3)).
    4. 60 daysPay or deny all or part of the claim after notice, absent factors beyond the insurer's control (s. 627.70131(7)).
    5. 1 yr / 18 moNotice of a new or reopened claim within 1 year of the date of loss; supplemental claims within 18 months (s. 627.70132).
    6. 5 yrsLawsuit for breach of a property insurance contract, measured from the date of loss (s. 95.11(2)(e)), after 10-business-day pre-suit notice (s. 627.70152).

    NFIP flood policy

    1. PromptlyGive prompt written notice of the flood loss to your flood insurer.
    2. 60 daysSigned, sworn proof of loss within 60 days after the loss, unless FEMA grants an extension (SFIP, 44 CFR Part 61, App. A).
    3. 60 daysOptional FEMA appeal within 60 days of the insurer's written denial (44 CFR 62.20). An appeal does not extend other deadlines.
    4. 1 yrLawsuit in U.S. District Court within one year after the written denial of all or part of the claim (42 U.S.C. 4072).

    Before you count the payout

    The hurricane deductible

    Florida homeowners policies usually carry a separate hurricane deductible stated as a percentage of the dwelling limit. Under s. 627.701(3)(a), insurers must offer personal-lines buyers choices of $500, 2%, 5% and 10% (with some exceptions for higher-value homes). On a $400,000 dwelling limit, a 5% deductible is $20,000, and that comes off the wind side of the claim before any payment.

    It applies once per calendar year, not per storm, for personal residential policies issued or renewed since May 2005 (s. 627.701(5)(a)). If a second hurricane hits the same year, the insurer applies the greater of the remaining hurricane deductible or your regular deductible. Flood policies have their own, separate deductibles.

    Evidence

    Document the water line before cleanup erases it

    The single most useful photo in a mixed loss is a tape measure against the interior water line, room by room. It separates flood damage below from wind and rain damage above, and it lets each insurer see its own share.

    • Measure the high-water mark inside and outside, from the floor and from the ground.
    • Photograph roof, soffits, windows and doors from several angles before tarping.
    • Bag and label samples of drywall or insulation from above the water line that are wet.
    • Keep wet carpet or damaged items until the adjusters have seen them, or photograph and keep a sample.
    • Save receipts for tarps, fans, dehumidifiers, lodging and food.

    Questions

    Common questions

    Does homeowners insurance cover hurricane flood damage?

    Usually not the flood part. Standard Florida homeowners forms exclude flood, surface water and storm surge. They do typically cover wind damage, including rain that enters through an opening the wind created. Rising-water damage is normally covered only by a separate flood policy from the NFIP or a private flood insurer.

    Is storm surge considered wind or flood?

    For insurance purposes, storm surge is generally treated as flood, even though the hurricane's wind pushed the water ashore. Homeowners policies commonly exclude water damage from waves, tidal water and storm surge whether or not driven by wind, and the NFIP flood definition includes overflow of tidal waters.

    How long do I have to report hurricane damage in Florida?

    For a homeowners or other property policy, Florida law generally bars a new or reopened claim unless notice is given within 1 year after the date of loss, and a supplemental claim within 18 months (s. 627.70132). For hurricanes the date of loss is the landfall date. An NFIP flood claim needs prompt notice and a proof of loss within 60 days after the loss unless FEMA extends it.

    Can I get paid by both my flood policy and my homeowners policy?

    Yes, for different damage. Each policy pays its own covered peril, and neither pays twice for the same damage. That is why separating flood damage from wind damage with measurements and photos is so important.

    What if my insurer says everything was flood?

    Ask for the written basis for the denial, keep your evidence, and get the policy and photos reviewed. A flood-based denial of wind damage can be challenged with engineering evidence, mediation, appraisal or litigation, depending on the facts and policy. Deadlines continue to run while you dispute.
