Hurricane claim deadlines in Florida

A hurricane that brings both wind and water starts two different clocks: one under Florida's property insurance statutes, one under the federal flood program. Missing either can forfeit that half of the claim. Here are the deadlines that matter, with the statutes behind them and notes on older losses.

Last reviewed: October 2026 · Deadlines depend on your policy and date of loss

The homeowners (windstorm) side

These rules apply to homeowners, condo-unit, dwelling and many commercial property policies covering Florida property, including surplus lines policies, unless a rule says otherwise.

The 60-day pay-or-deny rule applies to residential coverage and to commercial claims on structures of 10,000 square feet or less. It can be extended up to 30 days by an Office of Insurance Regulation order after a declared emergency, and it is tolled during DFS mediation or if you do not provide requested material information within 10 days.

Older losses and changing rules

These deadlines have changed several times. The 1-year and 18-month notice limits took effect December 16, 2022 (Senate Bill 2-A); before that, the limits were 2 years for new or reopened claims and 3 years for supplemental claims, and earlier versions gave windstorm and hurricane claims 3 years. The shorter insurer timelines (7, 30 and 60 days) took effect March 1, 2023, replacing 14-, 45- and 90-day periods. Which version governs an older hurricane depends on the date of loss and the policy, so get advice before assuming an older claim is too late, or still timely.

Dispute routes and how they affect time

  • DFS mediation (s. 627.7015). Available for most personal lines and commercial residential claims before appraisal or suit; the insurer pays the cost of mediation. The insurer's 60-day pay-or-deny period is tolled while mediation is pending.
  • Appraisal. A policy-based process for disputes over the amount of loss. It does not by itself stop the statute of limitations, and invoking it affects when a CRN can be filed on a residential claim.
  • Pre-suit notice (s. 627.70152). Required before suing on a residential or commercial property policy. If a statute of limitations would expire during the 10-business-day notice period, the statute provides that it is tolled for that period.
  • NFIP appeal (44 CFR 62.20). A FEMA appeal does not stop the one-year federal suit deadline.

Do not let the other policy's schedule set yours. A homeowners dispute can take months while the NFIP one-year suit period is running from a partial denial letter. Calendar each deadline separately.

Changes that affect strategy, not just dates

Florida's 2022–2023 reforms changed the economics of property claims. Senate Bill 2-A (December 2022) and House Bill 837 (March 2023) eliminated the one-way attorney fee statutes that previously required insurers to pay a prevailing policyholder's attorney fees in most property insurance suits; for most new disputes, those fee-shifting rules no longer apply. Post-loss assignment of benefits to contractors is prohibited for residential and commercial property policies issued on or after January 1, 2023 (s. 627.7152(13)). Those changes make the pre-suit process, mediation and appraisal more important, and they are reasons to ask early how fees and costs would work in your case.

A simple calendar to build today

  1. Write down the landfall date of the hurricane. That is your date of loss for the homeowners claim.
  2. Add 1 year (notice of a new claim) and 18 months (supplemental claim).
  3. Add 60 days from the flood loss for the NFIP proof of loss, and check for FEMA extensions.
  4. Note the date you gave notice to each insurer; add 60 days for the homeowners pay-or-deny deadline.
  5. When any denial letter arrives, add 60 days (FEMA appeal) and 1 year (federal suit) for flood.
  6. Keep the 5-year homeowners suit limit in view, remembering pre-suit notice must come first.

Deadline FAQ

How long do I have to file a hurricane claim in Florida?

Under current law, notice of a new or reopened property insurance claim generally must be given within 1 year after the date of loss, and a supplemental claim within 18 months (s. 627.70132). For hurricanes, the date of loss is the landfall date. Older losses may be governed by earlier, longer limits.

How long does my insurer have to pay a hurricane claim?

Generally 60 days after receiving notice of the claim to pay or deny all or part of it, unless factors beyond the insurer's control apply (s. 627.70131(7)). Late payments generally bear interest from the date of notice.

What is the deadline to sue my homeowners insurer?

Florida's statute of limitations for breach of a property insurance contract is 5 years from the date of loss (s. 95.11(2)(e)). Before suing, you must give 10 business days' pre-suit notice through DFS (s. 627.70152).

Does a FEMA appeal extend my time to sue on a flood claim?

No. Under 44 CFR 62.20, an appeal does not extend the policy's time limits. Suit must still be filed within one year of the written denial.
