Wind-driven rain claim

Hurricane rain arrives sideways at high speed and finds every weakness in a building. Whether the soaked ceilings, walls and floors are covered depends almost entirely on one fact: did the wind make an opening first? Here is how a wind-driven rain claim is judged in Florida and how to prove it.

Last reviewed: October 2026

The opening rule

Most homeowners and condo policies cover windstorm but restrict coverage for rain. The standard pattern: damage to the interior of a building, or to property inside it, caused by rain, snow, sleet, sand or dust is covered only if the direct force of wind or hail first damages the building and creates an opening in the roof or wall, and the rain enters through that opening.

Florida's statutes use the same idea. Section 627.4025(2)(a) defines "hurricane coverage" as coverage for loss or damage caused by the peril of windstorm during a hurricane, including ensuing damage to the interior of a building, or to property inside a building, caused by rain, snow, sleet, hail, sand, or dust if the direct force of the windstorm first damages the building, causing an opening through which the rain enters and causes damage.

So the rule has three parts, and every wind-driven rain claim turns on them:

  1. Wind damaged the building. Something physical: missing shingles, lifted flashing, a punctured wall, a broken window, a dislodged ridge vent, a torn soffit.
  2. That damage created an opening. Not a pre-existing gap, a worn seal or an open window.
  3. The rain came in through that opening and caused the interior damage being claimed.

Wind-driven rain is not flood

Insurers sometimes lump all hurricane water together. They should not. Rain entering from above through a wind-created opening is a windstorm loss under the homeowners policy. Flood is a general and temporary inundation of normally dry land from overflow of waters, rapid accumulation of surface water, or mudflow, and it enters from the ground up. A room can have both: a flood line at 18 inches and a collapsed, soaked ceiling above it. The ceiling is a wind-driven rain claim; the bottom 18 inches belong to the flood policy. See wind vs flood damage for how mixed rooms are split.

Finding and proving the opening

Openings are often small and easily lost in cleanup or repair. Before any roofer replaces anything:

  • Photograph each roof slope, ridge, hip, valley and penetration (vents, skylights, pipe boots, chimneys).
  • Photograph from the attic: daylight through the decking, wet truss stains, displaced insulation, water tracking down rafters.
  • Match interior stains to the exterior opening directly above or uphill of them, and take wide shots that show both locations in context.
  • Photograph broken windows, doors pushed out of their frames, torn screens and soffit or fascia pulled away.
  • Keep pieces: missing shingles found in the yard, broken glass, a dislodged vent cap.
  • Note the time water first appeared, and whether it was during the strongest winds.

Moisture-meter readings, infrared images and a roofer's or engineer's written opinion can tie these together. For more, see the documentation guide.

Roofs, tarps and the duty to mitigate

Policies require reasonable steps to prevent further damage after a loss. A blue tarp over an opening, water extraction and drying equipment are normally treated as part of the claim if reasonable, so keep receipts and photos of the work. Mitigation does not erase evidence if you photograph first. Delays in tarping, on the other hand, can give an insurer an argument that later damage came from neglect rather than the storm.

Florida has specific roof rules too. Some policies carry a separate roof deductible, which must be disclosed in large bold type and shown as a dollar amount on the declarations page (s. 627.701(4)(e)), and some policies pay for older roof coverings under a reduced reimbursement schedule. Check your declarations page and endorsements for both before you estimate what the wind side of the claim is worth.

Timelines that apply

A wind-driven rain claim is a homeowners or commercial property claim, so Florida's property-claim timelines apply. Notice of a new claim is generally barred unless given within 1 year after the date of loss, which for hurricanes is the landfall date; supplemental claims within 18 months (s. 627.70132). The insurer generally has 60 days after notice to pay or deny (s. 627.70131(7)). The full list is in our claim deadlines guide.

Wind-driven rain FAQ

Is wind-driven rain covered by homeowners insurance in Florida?

Generally yes, if the wind first damaged the building and created an opening through which the rain entered. Rain that gets in through closed windows, worn seals or pre-existing gaps, without wind damage, is often excluded.

Does the hurricane deductible apply to wind-driven rain damage?

Usually yes. If the damage happened during the statutory hurricane period, interior rain damage through a wind-created opening is part of the hurricane loss, so the hurricane deductible applies to the combined wind and rain damage.

Rain came in under the door during the hurricane. Is that wind-driven rain?

It depends where the water came from. Rain blown under a door that the wind damaged or forced open may be a wind claim. Water that pooled outside and rose in from the ground is usually treated as flood or surface water and excluded from homeowners coverage.

What if the roofer already fixed the roof?

Gather the roofer's photos, invoices and written description of what was found, plus any removed materials. Insurers can still evaluate the claim, but missing evidence of the opening makes disputes harder, so photograph before repairs whenever it is safe.
