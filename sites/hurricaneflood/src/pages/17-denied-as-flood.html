Hurricane claim denied as flood

It is one of the most common letters after a Florida hurricane: the homeowners insurer says the damage was caused by flood, surface water or storm surge, which the policy excludes. Sometimes that is right. Often it sweeps in roof, ceiling and upper-wall damage that wind caused. Here is how to respond.

Last reviewed: October 2026

Step 1: Read the letter for what it actually says

Florida requires a residential property insurer to give a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy, in relation to the facts or applicable law, for a payment, denial or partial denial (s. 627.70131(7)(a)). Look for:

  • Full or partial denial? Many "flood" letters deny only part of the claim. Identify exactly which items or areas were denied.
  • Which exclusion? Flood, surface water, waves, tidal water, storm surge, or a general "water damage" exclusion. Note whether it includes anti-concurrent causation wording ("regardless of any other cause or event contributing concurrently or in any sequence").
  • What evidence? Was there an engineer's report? A desk review based only on photos? Ask for copies of the report, photographs and the adjuster's estimate.
  • The date of the letter, which matters for your next deadlines.

Step 2: Test the denial against the physical evidence

A flood exclusion can only reach damage that flood caused, or, under clear anti-concurrent language, damage flood caused together with wind. It does not reach damage that wind caused alone. Ask these questions:

  • Is there damage above the interior water line? Ceilings, upper walls, attic, second floor?
  • Is there roof, window, door or soffit damage that created openings for rain?
  • Did damage occur before the water arrived, during the peak winds?
  • Did neighbors outside the flooded area suffer similar wind damage?
  • Was the "flood" actually rain coming through a wind-damaged roof (a wind-driven rain loss)?

The documentation checklist explains how to build that record, and the wind vs flood guide explains how courts and insurers analyze mixed causes, including Sebo v. American Home Assurance Co. and Florida's valued policy law, s. 627.702(1)(b).

Step 3: Protect the flood claim at the same time

If you have flood insurance, do not wait for the homeowners dispute to finish. File with the flood insurer, submit the NFIP proof of loss within 60 days after the loss (unless FEMA extends it), and calendar one year from any written flood denial for a federal lawsuit. If the flood insurer says the same damage was wind, keep both insurers' positions in writing; contradictory positions can be powerful evidence. Details are in the flood claim guide.

Step 5: Keep the clocks in view

Disputing a denial does not pause most deadlines. A supplemental claim must generally be noticed within 18 months of the date of loss (s. 627.70132), the homeowners suit limit is 5 years from the date of loss (s. 95.11(2)(e)), and NFIP suit is due one year after written denial. See all of them in the deadlines guide.

When to get legal help

Consider talking to a property insurance attorney when the denial relies on an anti-concurrent causation clause, when an engineer's report is the main evidence against you, when the home is a total or near-total loss, when the insurer applies the hurricane deductible in a way you do not understand, or when the flood and homeowners insurers each blame the other's peril. Pre-suit notice under s. 627.70152 requires a specific presuit settlement demand and supporting detail, and the 2022–2023 reforms changed how attorney fees work in these cases, so it pays to understand the process before you start it.

Denied-as-flood FAQ

Can my insurer deny roof damage because my house flooded?

A flood exclusion applies to damage caused by flood. Roof damage from wind, and rain damage through a wind-created opening, are generally windstorm losses. Whether an anti-concurrent causation clause affects any of it depends on the policy wording and the facts.

Is DFS mediation available for a flood-based denial?

For most personal lines and commercial residential homeowners claims, yes, under s. 627.7015, before appraisal or suit. It does not apply to NFIP flood policies, which follow federal procedures.

Do I have to send a notice before suing my homeowners insurer?

Yes. Section 627.70152 requires written notice of intent to initiate litigation, filed through the Department of Financial Services, at least 10 business days before filing suit and after the insurer's coverage determination.
