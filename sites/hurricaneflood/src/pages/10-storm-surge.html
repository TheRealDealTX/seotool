Storm surge damage insurance claim

Storm surge is the most destructive water a hurricane brings to Florida, and the most misunderstood on paper. Here is how a storm surge damage insurance claim actually moves through a flood policy, why the homeowners policy usually says no, and where the wind side of the same loss still gets paid.

Last reviewed: October 2026 · Florida and federal flood-policy rules

What storm surge is, in insurance terms

Storm surge is an abnormal rise of seawater generated by a storm, over and above the predicted astronomical tide. Hurricane winds pile water against the coastline and the storm's low pressure lifts the sea surface. On Florida's shallow Gulf shelf in particular, surge can push several feet of saltwater inland in a matter of hours, often carrying waves, sand and debris with it.

Insurance policies do not care that the wind caused the surge. They classify the damage by the water that did it. Under the National Flood Insurance Program's Standard Flood Insurance Policy (SFIP), "flood" includes a general and temporary condition of partial or complete inundation of two or more acres of normally dry land, or of two or more properties (one of which is yours), from the overflow of inland or tidal waters. Storm surge fits that definition squarely. It also covers collapse or subsidence of land along a shore caused by waves or currents exceeding anticipated cyclical levels.

Why the homeowners policy usually refuses surge damage

Most Florida homeowners, condo-unit and dwelling policies contain a water damage exclusion that lists flood, surface water, waves, tidal water, overflow of a body of water and storm surge. Many forms add the words "whether or not driven by wind." That phrase is aimed squarely at hurricanes: it tells you the insurer does not intend to pay for wind-pushed water, even though the policy covers windstorm.

Many of these exclusions are also introduced by anti-concurrent causation language, which says the exclusion applies regardless of any other cause contributing concurrently or in any sequence. Where a policy has clear wording of that kind, Florida courts have generally enforced it. Where it does not, Florida follows the concurrent cause doctrine described by the Florida Supreme Court in Sebo v. American Home Assurance Co. (2016). The exact wording of your policy is therefore the first thing to read. Our wind vs flood damage guide walks through how that plays out.

Bottom line: if surge water entered the home, expect the homeowners insurer to exclude the damage the surge caused and to look to your flood policy. The fight is about where surge damage stops and wind damage starts.

What the flood policy pays after a surge

Most Florida flood coverage is written through the NFIP, either directly or through "Write Your Own" private companies that issue the federal SFIP and handle claims under federal rules. Key features of the Dwelling Form for a house:

ItemNFIP Dwelling Form, in general
Building (Coverage A)Up to $250,000 for a one-to-four family residence. Replacement cost for a single-family principal residence insured to at least 80% of its replacement cost (or the program maximum); otherwise actual cash value.
Contents (Coverage B)Up to $100,000, only if purchased; paid at actual cash value.
Increased Cost of Compliance (Coverage D)Up to $30,000 toward elevation, floodproofing, relocation or demolition required by local floodplain rules after substantial damage.
Not coveredAdditional living expenses, loss of use, most property outside the building, and most finishes and contents in basements and below the lowest elevated floor of certain elevated buildings.
DeductiblesSeparate building and contents deductibles shown on your declarations page. The Florida hurricane deductible does not apply to flood policies.

Private flood policies sold in Florida are governed by their own contract terms and by s. 627.715, Florida Statutes. Some offer higher limits, replacement cost on contents or living expenses; some are narrower. Read the declarations page and the definition of "flood" in the policy you actually have.

The wind side of a surge loss

A home that took surge water almost always took wind as well. Even when the first floor was flooded, the homeowners policy's windstorm coverage may still owe for:

  • Roof covering, decking, flashing and vents damaged by wind.
  • Rain damage to ceilings, insulation, upper walls and contents above the water line, where wind first created an opening (see wind-driven rain claims).
  • Windows, doors, soffits, siding, screen enclosures and fences damaged by wind or wind-borne debris before or above the surge.
  • Loss of use or additional living expenses if wind damage alone would have made the home unlivable, subject to the policy's terms.

Florida's valued policy law adds a twist for total losses. Under s. 627.702(1)(b), when a building is a total loss caused partly by a covered peril (wind) and partly by an uncovered one (flood), the homeowners insurer owes only the amount of the loss caused by the covered peril, unless the covered peril alone would have caused the total loss. On a slab where only pilings remain, that question is usually answered with engineering evidence, neighborhood comparisons and weather data.

Step by step: filing a storm surge damage claim in Florida

  1. Get safe, then notify both insurers. Report the flood loss to your flood carrier and the wind loss to your homeowners carrier. They are separate claims with separate numbers and adjusters.
  2. Record the surge line. Before mucking out, photograph a tape measure at the interior and exterior high-water marks in every room and on every side of the building. Our documentation guide has a full checklist.
  3. Mitigate. Both policies expect you to protect the property from further damage. Remove standing water, dry out and tarp where safe, and keep receipts. The SFIP includes limited coverage for reasonable removal of property to safety and for debris removal.
  4. Calendar the NFIP proof of loss. The SFIP requires a signed, sworn proof of loss within 60 days after the loss, unless FEMA extends that deadline for a disaster. An adjuster's report is not automatically a proof of loss unless the insurer accepts it and you sign it.
  5. Track the Florida claim clock. Under s. 627.70131(7)(a) a residential property insurer generally must pay or deny all or part of the wind claim within 60 days after receiving notice, and explain the basis in writing.
  6. Compare estimates line by line. Make sure each item is assigned to one insurer or the other, and that nothing (attic insulation, upper drywall, roof decking) falls through the gap.
  7. Dispute in time. NFIP denials can be appealed to FEMA within 60 days of the written denial, but suit must still be filed within one year of the written denial. Homeowners disputes can go to DFS mediation, appraisal if the policy provides it, or litigation after the s. 627.70152 pre-suit notice.

Surge damage without flood insurance

If you had no flood policy, the surge damage itself is usually not covered by homeowners insurance. Options then narrow to FEMA Individual Assistance if the area receives a federal disaster declaration, SBA disaster loans, and any wind damage your homeowners policy does cover. Do not let a missing flood policy stop you from pursuing the wind claim: roofs, upper floors and wind-driven rain are commonly a large share of a coastal loss.

Storm surge claim FAQ

Does my homeowners policy cover storm surge in Florida?

Generally no. Most Florida homeowners policies exclude flood, waves, tidal water and storm surge, often "whether or not driven by wind." Surge damage is usually covered only by a flood policy. Wind damage to the same home is still a homeowners claim.

How long do I have to submit an NFIP proof of loss after a surge?

The Standard Flood Insurance Policy requires a signed, sworn proof of loss within 60 days after the loss. FEMA sometimes extends this after major hurricanes; check FEMA bulletins or ask your flood insurer in writing, and keep a copy of what you send.

Will the NFIP pay for a hotel while my home is repaired?

No. The NFIP Dwelling Form excludes additional living expenses and loss of use. Some private flood policies include living expenses, and your homeowners policy may pay loss of use if a covered peril such as wind made the home unlivable.

My house was a total loss from wind and surge. What does the homeowners insurer owe?

Under Florida's valued policy law, s. 627.702(1)(b), when a total loss is caused partly by a covered peril and partly by an excluded one, the insurer owes the part caused by the covered peril, unless the covered peril alone would have caused the total loss. Evidence about what the wind did before the surge arrived is central.

Can I sue my flood insurer in state court?

No. A suit on an NFIP policy must be filed in the United States District Court for the district where the property is located, within one year after the date of the written denial of all or part of the claim (42 U.S.C. 4072 and the SFIP).
