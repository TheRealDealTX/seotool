How a Florida hurricane claim is supposed to run

Every Destin homeowners policy is a contract with deadlines on both sides. Yours: report the loss promptly, protect the property from further damage, let the insurer inspect, and give it the documents it reasonably asks for. The insurer's duties are written into Chapter 627 of the Florida Statutes, mostly in section 627.70131:

  • Acknowledge your communication about the claim within 7 calendar days.
  • Begin its investigation within 7 days of receiving proof-of-loss statements, and complete any physical inspection within 30 days of them. The adjuster must identify themselves with a name and Florida license number.
  • Pay or deny the claim, in whole or in part, within 60 days of notice, unless factors beyond its control prevent it, and explain the decision in writing with reference to the policy.
  • Explain in writing why a payment is lower than any detailed estimate it prepared, and pay interest when it pays late.

When those things happen and the estimate matches the damage, there is nothing for a lawyer to do. Problems start when one of them does not.

What a hurricane claim lawyer actually does

A claim dispute is usually a dispute about facts: what the storm damaged, what caused it, and what it costs to repair to the Florida Building Code. A lawyer's job is to build the record that answers those questions and then use the procedures Florida law provides.

  1. Read the whole policy. Endorsements change the deal: roof payment schedules that pay older roofs at actual cash value, percentage hurricane deductibles, water-damage sublimits, ordinance-or-law coverage for code upgrades, and anti-concurrent-causation language for wind-and-water losses.
  2. Get the claim file. Adjuster photos, the estimate, engineer reports and recorded statements often reveal what the denial letter leaves out.
  3. Document the damage independently. Line-item estimates, roof inspections and, when cause is disputed, engineering opinions.
  4. Present and negotiate. Many disputes resolve once the insurer sees a complete, priced claim.
  5. Use the formal tools. Appraisal where the policy allows it, the Department of Financial Services mediation program under s. 627.7015, a Civil Remedy Notice under s. 624.155 for bad-faith conduct, and the pre-suit notice under s. 627.70152 that must be filed at least 10 business days before a property insurance lawsuit.

Roof claims on the Emerald Coast

Roofs are where most Destin hurricane claims are won or lost. Gulf-front homes and condos take sustained wind and salt; a roof that looks intact from the street can have lifted tabs, broken seals and creased shingles that let water in at the next rain.

Things to know before the adjuster arrives

  • Repair versus replace. Florida's building code has long required that when more than 25% of a roof section is repaired or replaced within 12 months, the whole section meet current code. Under s. 553.844(5), added in 2022, a roof built, repaired or replaced to the 2007 Florida Building Code or later needs only the repaired portion brought to current code. Whether your roof qualifies, and how much of it is damaged, are frequent points of disagreement.
  • Matching. Shingles and tiles discontinued years ago rarely match. For replacement-cost homeowners policies, s. 626.9744(2) requires reasonable repair or replacement in adjoining areas when replaced items do not match in quality, color or size; insurers often price only the missing pieces.
  • Roof age and payment schedules. Some policies pay older roofs at actual cash value. Separately, under s. 627.7011(5) an insurer may not refuse to write or renew a policy solely because a roof is under 15 years old.
  • Solicitation rules. Section 489.147 bars contractors from offering gifts or deductible waivers in exchange for a roof inspection or claim. A “free roof” pitch is a warning sign.

Water damage after a hurricane

Rain that enters through an opening the wind created, such as a lifted shingle, blown-out window or damaged soffit, is part of a wind claim. Florida's statutory definition of hurricane coverage in s. 627.4025 includes that ensuing interior damage. Insurers push back by calling stains “long-term” or “repeated seepage,” or by pointing to mold exclusions and sublimits.

The best answer is speed and a clear path of water: photos of the opening directly above the damage, photos taken right after the storm, moisture readings, and the drying company's daily logs. Dry out within a day or two and keep every receipt; reasonable emergency measures are your duty under the policy and are generally part of the claim.

Wind versus storm surge on the coast

Homeowners and wind policies exclude flood, including storm surge. Flood is paid only by an NFIP or private flood policy, which has its own deductibles, its own limits (NFIP building coverage tops out at $250,000 for a home) and its own deadlines, including a proof of loss generally due within 60 days. On Okaloosa Island, Holiday Isle or the bayfront, the same house can have both kinds of damage, and each insurer may point at the other.

Separate the evidence: water lines on walls and debris lines outside for the flood claim; roof, window, siding and soffit damage above the water line for the wind claim. If you were there, write down when the wind damage happened relative to the rising water. File with both carriers. Destin-specific problems we see

Short-term rentals

Vacation rentals need the right policy form. Lost rental income may sit under a separate coverage part, and the insurer may question whether the unit was owner-occupied.

Condo towers

The association's master policy covers the building; your HO-6 covers interiors, betterments and loss assessments. Assessments after a big storm can be large. Condo claim guide.

Second homes

Owners who live out of state find damage late. Reporting deadlines still run from landfall, so arrange an inspection quickly.

Surplus-lines policies

Many coastal homes are insured by non-admitted carriers. Section 627.70132's notice deadlines expressly apply to surplus-lines property policies too.
