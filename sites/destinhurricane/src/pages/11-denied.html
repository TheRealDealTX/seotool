The denial reasons Destin homeowners see most

  • “Wear, tear and deterioration”The adjuster attributes missing or creased shingles to age and sun rather than the storm. Common on Gulf-facing roofs, where UV and salt are real but so is a 100 mph eyewall.
  • “Flood or surge, excluded”Everything below a water line is called flood, including drywall soaked by rain that came through a broken window hours before the surge arrived.
  • “Pre-existing damage”The insurer points to Sally in 2020, or to an earlier claim, and says the damage was already there.
  • “Long-term leak”Interior staining is labeled repeated seepage over 14 days or more, which many policies exclude.
  • “Below deductible”Technically a payment decision, not a denial, but the effect is the same: the estimate lands under a 2% or 5% hurricane deductible.
  • “Late notice” or “failure to mitigate”The insurer says it was told too late, or that the damage grew because nobody tarped the roof. Common with second homes and rentals.
  • “Material misrepresentation”A denial based on statements in a recorded interview or examination under oath, or on the application.

Every one of these is a factual claim the insurer has to be able to back up. Many rest on a single inspection done during a catastrophe, when field adjusters may handle a dozen or more homes a day.

What Florida requires in a denial

Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), within 60 days after it receives notice of an initial, reopened or supplemental claim, the insurer must pay or deny the claim or a portion of it, unless factors beyond its control prevent that, and it must give you a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy, in relation to the facts or applicable law, for a denial or partial denial. If it pays less than its own detailed estimate shows, it must explain the difference in writing. Keep the letter and the envelope or email it came in.

A denial letter that quotes an exclusion but never ties it to what happened at your property is weaker than it looks.

A five-step response plan

  1. Request the complete policy with every endorsement, plus the adjuster's estimate and photographs. Some letters cite endorsements you have never read.
  2. Preserve evidence. Keep damaged shingles, flooring samples and photos. If repairs cannot wait, photograph each stage. Use the damage log.
  3. Get your own inspection. A licensed roofer or general contractor's line-item estimate, and in cause disputes an engineer's opinion.
  4. Put weather on the record. For Hurricane Isaias, the National Hurricane Center documented landfall near Destin at 105 mph and a 116 mph gust near Santa Rosa Sound. Nearby observations help answer “was it windy enough?”
  5. Talk to a lawyer before any examination under oath or recorded statement about a disputed claim, and before signing any release.
