Step one: read the denial letter closely

When a roof leak claim is denied, Florida law requires the insurer to give you a reasonable explanation in writing of the basis in the policy, in relation to the facts or applicable law, for the denial or partial denial (s. 627.70131(7)(a)). Pull that letter out and identify:

  • The exact policy provisions quoted – exclusion names, section numbers, and endorsement form numbers.
  • The facts the insurer relied on: adjuster notes, an engineer report, photos, or a claim of late notice.
  • Whether it is a full denial, a partial denial (for example, interior paid but roof denied), or a payment that only looks like an approval because it falls under the deductible.
  • Any deadline the letter mentions for requesting reconsideration, submitting documents, or appraisal.

Ask the insurer in writing for a complete copy of your policy and for the adjuster’s detailed estimate and any engineering or inspection report it relied on. Insurers must send detailed adjuster estimates within 7 days after they are generated (s. 627.70131(3)(e)).

Many of these are fact disputes about what caused the leak and when. A denial that rests on a short inspection, a few photos, or a desk review can sometimes be answered with better evidence: a licensed roofer or engineer’s report, weather data for the date of loss, moisture readings, and your own timeline.

When the claim was paid, but not enough

An underpaid roof claim is just as common as a denied one. Typical problems include an estimate that repairs a few shingles when the slope can’t be matched or repaired, omitted line items (drip edge, underlayment, permits, code upgrades), depreciation applied too heavily, or a payment schedule endorsement applied in a way the policy doesn’t support. Florida’s 25% roof rule and any law and ordinance coverage in your policy can change the math significantly.

If the insurer pays less than its own detailed estimate, s. 627.70131(7)(a) requires a written explanation of the difference.

Your options after a denial or low offer

1. Ask for reconsideration with new evidence

Send a short, factual letter with photos, your timeline, and any roofer or engineer report. Keep copies and note when it was sent. This sometimes resolves partial denials without anything more formal. If you have additional damage from the same event, a supplemental claim must be noticed within 18 months after the date of loss for current losses (s. 627.70132).

2. Florida DFS mediation

The Department of Financial Services runs a free, informal mediation program for residential property claims under s. 627.7015. A claim becomes eligible after the insurer has made its pay-or-deny decision (or elected to reinspect under the pre-suit process). The insurer pays the mediator’s cost, you can bring a lawyer, and nothing is binding unless both sides agree to a settlement. Learn more from the DFS Division of Consumer Services.

3. Appraisal

Most Florida homeowners policies contain an appraisal clause. Each side picks an appraiser, and an umpire resolves disagreements about the amount of loss. Appraisal is usually suited to pricing disputes rather than whether a loss is covered at all, and it involves costs, so read the clause carefully before invoking it.

4. Pre-suit notice and a lawsuit

Before suing under a property policy, s. 627.70152 requires a written notice of intent to initiate litigation filed through DFS at least 10 business days before suit, and not before the insurer has made a coverage determination. After a denial, the insurer must respond within 10 business days by accepting coverage, continuing to deny, or asserting a right to reinspect (with 14 business days to reinspect and decide). For disputes over amount, it must make a settlement offer or demand appraisal or another form of ADR. The statute of limitations for breach of a property insurance contract is generally five years from the date of loss (s. 95.11(2)(e)).

5. Civil Remedy Notice

Where an insurer has not attempted in good faith to settle a claim, s. 624.155 provides a bad-faith remedy that requires a Civil Remedy Notice filed with DFS and 60 days for the insurer to cure. Florida’s 2022–2023 reforms changed several rules around these claims, so this is an area to discuss with a lawyer.

What about attorney fees?

For many years, Florida law let a policyholder who won a judgment against an insurer recover attorney fees from the insurer. The 2022–2023 reforms eliminated that one-way fee rule for most property insurance cases, so how a lawyer is paid matters more than it used to. Mistakes that can hurt a denied claim

  • Tearing out the damaged roof or drywall before it is documented.
  • Letting the 1-year or 18-month notice windows pass while “waiting to see.”
  • Signing a contractor agreement that claims to transfer your claim, or letting a roofer argue coverage for you.
  • Giving a recorded statement or examination under oath without understanding what the policy requires.
  • Cashing a check marked “final” without understanding whether it closes the claim.

FAQ

Can I reopen a denied roof leak claim?

Yes, in many cases. A reopened claim under current s. 627.70132 must be noticed within 1 year after the date of loss, and a supplemental claim within 18 months. Submitting new evidence of storm damage can lead an insurer to change its decision.

How long does DFS mediation take?

DFS schedules conferences after a request is processed, and the insurer’s claim-handling deadlines are tolled while mediation is pending. A single conference is typical. Settlement is voluntary.

Should I hire a lawyer or a public adjuster?

Public adjusters help value and present claims; lawyers can also advise on coverage, handle pre-suit notice and litigation, and evaluate bad-faith issues. Many homeowners consult a lawyer once a claim has actually been denied.
