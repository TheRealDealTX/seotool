What a wear and tear roof denial means

Nearly every homeowners policy excludes wear and tear, marring, deterioration, and often related causes such as rust, mechanical breakdown, latent defects, and faulty workmanship or maintenance. A wear and tear roof denial applies that exclusion: the insurer is saying the leak happened because the roof was old or poorly maintained, not because of a sudden covered event like wind or hail.

The exclusion is legitimate – insurance is not a maintenance contract. But it is also easy to apply too broadly. Florida roofs live hard lives under sun, heat and storms, so almost any roof over a few years old shows some aging. The real question is whether age caused this leak, or whether a storm did damage that the aging simply sits alongside.

Why insurers lean on age

  • It shifts the cause. If the cause is excluded, the insurer doesn’t need to argue about scope or price.
  • Older roofs show signs that look similar. Granule loss, blistering, curling, cracked sealant and brittle shingles can be mistaken for, or used to explain away, wind creasing or hail bruising.
  • Short inspections. A quick visit, a few photos from a ladder or a drone, or a desk review of photos can miss localized storm damage.
  • Interior water gets bundled in. Once the roof is called “deteriorated,” the interior water damage is often denied as long-term seepage too.

Age damage vs. storm damage: what to look for

Usually points to ageCan point to a storm
Uniform granule loss across all slopesDamage concentrated on windward slopes or edges
Curling or cupping across the field of the roofHorizontal creases at the nail line of lifted tabs
Thermal cracking in a regular patternMissing tabs or shingles found in the yard after a storm
Long-dried, layered ceiling stainsA fresh stain that appeared right after a weather event
Rotted decking with no recent openingDisplaced or cracked tiles, bent flashing or vents

No single sign settles the question; a qualified inspector looks at the whole pattern and the timeline. Hail bruises, for example, are evaluated by feel and pattern, not just photos.

Evidence that answers a wear and tear denial

  1. Your timeline. When did you first see water, and what happened just before? A sudden leak after a known storm is powerful context.
  2. Weather records. Wind gust or hail reports near your home for the date of loss. Under s. 627.70132(3), the date of loss for weather events is tied to NOAA-verified dates.
  3. A licensed roofer’s or engineer’s report that explains damage slope by slope, with marked photos, and addresses the insurer’s theory directly.
  4. Roof age records. Permits, invoices and inspection reports. Insurers sometimes use the wrong installation date.
  5. Maintenance history. Receipts for inspections or repairs show the roof was cared for.
  6. The insurer’s own file. Ask for the adjuster’s estimate, photos, and any engineer report. You’re entitled to detailed adjuster estimates within 7 days after they are generated (s. 627.70131(3)(e)).

When the exclusion applies only in part

Even when some deterioration exists, coverage isn’t always all-or-nothing. Many policies distinguish between the excluded cause itself and ensuing or resulting loss from a covered peril. Whether an ensuing-loss clause helps depends on exact policy wording, and Florida courts read those clauses carefully. Practical examples where partial coverage often comes up:

  • A storm tore off shingles on an older roof – the storm damage may be covered even though the roof was near the end of its life (subject to depreciation or a roof payment schedule).
  • The roof itself is denied as worn, but a sudden storm-created opening let rain damage the interior.
  • A tree or limb punctured an aging roof; the falling object, not age, caused the opening.

In Florida the insurer generally bears the burden of proving that an exclusion applies once the homeowner shows a loss within the policy’s coverage, which is why the quality of the insurer’s evidence matters.

Don’t mix up the 15-year roof law

Homeowners sometimes read that Florida “protects roofs under 15 years old.” Section 627.7011(5) does say that, for policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2022, an insurer may not refuse to issue or renew a policy solely because a roof is less than 15 years old. That is an underwriting protection. It does not stop an insurer from investigating whether a particular leak came from wear and tear, though a relatively new roof can make an age-based denial harder to support.

FAQ

Can an insurer deny a roof claim just because the roof is old?

Age alone isn’t a cause of loss. The insurer has to tie the damage to an excluded cause such as deterioration. If a covered event like wind or hail caused the damage, an older roof may still be covered, though often at a depreciated or scheduled amount.

How old is “too old” for a roof claim in Florida?

There is no single legal cutoff for claims. Roof age affects underwriting rules under s. 627.7011(5) and may reduce payment under a roof schedule or ACV endorsement, but coverage still turns on the cause of the damage.

Should I get my own engineer report?

In a disputed cause-of-loss case, an independent report from a licensed roofer or engineer is often the most effective response to an insurer’s engineer. Make sure it addresses the insurer’s specific findings.

Does a wear and tear denial affect my water damage claim?

Often the insurer denies both together. Whether interior damage is covered can depend on whether a covered event created the opening and on how long water entered, so it is worth evaluating separately.
