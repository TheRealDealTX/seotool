Before you call the insurer

A Florida roof leak insurance claim starts the moment you notice water, not the moment you call. Two policy duties apply right away in nearly every homeowners form: give prompt notice, and protect the property from further damage. Neither requires you to have everything figured out.

  • Stop or slow the water. Buckets, towels, moving furniture, and a professional tarp if the roof is open. Reasonable emergency repair costs are commonly reimbursable – keep receipts and before/after photos.
  • Photograph and video first. Ceilings, walls, floors, the attic if safe, and the exterior from the ground. Turn on timestamps.
  • Write a short timeline. The date you first saw water, any storm before that, and what you did. This becomes the backbone of the claim.
  • Don’t throw out evidence. If damaged drywall or flooring has to come out for drying, photograph it and keep samples where practical.

Notice deadlines under s. 627.70132

Florida sets an outside deadline for reporting property claims. Under the current version of s. 627.70132, Florida Statutes, a claim or reopened claim is barred unless notice was given to the insurer, in accordance with the policy, within 1 year after the date of loss. A supplemental claim – for additional damage from the same peril the insurer already adjusted – must be noticed within 18 months after the date of loss.

These windows were shortened in 2021 and again in Florida’s December 2022 special-session reforms, so losses from earlier years may be governed by older, longer periods. For storm-related claims, s. 627.70132(3) defines the date of loss as the date the hurricane made landfall or the date NOAA verifies the windstorm, severe rain or other weather event.

The statute is the outer limit, not the goal. Your policy also requires prompt notice. Waiting months lets an insurer argue the delay made the damage worse or prevented a fair inspection. Report as soon as you reasonably can.

These timelines can be extended in limited situations defined as “factors beyond the control of the insurer,” such as an Office of Insurance Regulation order after a declared emergency, and they are tolled during DFS mediation or a policy’s alternative dispute resolution process. Payment made after the 60-day deadline generally carries interest under s. 627.70131(7)(a). You should also receive a Homeowner Claims Bill of Rights within 14 days of first reporting the claim (s. 627.7142).

The adjuster who inspects must give you a document with their name and Florida adjuster license number, and later communications about the claim should include that information too.

Getting ready for the inspection

The inspection is where many roof claims are won or lost. Insurers may inspect in person or use photos, video calls or drones (s. 627.70131(3)(d)).

  1. Be there. Walk the adjuster through your timeline and show every area of damage, inside and out.
  2. Have your own documentation ready – photos from day one, receipts, and any roofer or engineer report you obtained.
  3. Point out storm indicators such as lifted or creased shingles, missing tabs, displaced tiles, damaged vents or flashing, and debris.
  4. Ask what the adjuster is seeing, and take your own photos of the areas they photograph.
  5. Answer questions truthfully and only about what you know. If you don’t know when a stain first appeared, say so rather than guessing.

Estimates, partial payments and how the money flows

A first estimate is often not the final word. Florida requires insurers to label preliminary estimates and partial payments with a bold notice that the insurer is continuing to evaluate the claim (s. 627.70131(6)). If the insurer pays less than its own detailed estimate, it must explain the difference in writing.

For a replacement-cost policy, s. 627.7011(3)(a) generally requires the insurer to pay at least the actual cash value (replacement cost minus depreciation) first, less the deductible, and then pay the remaining replacement cost as work is performed and expenses are incurred. If a separate roof deductible applies, the insurer may limit the roof payment to actual cash value until you show you paid that deductible. Checks are often made out to you and your mortgage company together.

Some policies also allow the insurer to repair the property itself through a managed-repair program. Read your policy before signing any repair contract, and see our roof damage insurance claim Florida guide for repair-versus-replace issues.

Contractors, assignments and solicitation

Florida tightened the rules on roofers and claims:

  • No assignment of benefits on newer policies. For residential policies issued on or after January 1, 2023, a policyholder may not assign post-loss benefits; an attempted assignment is void (s. 627.7152(13)). Older policies issued between July 1, 2019 and January 1, 2023 were subject to strict assignment rules.
  • Contractor limits. Under s. 489.147, contractors may not offer gifts or deductible waivers for a roof inspection or claim, may not use advertisements that omit required fraud and deductible warnings, and may not interpret your policy or adjust your claim unless licensed as a public adjuster.
  • Cancellation rights. After a declared state of emergency, a homeowner may cancel a roof repair contract signed within 180 days of the event within 10 days or by the official start date, whichever comes first (s. 489.147(6)).

When the claim goes wrong

If the insurer denies the claim, pays only a fraction of the repair cost, or blows past the 60-day deadline, you have options: request DFS mediation, invoke appraisal if your policy has it, or speak with a lawyer about pre-suit notice under s. 627.70152. FAQ

Do I have to file a sworn proof of loss for a roof leak claim?

Many policies let the insurer request a sworn proof of loss, often within a set number of days of the request. Check your policy’s “Duties After Loss” section and calendar any deadline in the insurer’s letter.

Can I repair my roof before the adjuster comes?

You should make temporary repairs to prevent further damage, and keep receipts and photos. Permanent repairs before the insurer has a chance to inspect can create disputes, so document thoroughly and notify the insurer first when you can.

What if the insurer doesn’t respond within 60 days?

Section 627.70131(7) requires a pay-or-deny decision within 60 days of notice in most cases, and late payments generally accrue interest. A missed deadline is a violation of the insurance code, though by itself it is not a standalone basis for a lawsuit. It is a good time to get a claim review.

How long do I have to sue my insurer in Florida?

Section 95.11 sets a five-year period for an action for breach of a property insurance contract, running from the date of loss, and s. 627.70152 requires pre-suit notice. Notice deadlines under s. 627.70132 are much shorter, so the reporting deadline is usually the one to watch.
