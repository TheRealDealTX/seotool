The short answer

Is a roof leak covered by homeowners insurance in Florida? The honest answer is “it depends on the cause.” A standard Florida homeowners policy covers your house against direct physical loss unless an exclusion applies. Insurers then apply a group of exclusions that hit roofs harder than almost any other part of the home: wear and tear, deterioration, faulty workmanship or materials, and continuous or repeated leakage of water.

So the coverage question becomes a cause question. If a covered event caused the opening that let water in, the roof damage and the water damage that followed are generally covered, subject to your deductible and limits. If the roof simply wore out, the insurer will usually deny the claim for the roof itself – though, as discussed below, that is not always the end of the story for the interior damage.

What Florida policies typically cover

Every policy is different, and you should read your own declarations page and policy form. That said, the following situations are commonly covered:

  • Wind damage. Shingles, tiles or metal panels lifted, creased or torn off by wind, including hurricane and tropical-storm winds (subject to any hurricane deductible).
  • Hail. Bruised or fractured shingles and damaged flashing or vents.
  • Falling objects. A tree or limb that punctures the roof deck.
  • Resulting interior damage. Wet drywall, ceiling texture, insulation, flooring, cabinets and paint when the water came through a covered opening.
  • Reasonable emergency repairs. Tarping and temporary patches needed to protect the home from further damage. Keep the receipts.

The rain-entry condition

Many homeowners forms say rain damage to the interior is covered only if wind or hail first made an opening in the roof or walls through which the rain entered. In practice that means a heavy downpour leaking through old, worn flashing may be excluded, while the same downpour leaking through a spot where wind just peeled back shingles is covered. Photos of the exterior opening, taken as soon as it is safe, are some of the most valuable evidence you will ever gather.

The “14 days” and long-term seepage clauses

Policies commonly exclude damage from water that leaks continuously or repeatedly over a period of time. Some forms define that period as 14 days or more; others use wording such as “weeks, months or years.” Some Florida policies also limit coverage for water damage that the homeowner didn’t know about but that had been occurring for a while.

These clauses are why the date you first saw water matters so much. Write it down. If you noticed a small stain after a storm and called the insurer days later, say so clearly. If the insurer claims the leak is old, ask what evidence supports that conclusion – staining patterns, rot and mold growth stages can sometimes be interpreted in more than one way, and moisture mapping or a qualified inspector can help pin down the timeline. Deductibles: hurricane, all-other-perils and roof

Your claim payment is reduced by a deductible, and Florida policies can carry several:

  • Hurricane deductible. Under s. 627.701, Florida Statutes, this is often a percentage of the dwelling (Coverage A) limit, such as 2% or 5%, and applies to losses from a hurricane as Florida law defines that term (s. 627.4025).
  • All-other-perils (AOP) deductible. A flat dollar amount used for non-hurricane losses such as a thunderstorm or hail.
  • Separate roof deductible. Since a 2022 change, s. 627.701(10) lets insurers include a separate roof deductible of up to the lesser of 2% of Coverage A or 50% of the cost to replace the roof. It applies only to claims adjusted on a replacement-cost basis, and it does not apply to a total loss, a hurricane loss, a tree or other hazard that punctures the roof deck, or a roof loss needing repair of less than 50% of the roof. The policyholder must be offered the chance to opt out, and the policy must carry a bold notice about it.

When a roof deductible applies, s. 627.7011(3)(a) lets the insurer limit the roof payment to actual cash value until it receives reasonable proof that you paid the roof deductible.

Does roof age affect coverage?

Age matters in two different ways, and it helps to keep them apart.

Getting or keeping a policy. For policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2022, s. 627.7011(5) says an insurer may not refuse to issue or renew a homeowners policy solely because the roof is less than 15 years old. For roofs 15 years or older, the insurer must let you get an inspection by an authorized inspector at your expense, and it may not refuse coverage solely because of age if the inspection shows at least 5 years of useful life left. This rule is about underwriting – it does not by itself require an insurer to pay any particular claim.

How a claim is paid. Some policies include a roof payment schedule or an actual-cash-value endorsement for older roofs, which reduces what is paid for the roof surface based on its age and material. If you’re not sure whether you’re covered

  1. Get a copy of the full policy, including all endorsements, not just the declarations page.
  2. Note the first date you saw water and any storm in the days before.
  3. Photograph the interior and, safely, the exterior before any repairs.
  4. Protect the home: tarp, dry out, and keep receipts.
  5. Report the claim promptly – Florida’s outer deadline is in s. 627.70132, but the policy requires prompt notice. The Florida roof leak claim filing guide covers the timeline.

FAQ

Is a slow roof leak covered by insurance in Florida?

Often not, if the insurer can show water leaked repeatedly over a period of time, commonly 14 days or more under many policy forms. But “slow” is a conclusion, not a fact. If the leak started with a specific storm and you reported it promptly, the insurer’s timeline can be challenged.

Does homeowners insurance cover a roof leak from heavy rain?

Interior rain damage is usually covered only when wind or hail first created the opening the rain came through. Rain leaking through deteriorated flashing or worn shingles is commonly excluded as wear and tear.

Will a roof leak claim raise my premium in Florida?

Claims history can affect pricing and renewal decisions, and rules vary by insurer. That is a reason to understand your coverage before filing, but not a reason to delay reporting real damage past your policy’s notice requirements.

Is mold from a roof leak covered?

Many Florida policies cover mold only when it results from a covered water loss, and then often only up to a sublimit (for example $10,000). Check your policy’s mold or fungi provision and the declarations page.
