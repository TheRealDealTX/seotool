Hiring a denied claim lawyer in Florida does not mean going straight to court. Most of the work happens first: reading the policy, testing the insurer's reason for the denial, building a record of what caused the damage, and choosing between mediation, appraisal and litigation. This chapter explains that work and the statutes behind it.

What a denial actually decides

When a Florida property insurer denies a claim, it is taking a position: that the loss is not covered, that a policy condition was not met, or that the damage is worth less than your deductible. Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), Florida Statutes, the insurer must pay or deny within 60 days after it receives notice of the claim (unless factors beyond its control prevent it) and must give a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy for a denial or partial denial. If it pays less than its own detailed estimate, it must explain the difference in writing.

That written explanation is the starting point for a lawyer. It tells you which policy provision the insurer is relying on, and it frames the dispute. A denial is not a court ruling and does not end your rights; it is one party's reading of a contract.

Common denial reasons and the questions they raise

Denials tend to fall into a handful of categories. Each one raises a specific legal or factual question that a lawyer will test.

Typical denial reasons in Florida property claims
Insurer's stated reasonThe question to test
Wear and tear, age, deteriorationDid a covered event (wind, hail, a sudden leak) cause the damage you are claiming, even on an older roof or system?
Late noticeWas notice given within the one-year limit in s. 627.70132, and did any delay actually prejudice the insurer's investigation?
Repeated seepage or long-term leakWhat does the policy's exclusion say exactly, and what does the physical evidence show about duration?
Below the deductibleIs the insurer's estimate complete? Does it include all damaged areas, code-required work and matching where the policy provides it?
Flood or surge, not windWhich peril caused which damage? Wind and water damage are often mixed after a hurricane and must be separated.
Failure to cooperate or misrepresentationWhat did the insurer request, when, and was the request reasonable under the policy's post-loss conditions?

The pattern is the same in each row: the denial rests on a factual premise. If an independent inspection, photographs or weather data contradict that premise, the denial is open to challenge.

The first steps a lawyer takes

  1. Collect the documents. The certified policy with every endorsement, the denial or payment letter, the insurer's estimate and photographs, and every letter and email in the claim. Florida's Homeowner Claims Bill of Rights (s. 627.7142) also entitles you to a copy of any detailed estimate within 7 days after the insurer's adjuster generates it.
  2. Calendar the deadlines. The notice limits for new, reopened and supplemental claims in s. 627.70132, and the five-year limit to sue for breach of a property insurance contract in s. 95.11(2)(e), measured from the date of loss. See the Florida claim deadlines guide.
  3. Check the insurer's compliance. Did it acknowledge communications in 7 days, inspect within 30 days of receiving proof-of-loss statements, decide within 60 days, and explain its reasons? These duties come from s. 627.70131, and unfair claim settlement practices are listed in s. 626.9541(1)(i).
  4. Build the causation record. Independent inspections, contractor estimates, engineering opinions where warranted, and storm data. This is the evidence that answers the denial.
  5. Respond to the insurer's requests. Many policies require sworn proofs of loss, recorded statements or examinations under oath. A lawyer prepares you for these and keeps the record clean.

DFS mediation

Florida runs a state mediation program for property claim disputes under s. 627.7015, administered by the Department of Financial Services. It is open to personal lines and commercial residential policies; commercial coverages, auto, and liability coverages are excluded, as are disputes involving suspected fraud, no coverage based on agreed facts, amounts under $500 (unless the parties agree), and losses not reported within the s. 627.70132 limits.

  • The insurer generally pays the full cost of the mediation conference.
  • Mediation is nonbinding. Only a signed written settlement binds the parties.
  • You may rescind a signed settlement within 3 business days, unless you have already cashed or deposited the settlement check.
  • If the insurer failed to tell you about mediation as the statute requires, or the insurer requested mediation and either side rejected the result, you are not required to go through contractual appraisal before suing.

Mediation is often worth trying when the gap is a matter of dollars. A lawyer can attend with you and present the estimate and evidence. The DFS Division of Consumer Services helpline is 1-877-693-5236.

Appraisal, pre-suit notice, or suit

If mediation does not resolve the dispute, the choice is usually between appraisal under the policy and a lawsuit. Appraisal fits disputes over the amount of a covered loss. Litigation fits disputes over whether the loss is covered at all. Before any suit, Florida requires a pre-suit notice under s. 627.70152, given at least 10 business days before filing and only after the insurer has made a coverage determination. The insurer must respond within 10 business days, and in some situations it may demand appraisal or reinspect.

If the insurer's conduct goes beyond a good-faith disagreement, a lawyer will also consider a Civil Remedy Notice under s. 624.155. The bad faith guide explains the timing.

How representation is paid

Property insurance claims are commonly handled on a contingency fee: the lawyer is paid a percentage of what is recovered, and nothing if there is no recovery. Florida Bar Rule 4-1.5 requires a contingency fee agreement to be in writing, signed by the client and the lawyer, and to state how the fee is calculated. {{FIRM_LEGAL}} charges no upfront fees and is paid only if you recover; ask how case costs are handled under the written agreement.

The fee landscape changed in 2022 and 2023, when Florida ended one-way attorney fees in most property insurance suits. That affects how cases are valued and negotiated. The attorney fees guide covers the details.

Choosing a lawyer

You can confirm any Florida lawyer's license and discipline history through The Florida Bar's online member search. Ask how the lawyer will communicate with you, who will handle the day-to-day work, how costs such as inspections and engineering reports are advanced and repaid, and what happens if the case does not resolve. A lawyer should be able to explain the deadlines that apply to your loss in plain terms.

Frequently asked questions

When should I contact a denied claim lawyer in Florida?

As soon as you receive a denial or partial denial you disagree with, and well before the notice and suit deadlines. Early contact makes it easier to preserve evidence and to avoid statements or signatures that narrow your options.

Can a lawyer help if my claim was only partly denied?

Yes. A partial denial or a payment below the cost of repair is a common dispute. Section 627.70131(7)(a) requires the insurer to explain in writing why it paid less than its own detailed estimate.

Does The Lawgical Firm handle commercial property claims?

Yes. The firm handles residential and commercial property insurance claims in Florida. Note that DFS mediation under s. 627.7015 excludes most commercial coverages, so the path for a commercial claim can differ.

Will I have to go to court?

Not necessarily. Many disputes resolve through reconsideration, mediation, appraisal or the pre-suit notice process. Litigation is one option, and the decision is yours.

What does a free case review cost me?

Nothing. The review is free and does not commit you to anything. If the firm takes the case, there are no upfront fees and the firm is paid only if you recover.
