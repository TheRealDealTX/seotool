Denied Claim Law in Florida: what the statutes require of your insurer

Denied claim law is the body of Florida statutes and policy provisions that decides what happens after a property insurer says no: how fast the company had to act, what it had to explain in writing, how long you have to respond, and which doors are still open — mediation, appraisal, a pre-suit notice, a lawsuit, or a bad-faith claim. This reference walks through each rule and cites the statute so you can read it yourself.

The statute shelf

Denied claim law, statute by statute

Florida does not have one “denied claim” statute. The rules are spread across chapters 95, 624, 626 and 627 of the Florida Statutes. These are the provisions that come up in almost every denied homeowner or commercial property claim.

Fla. Stat. § 627.70131

The insurer's claim-handling timeline

Acknowledge in 7 days, inspect within 30 days of proof-of-loss, pay or deny within 60 days of notice.

A denial or partial denial must come with a written, reasonable explanation tied to the policy and the facts. Late payments bear interest from the date the insurer received notice.

Fla. Stat. § 627.70132

Your notice deadline

New or reopened claim: 1 year after the date of loss. Supplemental claim: 18 months.

For a hurricane, the date of loss is the date of landfall. A claim reported too late is barred, so dates matter more than almost anything else.

Fla. Stat. § 627.70152

Pre-suit notice of intent to sue

At least 10 business days before suit, on a DFS form, and only after the insurer's coverage decision.

The insurer then has 10 business days to respond. A suit filed without the notice must be dismissed without prejudice.

Appraisal

A policy clause, not a statute: each side picks an appraiser and an umpire breaks ties on the amount of loss.

Bad faith & the Civil Remedy Notice

60 days' written notice to DFS and the insurer before a statutory bad-faith suit.

If the insurer pays or cures within the 60 days, no action lies. Mere negligence is not bad faith.

Duties and rights

What the law requires of the insurer — and what it lets you do

Read side by side, the statutes describe a two-way process. The insurer has deadlines and explanation duties; the policyholder has notice duties, cooperation duties and a set of remedies when the answer is wrong.

The insurer must

  • Acknowledge claim communications within 7 calendar days (§ 627.70131(1)(a)).
  • Give you its adjuster's name and state license number (§ 627.70131(3)(b)).
  • Send a copy of any detailed estimate within 7 days after its adjuster generates it (§ 627.70131(3)(e)).
  • Pay or deny within 60 days after notice of an initial, reopened or supplemental claim (§ 627.70131(7)(a)).
  • Explain a denial or a payment below its own estimate in writing (§ 627.70131(7)(a)).
  • Not deny claims without a reasonable investigation or misstate policy provisions (§ 626.9541(1)(i)3).
  • Deliver the Homeowner Claims Bill of Rights within 14 days of the first claim communication on a personal residential policy (§ 627.7142).

The policyholder may

  • Request a free DFS mediation conference on most residential claims (§ 627.7015).
  • Invoke appraisal if the policy has an appraisal clause and the dispute is over the amount of loss.
  • Send a pre-suit notice of intent to initiate litigation and then file suit (§ 627.70152).
  • Sue for breach of the policy within 5 years of the date of loss (§ 95.11(2)(e)).
  • File a Civil Remedy Notice to start the statutory bad-faith process (§ 624.155).
  • Ask the DFS Division of Consumer Services for help at 1-877-693-5236.
  • Hire a lawyer on a contingency fee, paid only from a recovery, under a written agreement.

Procedure

The path of a denied claim under Florida law

  1. Day 0

    Loss and notice

    Report the claim to the insurer promptly. The outer limit is one year from the date of loss for a new claim (§ 627.70132).

  2. Days 1–60

    Investigation and decision

    Acknowledgment, inspection, estimate, then a pay-or-deny decision with a written explanation (§ 627.70131).

  3. After a decision

    Review the denial against the policy

    Compare the stated reason to the exact policy language, the estimate and the photographs. Request a certified copy of the policy.

  4. Optional

    Mediation or appraisal

    DFS mediation is free and nonbinding. Appraisal is binding on the amount of loss but usually not on coverage.

  5. 10 business days before suit

    Pre-suit notice

    Filed through DFS; the insurer must accept, keep denying, reinspect, offer or demand appraisal (§ 627.70152).

  6. Within 5 years of loss

    Lawsuit, then bad faith if warranted

    A breach-of-contract suit decides coverage and amount. A statutory bad-faith claim generally follows a favorable result and requires a Civil Remedy Notice (§ 624.155).

Changed in 2022–2023. Florida's December 2022 special session (SB 2-A) and 2023's HB 837 rewrote much of denied claim law. One-way attorney fees for policyholders in property suits are gone for most newer cases, notice deadlines were shortened to 1 year and 18 months, and assignment of benefits is barred for policies issued on or after January 1, 2023 (§ 627.7152(13)). Older losses and older policies can be governed by earlier versions of these laws. How the fee changes work.

Frequently asked questions

What is denied claim law?

It is a shorthand for the Florida statutes, regulations and policy provisions that govern a property insurer's denial and the policyholder's options afterward — chiefly sections 627.70131, 627.70132, 627.70152, 627.7015 and 624.155 of the Florida Statutes, plus the conditions and appraisal clause in your policy.

How long does a Florida insurer have to decide my claim?

Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), generally 60 days after it receives notice of an initial, reopened or supplemental claim, unless factors beyond its control prevent it. After a declared emergency, the Office of Insurance Regulation may extend that by no more than 30 days.

Is a denial letter final?

No. A denial is the insurer's position. You can ask for reconsideration with new evidence, request DFS mediation, invoke appraisal if the dispute is about the amount, or give pre-suit notice and sue within the limitations period.

Can I still get attorney fees paid by the insurer?

For most property insurance suits filed under current law, Florida no longer requires the insurer to pay the policyholder's attorney fees when the policyholder wins. Older claims may differ. See the attorney fees guide for details.
