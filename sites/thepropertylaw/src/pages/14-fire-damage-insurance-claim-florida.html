What a fire damage insurance claim in Florida includes

Fire is one of the few perils nearly every property policy covers, whether open perils or named perils. A fire damage insurance claim in Florida is rarely just about burned materials, though. A complete claim usually has several parts:

  • The structure: burned framing, roofing, finishes and systems, plus damage from smoke, heat, soot and the water and chemicals used to put the fire out, and debris removal.
  • Code upgrades: the cost of bringing repaired areas up to current building codes, under law and ordinance coverage.
  • Other structures: detached garages, fences, sheds and pool enclosures.
  • Personal property: furniture, clothing, electronics and everything else that was damaged or contaminated.
  • Loss of use: additional living expenses while the home is uninhabitable, or lost rent on a rental unit.

Each part has its own limit on the declarations page and its own proof requirements. Commercial fire claims add business income and extra expense, covered in our commercial property claim guide.

First steps after a fire

  1. Get the fire department's incident report number and keep a copy of the report when it is available.
  2. Report the claim to your insurer promptly. The 1-year notice deadline in s. 627.70132 applies, but fire claims benefit from much faster reporting because evidence is cleared quickly.
  3. Secure the property: board openings, tarp the roof, fence the site if needed. Keep receipts.
  4. Photograph and video everything before debris is removed, including inside cabinets and closets.
  5. Do not throw away damaged items until they are documented and, if the insurer wants to inspect them, until it has had the chance.
  6. Ask the insurer in writing about an advance for living expenses if you cannot stay in the home.

Smoke, soot and water damage

Disputes in partial-loss fire claims often center on how far the damage extends. Smoke and soot travel through HVAC ducts, attic spaces and wall cavities well beyond the burned room, and odor can persist in insulation, drywall and textiles. Insurers may propose cleaning and sealing where replacement is needed. An independent assessment by a qualified restoration contractor or industrial hygienist, documenting where soot and odor are present and what the industry cleaning standard requires, is often what resolves the scope. Water used to extinguish the fire is part of the fire loss; prompt drying limits secondary mold growth, which many policies only cover within a small sublimit.

Total losses and the valued policy law (s. 627.702)

Florida is a valued policy law state. Under s. 627.702, when a building, structure, mobile home or manufactured building in Florida is a total loss caused by a covered peril, such as fire, the insurer's liability for the building is the amount of money for which it was insured, assuming no unapproved increase in hazard and no fraud or criminal fault by the insured. In other words, the dwelling limit is owed; the insurer cannot pay less by arguing the home was worth less than the limit.

A few limits apply. The rule covers the building, not contents (except mobile and manufactured homes). It does not apply to partial losses, which are paid at the actual loss up to the limit. If a total loss is caused partly by a covered peril and partly by an excluded one, the insurer is generally liable only for the covered portion, unless the covered peril alone would have caused the total loss. Separately, under s. 627.7011, a total loss of a dwelling on a replacement cost policy is paid without holding back depreciation.

Additional living expenses and contents

Loss of use coverage (often Coverage D) pays the increase in living costs while the home is uninhabitable: rent, hotel, extra mileage, the difference in food costs and similar expenses, typically up to a dollar limit or a time limit. Save receipts and keep a simple ledger comparing normal monthly costs with post-fire costs.

Contents claims require an inventory. List items room by room with description, quantity, age, place of purchase and estimated replacement cost. Bank and credit card statements, online order histories and old photos and videos help prove ownership. If your policy pays replacement cost for contents, it may pay actual cash value first and the balance once items are replaced; read the loss settlement condition.

Cause and origin investigations and EUOs

For significant fires, insurers commonly retain a cause and origin investigator, and may request recorded statements, documents (financial records, phone records, receipts) and an examination under oath. These are permitted when the policy's conditions allow them, and cooperation is a policy duty. Under s. 627.70131(5), a policyholder's fraud, lack of cooperation or intentional misrepresentation can extend the insurer's deadlines.

An investigation does not mean you are suspected of anything; it is routine on large fires. But an EUO is sworn testimony, and answers given there can decide the claim. Many policyholders choose to have a lawyer prepare them for and attend the examination.

Vacancy and other fire-claim exclusions

The fire peril itself is broad, but a few policy provisions come up repeatedly in denials:

  • Vacancy: many policies restrict coverage for certain perils, such as vandalism, after a building has been vacant for a stated period, often 30 or 60 consecutive days. Policies define “vacant” differently from “unoccupied.”
  • Intentional acts: loss intentionally caused by an insured is excluded, though some policies protect an innocent co-insured.
  • Concealment or fraud: misrepresentations in the claim can void coverage.
  • Material change in use: a residence used as a short-term rental or business may raise coverage questions.

Frequently asked questions

Does Florida's valued policy law cover contents after a fire?

No. Section 627.702 applies to buildings and structures (and to mobile and manufactured homes). Personal property is paid under the policy's contents loss settlement terms, up to the contents limit.

How long does the insurer have to pay a fire claim?

For residential policies, s. 627.70131(7) generally requires payment or denial within 60 days after the insurer receives notice, unless factors beyond its control prevent it, such as a policyholder's failure to provide requested information.

Is smoke damage from a neighbor's fire covered?

Usually yes. Most policies cover smoke damage from a hostile fire whether it started on your property or nearby, subject to exclusions such as smoke from agricultural or industrial operations in some forms.

Do I have to give an examination under oath?

If the policy requires it as a condition and the insurer properly requests it, refusing can jeopardize the claim. You can usually have a lawyer present, and you may request a copy of the transcript.

Can I rebuild a different home on the lot?

Many policies allow rebuilding at another location or in a different design, but replacement cost is generally capped at what it would cost to rebuild the original home. Confirm with the insurer in writing before committing.
