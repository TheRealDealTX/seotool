01 · The contract

How property law reads your insurance policy

An insurance policy is a contract, and Florida courts read it like any other contract: the words control, and ambiguous coverage language is generally read in favor of the policyholder. Every homeowners or commercial property policy is built from the same five parts.

Read the full guide to policy parts and exclusions

  1. Declarations

    The summary page: named insureds, property address, policy period, coverage limits (dwelling, other structures, contents, loss of use), and deductibles, including the separate hurricane deductible Florida law allows.

  2. Insuring agreement

    The promise to pay. Most homeowners forms cover the dwelling on an “open perils” basis (everything not excluded) and contents on a “named perils” basis (only what is listed).

  3. Exclusions

    What the policy takes away: flood, earth movement, wear and tear, long-term seepage, mold limits and more. The insurer generally carries the burden of proving an exclusion applies.

  4. Conditions

    Your duties and theirs: prompt notice, protecting the property, documenting the loss, sworn proof of loss, examination under oath, appraisal and suit-against-us clauses.

  5. Endorsements

    Amendments that add, remove or limit coverage, such as sinkhole coverage, roof payment schedules, water damage limits, ordinance or law coverage and Florida special provisions.

02 · The claim

From date of loss to decision

Florida's claim-handling statute, s. 627.70131, puts insurers on a clock once a claim is reported. Here is the usual path for a residential claim.

  1. Day 0

    Loss and mitigation

    Protect the property from further damage, photograph everything and keep receipts for emergency repairs.

  2. Within 1 year

    Notice of claim

    Report the claim under the policy's terms. Supplemental claims have 18 months.

  3. 7 days

    Acknowledgment

    The insurer must acknowledge and send claim forms and instructions.

  4. 30 days

    Inspection

    A physical inspection, if needed, within 30 days after proof-of-loss statements arrive.

  5. 60 days

    Pay or deny

    Payment or denial, in whole or in part, with a written explanation of the policy basis.

If you disagree with the decision, Florida offers several routes: appraisal, DFS mediation, the pre-suit notice and, ultimately, a lawsuit.

04 · Types of loss

Common Florida losses and where property law gets specific

The same policy treats different causes of damage very differently. These are the losses Florida owners report most and the rule that tends to decide each one.

Wind & hurricane

Hurricane deductible applies

A separate hurricane deductible applies once per calendar year (s. 627.701). Notice runs from landfall. Wind-driven rain and flood are treated differently.

Wind claims
Water & plumbing

Sudden vs. long-term

Sudden pipe bursts are usually covered; leaks that continue for weeks are often excluded. Some policies now cap water damage or steer repairs through the insurer's own contractor program.

Water exclusions
Roof

Age and payment schedules

Insurers cannot refuse to renew solely because a roof is under 15 years old (s. 627.7011(5)), but roof endorsements can limit payment to depreciated value.

Roof endorsements
Fire & smoke

Total loss and ALE

A total loss by a covered peril triggers the valued policy law (s. 627.702). Smoke, soot and water used to put out the fire are part of the claim.

Fire damage claims
Sinkhole

Testing and repair rules

Notice within 2 years of discovery, engineer or geologist testing, and payment tied to a repair contract (ss. 627.706–627.7074).

Sinkhole claims
Mold

Sublimits and causation

Mold is usually limited to a small sublimit and covered only when it results from a covered water loss. Fast mitigation matters.

Mold limits
05 · Getting help

What a property insurance lawyer does

Most claims never need a lawyer. When an insurer denies a claim, pays far less than repair estimates, or stops responding, Florida law gives the policyholder tools that are hard to use alone: pre-suit notice, civil remedy notices, appraisal strategy and litigation.

A Florida property insurance lawyer reads the full policy (not just the declarations), compares the insurer's estimate with independent estimates, tracks the statutory deadlines, and handles communications with the insurer and its lawyers. The 2022–2023 reforms changed how attorney fees work in these cases, so ask any lawyer how fees and costs are handled before you sign.

When to call a property insurance lawyer in Florida

Signs a claim needs a second look

  • A denial letter cites wear and tear, pre-existing damage or a late notice
  • The payment is far below a licensed contractor's estimate
  • No pay-or-deny decision 60 days after you reported the claim
  • Repeated requests for documents you already sent
  • A request for an examination under oath or a recorded statement
  • The insurer invoked appraisal and you are unsure how to respond
  • A commercial claim with business interruption or coinsurance issues
06 · Questions

Florida property law FAQ

Short answers. Each links to the guide with the details and statute citations.

What is property law, and how does it apply to insurance claims?

The term broadly covers ownership and use of real property. For an insurance claim, the relevant part is Florida property insurance law: the policy contract plus the statutes in Chapter 627 (claim handling, notice, mediation, pre-suit notice, sinkholes) and Chapter 624 (bad faith) that control how insurers must handle and pay claims.

How long do I have to report a property damage claim in Florida?

Under the current version of s. 627.70132, notice of a new or reopened claim must be given within 1 year after the date of loss, and a supplemental claim within 18 months. Sinkhole claims follow a separate 2-year rule in s. 627.706(5). Earlier versions of the statute allowed more time, so the rule for an older loss may differ.

How long does a Florida insurer have to pay or deny my claim?

For residential claims and certain commercial claims on buildings of 10,000 square feet or less, s. 627.70131(7) generally requires payment or denial within 60 days after the insurer receives notice, unless factors beyond its control prevent it. Late payments generally carry interest.

Do I have to go to appraisal or mediation before suing?

Not always. DFS mediation is optional for the policyholder, and appraisal depends on your policy's clause and whether a party invokes it. What is required before suing on any residential or commercial property policy is the written notice of intent to initiate litigation under s. 627.70152, filed at least 10 business days before suit.

Can I still recover attorney fees from my insurer?

Florida's one-way attorney fee statute for property insurance suits was eliminated in the 2022 special session and the 2023 tort reform law. For current policies, each side generally pays its own fees unless another rule, such as an offer of judgment, applies. Ask the lawyer you consult to explain the fee agreement in writing.
