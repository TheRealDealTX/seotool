How a sinkhole claim in Florida differs

A sinkhole claim in Florida is not handled like a roof or water claim. Sections 627.706 through 627.7074 of the Florida Statutes set their own coverage definitions, a separate notice deadline, mandatory testing procedures, payment rules tied to a repair contract, and a neutral evaluation process run through the Department of Financial Services. Central Florida, where limestone geology makes subsidence common, sees the largest share of these claims, but they arise statewide.

The first question is always which coverage you have, because Florida recognizes two very different kinds.

Two coverages: collapse vs. sinkhole loss

Required?Yes. Every insurer writing property insurance in Florida must provide it (s. 627.706(1)(a))No. Insurers must make it available for an additional premium and may inspect first (s. 627.706(1)(b))

Insurers may limit both coverages to the principal building. Contents and additional living expenses under sinkhole coverage apply only if there is structural damage to the covered building. Residential policies with sinkhole coverage may carry a sinkhole deductible of 1%, 2%, 5% or 10% of the dwelling limit, with premium discounts for higher deductibles.

What counts as “structural damage”

Section 627.706(2)(k) defines structural damage narrowly. It includes interior floor displacement or deflection beyond acceptable building code tolerances, foundation displacement that compromises primary structural members, leaning or buckling of load-bearing walls beyond code tolerances, damage that makes imminent collapse significantly likely, and “substantial structural damage” as the Florida Building Code defines it. Cosmetic cracks alone usually do not qualify, which is why engineering evidence decides most of these cases.

The 2-year notice rule

Sinkhole claims do not use the 1-year rule that governs most claims. Under s. 627.706(5), any claim, including initial, supplemental and reopened claims, is barred unless notice is given under the policy terms within 2 years after the policyholder knew or reasonably should have known about the sinkhole loss. Because the clock runs from when you knew or should have known, keep dated photos of cracks and doors that begin to stick, and report early rather than waiting to see whether things get worse.

Inspection and testing (s. 627.707)

When you report a possible sinkhole loss, the statute sets out a sequence:

  1. Inspection. The insurer must inspect the premises to determine whether there is structural damage that may be the result of sinkhole activity.
  2. Written notice. After the initial inspection, the insurer must notify you in writing of what it found and of your testing rights.
  3. Testing. If structural damage is confirmed but the cause is not, or the damage is consistent with sinkhole loss, and the policy covers sinkhole loss, the insurer must have a professional engineer or professional geologist test and analyze the site. The insurer pays those fees.
  4. Denial without testing. If the insurer denies without testing, you may demand testing in writing within 60 days after receiving the denial. You pay 50% of the testing cost, up to $2,500, and the insurer reimburses that share if its engineer or geologist confirms a sinkhole loss.

The resulting report addresses whether sinkhole activity caused structural damage and, if so, the recommended methods to stabilize the land and building and repair the foundation.

Payment and the repair contract

If a sinkhole loss is verified, the insurer must pay to stabilize the land and building and repair the foundation in line with the engineer's or geologist's recommendations. The money is tied to the work:

  • Until you enter a contract for subsurface stabilization and foundation repairs, the insurer may limit payment to actual cash value, excluding underpinning, grouting and other below-foundation repairs.
  • You must enter that repair contract within 90 days after the insurer confirms coverage and notifies you; the period is tolled during neutral evaluation.
  • Once the contract is signed, the insurer pays as the work is performed and may not require you to advance payment for the repairs.
  • Stabilization and repairs to the structure and contents should generally be completed within 12 months after the contract is signed, unless the parties agree otherwise or the claim is in neutral evaluation, appraisal, mediation or litigation.
  • Accepting a rebate from a repair contractor voids the coverage, and offering one is a felony.

Neutral evaluation (s. 627.7074)

Disputes over whether a sinkhole caused the damage, how to repair it and what it should cost can go to neutral evaluation. Either party may request it once a sinkhole report has been issued; it is nonbinding, but mandatory if either side requests it. The insurer pays the evaluator's reasonable costs. The evaluator must address causation, all methods of stabilization and repair above and below ground, and their costs, and should make reasonable efforts to hold the conference within 90 days after DFS receives the request, with a written report due within 14 days after the conference. Filing a request tolls the time to sue for 60 days after the process ends or the s. 95.11 period, whichever is later, and the evaluator's recommendation is admissible in later litigation.

Practical points for owners

  • Check the declarations for “sinkhole loss” coverage before assuming you have it.
  • Date-stamp photos of cracks, sloping floors, sticking doors and separating trim as they appear.
  • Get the insurer's engineering report and the raw testing data, not just the summary letter.
  • Calendar the 60-day testing demand window after any denial and the 90-day repair contract window after coverage is confirmed.
  • Have your own engineer review recommendations that rely only on surface (chemical grout) repairs where deeper stabilization may be needed.

Frequently asked questions

Does my Florida homeowners policy cover sinkholes?

Every policy must cover catastrophic ground cover collapse, which has strict requirements including government condemnation of the building. Broader sinkhole loss coverage is optional and must be purchased for an additional premium, so check your declarations and endorsements.

How long do I have to report a sinkhole claim?

Under s. 627.706(5), within 2 years after you knew or reasonably should have known about the sinkhole loss. This applies to initial, supplemental and reopened sinkhole claims.

Who pays for sinkhole testing?

When testing is required under s. 627.707(2), the insurer pays. If the insurer denies without testing and you demand testing within 60 days, you pay half of the cost up to $2,500, refundable if a sinkhole loss is confirmed.

Can I keep the sinkhole payment and not repair?

The statute ties full payment to a stabilization and foundation repair contract. Before that contract, payment can be limited to actual cash value excluding below-foundation work, and the contract must be signed within 90 days after coverage is confirmed.

Is neutral evaluation the same as DFS mediation?

No. Neutral evaluation under s. 627.7074 is specific to sinkhole disputes and focuses on cause and repair method, with a qualified evaluator issuing a written recommendation. DFS mediation under s. 627.7015 is a general settlement conference for residential claims.
