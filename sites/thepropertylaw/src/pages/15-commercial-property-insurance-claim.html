How a commercial property insurance claim differs

A commercial property insurance claim is made under a policy covering a business or income-producing building: an office, a retail center, a restaurant, a hotel, a medical facility, a house of worship, an industrial site, or a condominium or homeowners association's common property. The causes of loss are familiar (hurricane and windstorm, water, roof leaks, fire, plumbing failures, mold, sinkholes, theft and vandalism), but commercial policies are usually built from different forms, carry higher limits and deductibles, and include coverage for lost income that a homeowners policy does not.

Florida's claim statutes also treat commercial policies differently in a few places. Knowing which rules apply to your building is the first step.

Larger commercial claims fall outside the 60-day pay-or-deny statute, so the timeline is governed largely by the policy and the insurer's general duties. That makes a written claim schedule and regular written follow-up even more important.

Business interruption and extra expense

Business income coverage pays the net income the business would have earned plus continuing normal operating expenses (including payroll, if covered) during the period of restoration: generally from a short waiting period after the physical loss until the property should be repaired, rebuilt or replaced with reasonable speed. Extra expense coverage pays the added costs of staying open or reopening sooner, such as a temporary location or expedited equipment.

These claims are accounting-driven. Expect the insurer to request profit and loss statements, tax returns, sales records and payroll for periods before and after the loss. Common disputes involve the length of the restoration period, whether income would have dropped anyway (for example, after a storm reduced customer traffic area-wide), and how to project future earnings. Many policies also include extended business income for a period after reopening and civil authority coverage when access is prohibited by an order following nearby damage. {{FIRM}} works with forensic accountants to document and present business interruption claims.

Coinsurance, deductibles and valuation

Most commercial property forms include a coinsurance clause. If the building is insured for less than a stated percentage of its value at the time of loss (commonly 80% or 90%), the payment on a partial loss is reduced proportionally. For example, with a 90% coinsurance clause on a building valued at $2,000,000, the required limit is $1,800,000; if the limit is $1,200,000, the insurer pays two-thirds of the loss (less the deductible). Agreed value endorsements can suspend coinsurance.

Commercial windstorm and named storm deductibles are typically a percentage of the insured value of each building, which on a large property can be substantial. Valuation (replacement cost vs. actual cash value), ordinance or law coverage, and debris removal limits are also set by the form and endorsements, so a complete certified copy of the policy is essential.

Condominium and HOA claims

Association policies cover common elements and, depending on the declaration and Florida's condominium statutes, parts of the units. Two points are particular to associations:

  • Association policies are commercial residential, so DFS mediation under s. 627.7015 is available.
  • Unit owners may have loss assessment coverage on their own policies. Under s. 627.70132(4), notice of a loss assessment claim must be given within the later of 1 year after the date of loss or 90 days after the association votes to levy the assessment, but no later than 3 years after the date of loss.

Documenting a commercial loss

  • Building plans, prior inspection and maintenance records, and roof reports, to rebut wear-and-tear and pre-existing damage arguments.
  • Photos and video of each building, unit and system, with a site map.
  • Mitigation contracts, invoices and moisture readings.
  • Equipment and inventory lists with values.
  • Financial records for the business income claim, organized by month.
  • A log of all communications with the insurer, adjusters and consultants.

Resolving commercial claim disputes

Commercial policies almost always contain an appraisal clause, and appraisal is often used for disputes over the amount of loss on large buildings. Because DFS mediation is not available for most commercial coverages, the main paths are negotiation with supporting engineering and accounting reports, appraisal, and litigation after the pre-suit notice under s. 627.70152. The insurer must respond to that notice within 10 business days with a settlement offer or by requiring appraisal or another alternative dispute resolution method.

Frequently asked questions

Does the 60-day pay-or-deny rule apply to my commercial building?

Only if the insured structure is 10,000 square feet or less (or the claim is for contents under a commercial tenant policy for premises of that size), and not for nonresidential commercial policies covering structures in more than one state.

Can my business use DFS mediation?

Only if the policy is commercial residential, such as a condominium or homeowners association policy. Commercial coverages for other businesses are excluded from the program.

What is the period of restoration?

The time business income is paid for. It generally begins after a short waiting period following the physical loss and ends when the property should have been repaired or replaced with reasonable speed, not necessarily when the work actually finishes.

How does coinsurance reduce a claim?

If the limit is below the required percentage of the building's value, the insurer pays only the proportion of the loss that the actual limit bears to the required limit, minus the deductible.

Can a contractor take an assignment of my commercial claim?

Not for commercial property policies issued on or after January 1, 2023. Section 627.7152(13) makes such post-loss assignments void.
