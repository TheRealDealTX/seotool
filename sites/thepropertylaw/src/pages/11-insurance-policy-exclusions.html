Why exclusions decide so many claims

With insurance policy exclusions explained properly, most denial letters become easier to read. Florida homeowners policies usually cover the dwelling against “risks of direct physical loss” and then carve out what they will not pay for. That means a claim is often won or lost not on whether damage happened, but on whether a specific exclusion, limitation or endorsement applies to it. To answer that question you have to read the policy in the order it was built, from the declarations to the endorsements, because later parts change earlier ones.

Under Florida law, coverage language that is genuinely ambiguous is generally read in favor of the policyholder, exclusions are read more narrowly than coverage grants, and the insurer generally has the burden of proving that an exclusion applies. Once it does, the burden can shift back to the policyholder to show an exception to the exclusion. Those principles do not rescue every claim, but they explain why the exact wording matters.

Part 1: the declarations page

The declarations (“dec page”) is the summary at the front. It lists:

  • Named insureds, the property address and the policy period. A loss outside the period, or at an address not listed, is not covered.
  • Coverage limits, typically Coverage A (dwelling), B (other structures), C (personal property) and D (loss of use) on a homeowners form.
  • Deductibles, usually an all-other-perils deductible and a separate hurricane deductible. Under s. 627.701, insurers must offer hurricane deductible options of $500, 2%, 5% and 10% of the dwelling limit (with some exceptions for higher-value homes), and the hurricane deductible applies once per calendar year to all covered hurricane losses.
  • Every form and endorsement number that makes up the policy. This list is how you confirm you have the complete policy.
Get the whole policy. The dec page alone is not the policy. Ask the insurer or agent for a certified copy including every listed form; the endorsements often contain the language that limits a claim.

Part 2: the insuring agreement

The insuring agreement is the promise. Two structures dominate:

  • Open perils (all risks). Covers direct physical loss unless excluded. Most Florida HO-3 style policies use this for the dwelling and other structures. The policyholder shows a loss occurred during the policy period; the insurer must then prove an exclusion.
  • Named perils. Covers only causes listed, such as fire, windstorm, theft or sudden discharge of water. Personal property under most homeowners forms, and many older or lower-cost policies, work this way. Here the policyholder must show the loss was caused by a listed peril.

The insuring agreement also defines key words like “sudden and accidental,” “collapse” and “residence premises.” Definitions are part of the coverage grant, so read the definitions section before the exclusions.

Part 3: the exclusions section

Exclusions come in two kinds. Some remove coverage for a cause of loss no matter what else contributed; these usually start with “anti-concurrent causation” wording such as “regardless of any other cause or event contributing concurrently or in any sequence.” Others exclude a type of damage but restore coverage for “ensuing loss”: for example, the cost to fix a worn-out pipe is excluded, but the water damage it causes to floors and drywall may be covered.

When an exclusion appears in a denial letter, ask three questions: Does the exclusion's wording actually describe what happened? Is there an exception or ensuing-loss clause that gives coverage back? Has an endorsement replaced the standard exclusion with different language?

Part 4: conditions

Conditions are the rules of the claim. Common ones in Florida policies:

  • Duties after loss: prompt notice, protecting the property from further damage, keeping repair receipts, preparing an inventory, showing the damaged property, providing records and submitting to an examination under oath when requested.
  • Proof of loss: a signed, sworn statement of the amount claimed, typically due within a set number of days after the insurer requests it.
  • Appraisal: the clause that lets either side send an amount-of-loss dispute to appraisers and an umpire.
  • Loss payment: when payment is due and how mortgage companies are named on checks.
  • Suit against us: the policy's requirements before suing, which must be read alongside Florida statutes such as s. 627.70152.

Failing a condition can reduce or defeat a claim, but Florida courts often look at whether the failure actually prejudiced the insurer. Do not count on that; comply, and keep copies of everything you send.

Part 5: endorsements

Endorsements amend the base form and are listed on the declarations. In Florida they commonly:

  • Add sinkhole loss coverage, which s. 627.706 requires insurers to offer for an additional premium; catastrophic ground cover collapse coverage is included regardless.
  • Change roof payment. Some policies pay actual cash value or a schedule based on roof age. Separately, s. 627.7011(5) bars refusing to issue or renew a homeowners policy solely because a roof is less than 15 years old.
  • Limit water damage, for example by capping non-weather water claims or offering a managed repair program.
  • Set law and ordinance coverage. Under s. 627.7011, Florida homeowners policies include 25% of the dwelling limit for code-upgrade costs unless the policyholder rejects it in writing; 50% may be offered.
  • Add Florida special provisions that conform the national form to Florida statutes.

Frequently asked questions

Who has to prove an exclusion applies?

Generally the insurer. Under an open perils policy, once you show a loss during the policy period, the insurer must prove that an exclusion applies. If it does, you may need to show that an exception, such as an ensuing-loss clause, restores coverage.

Is flood damage excluded from my homeowners policy?

Almost always. Standard Florida homeowners policies exclude flood and storm surge. Flood is insured through a separate policy, such as one from the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood insurer. Wind damage from the same storm is still claimed under the homeowners policy.

What does anti-concurrent causation mean?

It is language that excludes a loss caused by an excluded peril even if a covered peril also contributed. Whether it applies depends on the precise wording and the facts, so a denial based on it deserves careful review.

Can an endorsement reduce coverage without my knowing?

Endorsements are part of the policy listed on the declarations, and insurers must give required notices when coverage is reduced at renewal. Review each renewal packet and request the full policy if anything is unclear.

Is mold covered in Florida?

Usually only in limited form. Many policies exclude mold except when it results from a covered loss, and then cap remediation with a sublimit. Check your declarations and endorsements for the amount.
