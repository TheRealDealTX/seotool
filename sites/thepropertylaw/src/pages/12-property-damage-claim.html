Starting a property damage claim

A property damage claim is a request for payment under your own policy after physical damage to your home or building. In Florida the clock starts on the date of loss: under s. 627.70132 a new claim must be reported within 1 year (18 months for a supplemental claim), and the insurer's handling deadlines under s. 627.70131 begin when it receives notice. The steps below follow the order things usually happen and point out where claims tend to go wrong.

  1. Make the property safe. Shut off water or power if needed and get people out of unsafe areas.
  2. Document before you clean up. Photograph and video every room and the exterior, including close-ups and wide shots, before anything is moved or removed.
  3. Report the claim. Use the insurer's claim line or portal, write down the claim number, and follow up in writing.
  4. Mitigate. Make reasonable temporary repairs (tarping, water extraction, boarding windows) and keep receipts.
  5. Keep a claim file. Every letter, email, estimate, photo and receipt in one place, with a log of calls: date, name, what was said.

Your duties after a loss

Every Florida property policy has a “duties after loss” condition. The exact list varies, but expect to be required to:

  • Give prompt notice to the insurer (and to police for theft or vandalism).
  • Protect the property from further damage and keep records of what that cost.
  • Prepare an inventory of damaged personal property with quantities, descriptions, values and any receipts or photos.
  • Show the damaged property as often as reasonably required, and allow inspection of undamaged areas the insurer reasonably needs to see.
  • Provide records and documents the insurer requests, and permit copies.
  • Submit to an examination under oath, outside the presence of other insureds, if the policy permits and the insurer asks.
  • Send a signed, sworn proof of loss within the time the policy sets after the insurer requests it.
Missing documents toll the clock. Under s. 627.70131(8), if you do not provide requested material information within 10 days, the insurer's deadlines can be tolled until it arrives. Respond promptly and in writing.

The inspection and the estimate

The insurer will usually send an adjuster, whether a staff adjuster, an independent adjuster, or a desk adjuster using photos. Florida law requires the insurer to begin its investigation within 7 days after receiving proof-of-loss statements and, when a physical inspection is needed, to complete it within 30 days. The adjuster must give you their name and license number.

Be present for the inspection if you can, walk the adjuster through every damaged area, and point out damage that is easy to miss: ceiling stains in closets, lifted shingles, soft drywall, cracked tiles, damaged fences and outbuildings. If the adjuster prepares a detailed estimate, the insurer must send you a copy within 7 days after it is generated. Compare it line by line with an estimate from a licensed contractor; differences in quantities, pricing and missing items are where most underpayments come from.

How the insurer calculates payment

Three concepts drive the number on the check:

ConceptMeaningFlorida rule
Replacement cost (RCV)Cost to repair or replace with like kind and quality, with no deduction for depreciationUnder s. 627.7011(3), the insurer must initially pay at least actual cash value less the deductible, then pay the remaining replacement cost as work is performed
Actual cash value (ACV)Replacement cost minus depreciationRoof endorsements may limit roof payments to ACV; on a dwelling total loss, replacement cost is paid without holdback of depreciation
Law and ordinanceExtra cost to meet current building codes25% of the dwelling limit is included unless rejected in writing; 50% may be offered

Florida also has a valued policy law, s. 627.702: when a covered peril causes a total loss of an insured building, the insurer owes the amount the building was insured for. When a total loss is caused partly by a covered peril and partly by an excluded one (wind and flood, for example), the insurer generally owes only for the damage the covered peril caused, unless the covered peril alone would have caused the total loss.

Deductibles, including the hurricane deductible

Most Florida policies have two deductibles: an all-other-perils deductible stated in dollars, and a hurricane deductible usually stated as a percentage of the dwelling limit. Under s. 627.701, the hurricane deductible applies on an annual basis to all covered hurricane losses in a calendar year. If a second hurricane damages the property in the same year, the deductible for that loss is generally the greater of the remaining hurricane deductible or the all-other-perils deductible. A 2% deductible on a $400,000 dwelling limit is $8,000, so the deductible alone can exceed a small roof or fence repair.

Appraisal: resolving a dispute over the amount

If you and the insurer agree the loss is covered but disagree about the amount, the policy's appraisal clause offers a faster route than litigation. The usual steps:

  1. Either party makes a written demand for appraisal.
  2. Each side names a competent, independent appraiser within the time the policy states (often 20 days).
  3. The two appraisers select an umpire; if they cannot agree, either may ask a court to appoint one.
  4. The appraisers set the amount of loss. Items they disagree on go to the umpire. A written award agreed to by any two of the three is binding on the amount.

Each side typically pays its own appraiser and shares the umpire's fee, which is worth weighing against the size of the dispute. Appraisal generally does not resolve whether a loss is covered, and an appraisal award is not a court finding of breach for purposes of a bad faith claim under s. 624.1551. For residential claims, a civil remedy notice cannot be filed within 60 days after appraisal is invoked.

If the claim is denied or underpaid

Read the denial or payment letter carefully. Section 627.70131(7) requires the insurer to explain in writing the policy basis for a denial or partial payment. Then:

  • Request a certified copy of the full policy and the adjuster's estimate and photos.
  • Match the cited exclusion or condition to the actual policy wording (see insurance policy exclusions explained).
  • Get an independent estimate and, where cause is disputed, an engineer or other qualified inspector.
  • Consider DFS mediation for residential claims, which the insurer pays for.
  • Note your deadlines: supplemental claim notice within 18 months, and the pre-suit notice before any lawsuit.
  • Talk with a Florida property insurance lawyer before signing a release or cashing a check marked “final.”

Frequently asked questions

Should I make repairs before the adjuster comes?

Make temporary repairs needed to prevent further damage, and keep receipts and photos. Hold off on permanent repairs or tear-out until the damage is documented and the insurer has had a reasonable chance to inspect, unless waiting would cause more damage.

What is a supplemental claim?

A claim for additional loss from the same peril that the insurer already adjusted, or for costs discovered while completing repairs on a timely claim. Under s. 627.70132 it must be reported within 18 months after the date of loss.

Can I hire a contractor and assign my claim to them?

Not under residential or commercial property policies issued on or after January 1, 2023. Section 627.7152(13) makes such assignments of post-loss benefits void. You can still hire contractors and pay them from claim proceeds.

Why did my check include my mortgage company?

Most policies list the lender as a mortgagee and require it to be named on payments for the dwelling. The lender may hold funds and release them as repairs progress.

What if the insurer has not decided my claim after 60 days?

For most residential claims the insurer must pay or deny within 60 days after notice, absent factors beyond its control or tolling. Ask in writing for a decision, and note that late payments generally carry interest. A lawyer can advise on next steps.
