What Florida property insurance law covers

Florida property insurance law is the combination of your policy and the statutes the Legislature has added to it. The policy sets out what is covered and what you must do after a loss. The statutes, mostly in Chapter 627 of the Florida Statutes with the bad faith remedy in Chapter 624, add deadlines and duties the policy cannot take away: how quickly the insurer must acknowledge and decide a claim, how long you have to report damage, what alternative dispute options exist, and what notice must be given before anyone files suit.

These rules apply to homeowners, condo unit owner and dwelling policies, and many also apply to commercial property policies. Florida rewrote large parts of this law in 2021, again in a December 2022 special session (Senate Bill 2-A), and in the 2023 tort reform law (House Bill 837). That history matters: the rule that applies to your claim can depend on when your policy was issued and when the loss happened.

Notice of claim deadlines (s. 627.70132)

The single most common way a valid claim is lost is late notice. Section 627.70132 says a claim or reopened claim is barred unless notice is given to the insurer under the policy's terms within 1 year after the date of loss. A supplemental claim is barred unless notice is given within 18 months after the date of loss.

For hurricanes, windstorms, tornadoes, severe rain and other weather events, the statute defines the date of loss as the date the hurricane made landfall or the date the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration verifies the weather event. The time limits are tolled for a named insured servicemember deployed to a combat zone or combat support posting.

These windows are much shorter than they used to be. Before the 2022 changes, the 2021 version of the statute allowed 2 years for initial and reopened claims and 3 years for supplemental claims, and earlier versions differed again. If your loss or policy predates the current text, the deadline that applies may be different, and courts have addressed how the amendments apply to older policies. Report early regardless.

The insurer's claim-handling timeline (s. 627.70131)

Once you report a claim, s. 627.70131 sets this schedule:

  • 7 calendar days to review and acknowledge receipt of a communication about the claim, unless payment is made within that time. If the communication is a claim notice, the acknowledgment must include the necessary claim forms, instructions and a phone number.
  • 7 days after receiving proof-of-loss statements to begin whatever investigation is reasonably necessary.
  • 30 days after receiving proof-of-loss statements to physically inspect the property, if an inspection is needed. The adjuster must give you their name and state license number, and later communications must include them.
  • 7 days after an adjuster generates a detailed estimate to send you a copy of it.
  • 60 days after receiving notice of an initial, reopened or supplemental claim to pay or deny it, in whole or in part, unless factors beyond the insurer's control prevent it.

The pay-or-deny rule comes with a written explanation requirement: the insurer must give you a reasonable explanation, in writing, of the policy basis for its decision, and if it pays less than the amount in its own detailed estimate it must explain the difference. A payment made after the 60-day window generally bears interest at the statutory judgment rate from the date the insurer received notice.

Some limits are worth knowing. The 60-day rule applies to residential policies and to commercial policies on structures of 10,000 square feet or less (and commercial tenant contents coverage for premises of that size). The timelines are tolled during DFS mediation or another alternative dispute process, and can be tolled if you fail to provide requested material information within 10 days. After a declared state of emergency the Office of Insurance Regulation can extend the deadlines, but not the pay-or-deny period by more than 30 days. A missed deadline is a violation of the insurance code, but the statute says it is not, by itself, a basis for a private lawsuit; its practical value is as evidence of how the claim was handled.

DFS mediation (s. 627.7015)

The Florida Department of Financial Services runs a mediation program for property claim disputes. The key rules:

  • Eligible policies: personal lines residential and commercial residential policies (for example, a condominium association's building policy). Commercial coverages, auto, and liability disputes are not eligible.
  • Cost: the insurer pays for the mediation conference. If you miss a scheduled conference without good cause you may have to pay to reschedule.
  • Nonbinding: neither side has to settle. If you do sign a settlement, you have 3 business days to rescind it, unless you have already cashed or deposited the settlement check.
  • Exclusions: disputes where the insurer has a reasonable basis to suspect fraud, where there is no coverage based on undisputed facts about the cause of loss, where the amount in controversy is under $500 (unless the parties agree), or where notice was not timely under s. 627.70132.
  • Insurer's notice duty: the insurer must tell you about mediation when you file a covered first-party claim. If it fails to, or if the insurer requested mediation and either side rejected the result, you are not required to go through the policy's appraisal process before suing for breach of contract.

Appraisal under Florida law

Appraisal is not a statute; it is a clause in most Florida property policies. When the parties disagree on the amount of loss, either side can demand appraisal. Each picks an appraiser, the two appraisers pick an umpire, and an award signed by any two of the three sets the amount. Florida courts generally treat coverage questions as matters for a judge, while appraisers decide the amount of loss; when the insurer has admitted coverage, appraisers may also resolve how much damage the covered peril caused. Paying an appraisal award does not count as a court ruling that the insurer breached the policy. See the step-by-step in our property damage claim guide.

Pre-suit notice of intent to litigate (s. 627.70152)

Since 2021, a lawsuit on any residential or commercial property insurance policy, including surplus lines policies, must be preceded by a written notice of intent to initiate litigation. Under the 2026 text:

  1. The notice is filed with the Department of Financial Services on its form and copied to the insurer, at least 10 business days before filing suit, and not before the insurer has made its coverage determination under s. 627.70131.
  2. It must state the alleged acts or omissions. After a denial of coverage it includes an estimate of damages if known; for other disputes (such as underpayment) it includes a presuit settlement demand itemizing the damages and the disputed amount.
  3. The insurer must respond in writing within 10 business days. After a denial it may accept coverage, continue to deny, or assert a right to reinspect (with 14 more business days to decide). For other disputes it must make a settlement offer or require appraisal or another alternative dispute resolution.
  4. If appraisal or other ADR the insurer requires is not concluded within 90 days after the 10-business-day period, you may sue without another notice.

A suit filed without the notice, or too early, must be dismissed without prejudice. The statute also tolls the limitations period in several situations so the notice process does not cost you the right to sue.

Bad faith and the civil remedy notice (ss. 624.155, 624.1551)

Section 624.155 lets a person damaged by certain insurer conduct sue for extra-contractual damages, including an insurer's failure to settle a claim in good faith when, under all the circumstances, it could and should have done so had it acted fairly and honestly toward its insured. Before suing, the claimant must file a civil remedy notice with DFS on its form, stating with specificity the statute violated, the facts, the people involved and the relevant policy language. The insurer then has 60 days to pay the damages or correct the violation; if it does, no bad faith action lies.

Three later changes matter for property claims:

  • Breach first. Section 624.1551, added in 2022, says a bad faith claim against a property insurer requires first establishing, through a court ruling, that the insurer breached the policy, and a final judgment. Paying an appraisal award or accepting an offer of judgment does not count.
  • Appraisal timing. For a residential property claim, a civil remedy notice may not be filed within 60 days after either party invokes appraisal.
  • Negligence is not bad faith. Since the 2023 amendments, mere negligence alone is not enough, and the claimant's own good faith in providing information and making demands can be weighed.

How long you have to sue (s. 95.11)

Notice deadlines and suit deadlines are different. Under the 2026 text of s. 95.11(2)(e), an action for breach of a property insurance contract falls in the five-year category, with the period running from the date of loss. In practice the 1-year notice deadline usually controls far sooner, and the pre-suit and civil remedy notice steps add their own timing. Treat the notice deadline as the real clock.

Frequently asked questions

Does Florida property insurance law apply to surplus lines policies?

Many provisions do. Section 627.70152 expressly covers suits under residential or commercial property policies issued by eligible surplus lines insurers, and s. 627.70131(9) applies its claim-handling rules to surplus lines insurers writing residential property. Check each statute's scope language.

What if my insurer misses the 60-day deadline?

Late payment generally accrues interest from the date the insurer received notice. The statute says a violation alone does not create a private cause of action, but a missed deadline can support a complaint to DFS and can be relevant evidence in a later dispute.

Is DFS mediation available for my business property?

Only if the policy is personal lines residential or commercial residential, such as a condominium or homeowners association policy. Commercial coverages for offices, restaurants or warehouses are excluded from the DFS program under s. 627.7015(1).

Which version of the law applies to my claim?

It depends on the provision. Some changes apply to policies issued or renewed after a date; others to losses or suits after a date. Bring your declarations page, the policy period and the date of loss to any consultation so the right version can be identified.
