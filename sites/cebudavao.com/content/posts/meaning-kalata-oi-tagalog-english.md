The **oi meaning** is simple: *oi* (also spelled *oy* or *uy*) is an interjection that works like "hey" in English. Filipinos use it to call someone's attention, to show surprise, or to tease a friend, and the exact feeling depends on your tone of voice. Below we unpack how *oi* works in Bisaya and Tagalog, then cover the colourful local words for being annoyed or pissed off.

## Oi Meaning in English: The Short Answer

In English, *oi* is closest to "hey!", "hoy!" or "look!". The British also use "oi" to get someone's attention, usually in a slightly rough way, so if you hear it in a London pub and in a Cebu jeepney, the core meaning is surprisingly similar.

In the Philippines the word is softer and far more flexible. A single *uy* can mean "hi there", "wait a minute", "be careful", "I can't believe it" or "stop that", all depending on how it is said.

| Spelling | Where you hear it | Closest English meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *Oi / Oy* | Cebu, Davao, all of Visayas and Mindanao | Hey! Look! |
| *Uy* | Nationwide, very common in Tagalog areas too | Hey! Oh! Wow! |
| *Hoy* | Nationwide | Hey you! (stronger, can sound rude) |
| *Uy, uy, uy* | Teasing among friends | Ooh, what's this? |

## What Is the Meaning of Oi in Bisaya?

In Cebuano Bisaya, *oi* is part of everyday speech. You will hear it at the *sari-sari* store, in offices in Cebu IT Park and on the streets of Davao. Here are the most common uses.

### 1. Calling attention

- *Oi, Dodong, diri ra ko!* — "Hey, Dodong, I'm over here!"
- *Oi, naa kay bisita.* — "Hey, you have a visitor."

### 2. Surprise or disbelief

- *Oi, nindot kaayo!* — "Oh wow, that's so beautiful!"
- *Uy, ikaw diay na?* — "Oh, it's you?"

### 3. Gentle warning

- *Oi, hinay-hinay lang.* — "Hey, slow down." (literally "slowly, slowly")
- *Oi, ayaw!* — "Hey, don't!"

### 4. Teasing

When a friend shows up looking fresh in a new shirt, someone will almost certainly say *"Uy, gwapo!"* with a grin. If you want more compliments like that, our guide to [what gwapo means](/culture/gwapo/) covers the whole family of words.

## Oi in Tagalog

Tagalog speakers prefer *uy* and *hoy*. *Uy* is friendly and light, while *hoy* is sharper. Shouting *"Hoy!"* at a stranger can sound rude, so travellers should stick with *uy* or a polite *"Excuse me po."*

- *Uy, kumusta ka na?* — "Hey, how are you?"
- *Hoy, bayaran mo ako!* — "Hey, pay me back!" (not very polite)

The particle *po* adds respect when speaking to elders, so *"Uy, salamat po"* sounds warm and courteous.

## What Is "Pissed Off" in Tagalog?

People often search for the Tagalog of pissed off, and there is no single word. The best choice depends on how angry the person is.

| English | Tagalog | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Annoyed, irritated | *Naiinis* / *inis* | The most common, mild to medium |
| Pissed off | *Naaasar* / *asar* | Informal, someone is getting on your nerves |
| In a bad mood | *Badtrip* | Taglish slang, very common |
| Angry | *Galit* / *nagagalit* | Stronger, real anger |
| Fed up | *Sawang-sawa na* | Tired of something repeated |
| Quick to take offence | *Pikon* | Someone who gets pissed easily |

So the meaning of pissed in Tagalog is usually *inis* or *asar*. "You're pissing me off" in Tagalog would be *"Naiinis na ako sa iyo"* or, more casually, *"Inaasar mo ako."* Saying *"Badtrip!"* is a quick way to say "this is so annoying."

## "Pissing Me Off" in Bisaya

Cebuano has its own rich set of words for annoyance. These are the ones you are most likely to hear in Cebu and Davao.

| Bisaya | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *Nalagot* | Annoyed, irritated | *Nalagot ko nimo.* — "You're annoying me." |
| *Kalagot!* | "How annoying!" | *Kalagot sa traffic!* — "This traffic is so annoying!" |
| *Nasuko* | Angry | *Ayaw kog sukoa.* — "Don't make me angry." |
| *Kasuko* | Anger / "how infuriating" | Used as an exclamation |
| *Samok* | Noisy, bothersome | *Ayaw samoka ko.* — "Don't bother me." |
| *Kalain* | "How awful", "too bad" | *Kalain sa iyang gibuhat.* — "What he did was so bad." |
| *Pikon* | Easily offended | Shared with Tagalog |

Note the difference between *kalagot* and *kalain*. *Kalagot* is about irritation ("that annoys me"), while *kalain* expresses disapproval or pity ("that's terrible"). *Kalain* can also be sympathetic, as when someone hears bad news about a friend and simply says *"Kalain uy"*, roughly "oh, that's too bad."

For another everyday Cebuano exclamation of exasperation, read our explainer on [the meaning of kayasa](/personal/cebuano-expression-kayasa-mean/).

> **Tip:** Tone matters more than the word. A laughing *"Kalagot nimo!"* between friends is teasing, while the same words said flatly can start a real argument.

## Other Words People Search: Seldom and Scenario in Tagalog

Two more questions often come up alongside the oi meaning:

- **What is seldom in Tagalog?** *Bihira* or *madalang*. Example: *Bihira siyang umuwi* — "He seldom comes home." In Bisaya: *talagsa ra*.
- **What is scenario in Tagalog?** *Senaryo* is the borrowed word, while *sitwasyon* (situation) or *tagpo* (scene) are used in everyday speech.

## How to Use Oi Without Sounding Rude

1. Use *uy* or *oi* with friends, family and people your age.
2. With elders or strangers, add a respectful word: *"Oi, Nang"* (to an older woman) or *"Uy, Kuya"* (to an older man).
3. Avoid shouting *"Hoy!"* at waiters, drivers or vendors. A raised hand and *"Excuse me po"* or *"Kuya, palihog"* (Bisaya for "please") works much better.
4. Smile. Filipinos read tone and facial expression closely.

## Why Little Words Like Oi Matter

Interjections such as *oi*, *uy*, *ay* and *ambot* ("I don't know") carry much of the emotion in Filipino conversation. Mastering them makes you sound natural far faster than memorising long sentences. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the meaning of oi?

*Oi* is an interjection meaning "hey" or "look". Filipinos use it to get attention, express surprise or tease someone, and the tone decides whether it sounds friendly or sharp.

### Is oi the same as uy?

Yes, they are variations of the same interjection. *Oi* and *oy* are very common in Bisaya-speaking Cebu and Davao, while *uy* is heard nationwide.

### What is "pissed off" in Tagalog?

The most common words are *naiinis* (annoyed) and *naaasar* (pissed off). *Badtrip* is popular slang for being in a bad mood, and *galit* means genuinely angry.

### How do you say "you're pissing me off" in Bisaya?

Say *"Nalagot na ko nimo"* ("I'm getting annoyed with you"). For something stronger, *"Ayaw kog sukoa"* means "Don't make me angry."

### Is it rude to say oi to someone?

Not usually. *Oi* is casual and friendly among peers, but with elders or strangers it is more polite to add a term like *Kuya*, *Ate* or *po*.
