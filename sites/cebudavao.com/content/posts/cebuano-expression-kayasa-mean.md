The **kayasa meaning** is simple once you hear it in context: *kayasa* (sometimes written *ka-yasa*) is a Cebuano exclamation of exasperation, roughly "ugh!", "darn it!" or "what a hassle!". It is the kind of word you mutter when the jeepney passes you by or the brownout hits just as your rice is cooking. This guide explains how it is used and covers other everyday Bisaya expressions, including the rude ones visitors should avoid.

## Kayasa meaning and how it is used

Cebuano speakers have many ways to vent frustration, and a lot of them come in softened versions. Just as English speakers say "shoot" or "darn" instead of stronger words, Bisaya speakers often bend a harsh expletive into a milder, playful-sounding one. *Kayasa* belongs to this family of exclamations: it carries the feeling of annoyance without the full force of a curse.

You will usually hear it on its own, with a sigh or a laugh:

- *Kayasa, nabasa akong sapatos!* — "Ugh, my shoes got wet!"
- *Kayasa uy, traffic na pud.* — "Argh, traffic again."
- *Kayasa ani oy!* — "What a pain this is!"

The tone matters. Said with a smile among friends, it is light and even funny. Said sharply at a person, it can sound rude, so it is best kept for situations and things rather than people.

> **Tip:** Tone and audience change everything in Bisaya. A word that is fine with your barkada (group of friends) may sound disrespectful in front of elders, teachers or customers.

## Common Cebuano expressions of annoyance and surprise

Here are expressions you will hear every day in Cebu and Davao, ranked roughly from harmless to rude.

| Expression | Rough meaning | Politeness level |
|---|---|---|
| *Hala!* | Oh no! / Uh-oh! / Watch out! | Harmless, used by everyone |
| *Ay sus!* | Oh my! / Good grief! | Harmless, common with older people |
| *Ambot!* | I don't know! / Whatever! | Casual, can sound dismissive |
| *Samok!* | Annoying! / Noisy! / Such a bother! | Casual, fine among friends |
| *Lagot!* | Irritating! / I'm so annoyed! | Casual, fine among friends |
| *Kapoy!* | So tiring! | Harmless |
| *Kayasa!* | Ugh! / What a hassle! | Casual, mild exclamation |
| *Buang!* | Crazy! / You're nuts! | Can insult if aimed at someone |
| *Yawa!* | Literally "devil"; a curse | Rude, avoid |
| *Atay!* | Literally "liver"; a strong curse | Rude, avoid |

### Hala

*Hala* is the friendliest word on the list. It can express surprise ("Hala, nag-ulan!" — "Oh no, it's raining!"), a warning, or even playful teasing ("Hala ka!" — "You're in trouble now!"). Children and grandparents use it alike.

### Samok and lagot

*Samok* describes something noisy, bothersome or troublesome: *samok kaayo ang silingan* means "the neighbours are so noisy". *Lagot* expresses irritation, and its stronger forms such as *kalagot* are covered in our explainer on [Oi, uy and other Bisaya expressions of annoyance](/culture/meaning-kalata-oi-tagalog-english/).

### Atay and yawa

These two are where visitors often get into trouble. They are heard constantly in movies, on the street and online, and some people use them casually among close friends. But they are genuine curses. Saying them to a stranger, an elder or a shopkeeper is offensive, and saying them in anger at someone can start a real fight.

## Bad words in Bisaya: what to avoid

People often search for "bad words in Bisaya" out of curiosity, and it is useful to know what to avoid. The safest rule for visitors is this: if you heard it shouted in anger or bleeped on TV, do not use it.

Words and patterns to avoid in polite company:

- **Curses built on *yawa* (devil) and *atay* (liver)**, including variations like *giatay* and *yawaa*.
- **Insults about intelligence or sanity**, such as calling someone *buang* (crazy) or *bogo* (stupid), unless you are joking with very close friends.
- **Anything involving someone's mother or family.** As in many cultures, these insults are taken very seriously.
- **Tagalog borrowings used as curses.** Expressions such as *bwisit* (originally a word for a jinx or a nuisance) are common but still sound irritated and impolite in formal settings.

## Other words people ask about

### How to pronounce lamia

*Lamia* is the exclamation "how delicious!" from *lami* (tasty). It is said in three syllables, roughly "la-mee-ya", and you will hear it the moment someone tastes good lechon. *Lami kaayo* means "very delicious".

### Definitely meaning in Tagalog and Bisaya

There is no single word for "definitely" in Tagalog, but common choices are *siguradong* or *tiyak* ("certainly, surely") and *talaga* ("really, truly"). In Bisaya you would say *sigurado gyud* ("surely, for sure") or simply add *gyud* for emphasis: *Moanhi gyud ko* — "I'll definitely come."

| English | Tagalog | Bisaya |
|---|---|---|
| Definitely | Siguradong / tiyak / talaga | Sigurado gyud |
| Really? | Talaga? | Tinuod? |
| For sure | Sigurado | Sure gyud / sigurado |
| I don't know | Ewan ko / Hindi ko alam | Ambot / Wala ko kahibalo |

## Why Cebuanos soften their swear words

Cebuano culture places a high value on *respeto* (respect) for elders and on keeping social harmony. Softened exclamations let people release frustration without offending anyone nearby. That is why you will hear so many mild, almost comic expressions in daily speech, and why a grandmother might say *ay sus* where a younger person might say something stronger.

Humour also plays a big role. Many Bisaya exclamations are said with a grin, and exaggerated frustration is part of everyday comedy, on radio, in teleseryes and in online videos from Cebu and Davao. For more on how the local languages differ, read about [the languages of Cebu and Davao](/culture/the-trouble-with-filipino-tongues/).

## Using these words as a visitor

Locals generally love it when foreigners and Filipinos from other regions try Bisaya. A cheerful *hala!* or *lami kaayo!* will usually earn a laugh and a smile. Save the mild exclamations like *kayasa* and *samok* for casual moments with friends, and leave the curses alone entirely. You will not lose any fun by skipping them, and you will avoid awkward moments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does kayasa mean in Cebuano?

*Kayasa* is a mild Cebuano exclamation of exasperation, similar to "ugh!" or "what a hassle!" in English. It expresses annoyance without the full force of a curse.

### Is kayasa a bad word?

It is generally considered mild, but it still signals irritation. Use it about situations rather than at people, and avoid it in formal settings or in front of elders.

### What are the most offensive bad words in Bisaya?

Curses built on *yawa* (devil) and *atay* (liver), and any insult involving someone's family, are the ones most likely to offend. Visitors should avoid them completely.

### What does hala mean in Bisaya?

*Hala* means "oh no!" or "uh-oh!" and can also be a warning or playful teasing. It is harmless and used by people of all ages.

### How do you say "definitely" in Bisaya?

You can say *sigurado gyud*, or add *gyud* to a sentence for emphasis, as in *Moanhi gyud ko* ("I'll definitely come").
