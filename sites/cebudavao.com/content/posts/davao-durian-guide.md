Eating durian Davao-style is a must: it is the city's signature fruit, and Davao proudly calls itself the durian capital of the Philippines. The peak season usually runs from around August to October, when stands overflow with varieties such as Puyat, Arancillo and Duyaya, alongside Thai-origin types like Monthong. Eat it fresh at a roadside stall, try it in candy or ice cream, and just don't try to sneak it into your hotel room.

## Durian Davao quick facts

| Detail | Information |
|---|---|
| Local names | Durian (same in Bisaya, Tagalog and English) |
| Peak season | Roughly August to October; some fruit available outside this window |
| Popular varieties | Puyat, Arancillo, Duyaya, Chanee, Monthong, plus native types |
| Where it grows | Farms around Davao City and the wider Davao Region |
| Where to buy | Fruit stands and markets around the city, including the Magsaysay area |
| Taste | Creamy, sweet, custard-like, sometimes slightly bitter |
| Smell | Strong; many hotels, taxis and airlines don't allow it |

## Why Davao is the durian capital

Durian thrives in Davao's warm climate and fertile volcanic soils near Mount Apo, and the region sits outside the main typhoon belt, which helps fruit trees. The fruit is part of the city's identity: you'll see it at fruit stands, on souvenirs, in pasalubong boxes, and celebrated during the [Kadayawan Festival](/festivals/whats-so-interesting-about-the-kadayawan-festival/) in August, when harvest season is getting into full swing.

There's a popular local saying that durian "smells like hell but tastes like heaven". Visitors either fall in love at first bite or politely pass. Either way, trying it is a Davao rite of passage.

## Durian varieties you'll find in Davao

Davao growers have produced and adopted many varieties. Here are the names you're most likely to see at stands. Availability changes from week to week, so ask the vendor what's good that day.

| Variety | Typical traits | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Puyat | Thick, creamy flesh; rich, sweet taste | A Davao favourite and often priced higher |
| Arancillo | Sweet, golden flesh | Well-known local variety |
| Duyaya | Sweet, aromatic | Popular among regulars |
| Chanee | Yellow flesh; robust flavour | Thai-origin variety grown in Davao |
| Monthong | Large fruit with thick, mild, sweet flesh | Thai-origin; good for first-timers |
| Native / *lokal* | Smaller fruit; stronger smell and flavour | Often cheaper; loved by purists |

> **Tip:** First time? Ask for a milder variety like Monthong. If you like it, move on to richer, stronger varieties like Puyat or a native fruit.

## When is durian season in Davao?

Davao's durian season usually peaks from about August to October, with fruit starting to arrive earlier in some years and lingering later in others. Weather, farm location and variety all play a part. Outside the peak, durian is still sold, but there's less choice and prices tend to be higher.

## How to choose a good durian

You don't need to be an expert. Use your senses and let the vendor help.

1. **Smell it.** A ripe durian has a strong, sweet aroma near the base. Little smell may mean underripe.
2. **Check the stem.** A fresh, moist stem suggests it fell or was cut recently. A dry, shrivelled stem means it's been sitting a while.
3. **Shake gently.** Some people listen for seeds moving slightly inside, which can mean the flesh has softened.
4. **Look at the shell.** Avoid fruit with cracks, wet spots or signs of insects. Thorns should feel firm.
5. **Ask the vendor to open it.** Most stands will open the fruit for you and let you see the pods before you pay.
6. **Check the pods.** Good flesh is creamy, evenly coloured and not watery.

Prices are usually by the kilo and vary a lot with variety and season. Ask for the price per kilo before the vendor weighs it, and confirm the total before they open it.

## Where to buy durian in Davao

Fruit stands are found throughout the city, but the area around Magsaysay Park and the nearby fruit market zone is the best-known spot for visitors. Stalls there sell durian, mangosteen, pomelo, rambutan and marang, and many have tables where you can eat on the spot. Eating at the stall solves the hotel problem neatly.

Public markets like Bankerohan and roadside stands on the way to Calinan and Toril also sell durian, often straight from nearby farms. ## Durian bans: hotels, taxis and planes

Durian's smell clings to rooms, cars and cabins, so many hotels, taxis and airlines don't allow fresh durian. You'll often see "No durian" signs at hotel entrances in Davao.

- **Hotels:** Most prohibit durian in rooms, and some may charge a cleaning fee.
- **Taxis and Grab:** Ask before bringing durian on board; many drivers say no.
- **Flights:** Airlines commonly restrict fresh durian in the cabin and in checked bags. Check your airline's rules and our guide to [what you can bring on flights](/travel/baggage-items-allowed-and-prohibited-on-a-flight-by-the-tsa/).

Durian is also very filling and rich. If you have a health condition, eat it in moderation and ask your doctor if you're unsure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is durian season in Davao?

Durian season in Davao usually peaks from around August to October. Some fruit is available outside those months, but with less choice and often higher prices.

### What is the best durian variety in Davao?

Puyat is one of the most prized local varieties for its thick, creamy flesh. Arancillo, Duyaya and Thai-origin Monthong are also popular, and the "best" depends on your taste.

### Where can I buy durian in Davao City?

The fruit stands around Magsaysay Park are the best-known spot for visitors. Public markets and roadside stands toward Calinan and Toril also sell durian.

### Can I bring durian into my hotel in Davao?

Most hotels in Davao don't allow durian because of the strong smell. Eat it at the fruit stand, or bring home packaged durian products instead.
