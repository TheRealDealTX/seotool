Fake News in the Philippines: How to Spot It and Fact-Check

Not every viral post is true. Learn the red flags of fake news in the Philippines, how satire gets mistaken for news, and the free tools Filipinos use to fact-check.

Fake news in the Philippines spreads fastest on social media, through shared screenshots, edited photos, copycat pages and satire mistaken for real reporting. The good news is that most false stories show the same warning signs, and a few minutes of checking with trusted fact-checkers and simple tools can save you from sharing them. This guide shows you what to look for and where to verify.

Why fake news in the Philippines spreads so easily

The Philippines is one of the world's most active social media countries, and for many people Facebook, messaging apps and video platforms are the main source of news. That creates a perfect channel for misinformation. Stories that trigger strong emotions, such as anger, fear or national pride, are shared quickly, often before anyone reads past the headline.

Elections, disasters and health scares tend to bring waves of false claims. During typhoons, for example, fake warnings and edited weather maps circulate alongside real advisories. For real storm information, rely on official sources like PAGASA and our explainer on PAGASA typhoon signals.

Satire vs fake news: the Adobo Chronicles example

Not every untrue article is meant to deceive. Satire sites write deliberately made-up stories for humour or commentary. The problem starts when a satirical headline is screenshotted and shared without context, and readers take it as fact.

Adobo Chronicles is a well-known example often discussed in the Philippines: it presents itself as satire, but its headlines have been shared as if they were real news. The lesson isn't that one site is "fake"; it's that you should always check what kind of publication you're reading. Look for an "About" page, a disclaimer, or a tagline that explains the site's purpose.

Tip: Before sharing, ask three questions: Who made this? What's the evidence? What do other trusted sources say? If you can't answer all three, don't share yet.

Trusted fact-checkers in the Philippines

Several organisations regularly check viral claims. Search their sites for the story you're unsure about.

Fact-checkerWhat they do
VERA FilesIndependent fact checks of claims by public figures and viral posts
RapplerNews site with a dedicated fact-check section
Tsek.phCollaborative fact-checking project involving newsrooms and universities, active around elections
AFP Fact CheckInternational news agency fact-checking, including Philippine claims

Local and regional newsrooms also correct false stories that affect their areas. In Cebu and Davao, established outlets such as SunStar are useful references; see our guides to SunStar Superbalita Cebu and Davao news sources.

How to verify a photo or video

Images are the most shared kind of fake news because they feel like proof. Here's how to check them:

  1. Reverse image search. Use Google Lens, Google Images, Bing Visual Search or TinEye. Upload the image or paste its link to see where else it has appeared and when.
  2. Check the oldest result. If the photo appeared years earlier in a different context, the new story is misleading.
  3. Look closely for edits. Strange shadows, warped backgrounds, mismatched fonts on "official" documents and blurry logos are common signs.
  4. For videos, grab key frames. Take screenshots and search them the same way. Check the video's earliest upload.
  5. Check location clues. Signs, languages, weather, vehicles and landmarks can show whether a video was really taken where the caption says.
  6. Be careful with AI-generated images. Look for odd hands, distorted text and unnatural textures, and check whether any credible outlet has published the same image.

How to check a Facebook page or website

  • Page transparency. On Facebook, open the page's transparency or "About" section to see when it was created, whether its name has changed, and where its managers are located.
  • Domain check. Look carefully at the web address. Real outlets use consistent domains; fake ones may add extra letters or unusual endings.
  • Contact details. Legitimate news organisations list editors, an address and a way to send corrections.
  • Other stories. Scroll through the page. If it's all outrage, miracle cures or celebrity deaths, be sceptical.

Health, money and government scams

Some of the most harmful fake posts aren't political at all. Watch out for:

  • Miracle cures. Posts claiming a plant, gadget or animal cures serious disease. Consult a doctor and check the Department of Health. Our story on the tuko-buying craze is a classic example of a medicine rumour that went viral.
  • Fake ayuda or job offers. Links promising government aid or jobs in exchange for your details or a fee. Check the official agency website or page.
  • Fake bank messages. Texts or emails asking for your OTP or PIN. Banks don't ask for these; see our tips in the BPI card guide.

What to do when family shares fake news

It's common for a relative to forward a dubious post to the family group chat. Be kind rather than confrontational. Share a fact-check link, explain how you checked, and suggest the habit of checking before forwarding. People are more likely to change their minds when they don't feel embarrassed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if news in the Philippines is fake?

Check the source, look for an author and date, see if trusted outlets report the same thing, and search fact-checkers like VERA Files, Rappler, Tsek.ph or AFP Fact Check. Be extra careful with shocking headlines and old photos.

Is Adobo Chronicles a real news site?

Adobo Chronicles presents itself as satire, so its stories aren't meant to be read as factual news. Problems arise when its headlines are shared without that context.

What are the best fact-checking sites in the Philippines?

Well-known options include VERA Files, Rappler's fact-check section, Tsek.ph and AFP Fact Check. Searching more than one gives a fuller picture.

How do I check if a photo is fake?

Run a reverse image search with Google Lens or TinEye to find earlier copies of the photo. If it appeared before in a different context, the new claim is likely misleading.
