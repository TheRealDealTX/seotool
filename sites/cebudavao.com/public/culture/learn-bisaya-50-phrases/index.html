Learn Bisaya: 50 Essential Cebuano Phrases for Travellers

Fifty everyday Cebuano phrases for greetings, getting around, eating, shopping and counting, with pronunciation tips to help you sound friendly in Cebu and Davao.

The fastest way to learn Bisaya is to start with a handful of everyday phrases: greetings, thank-yous, directions, food and prices. Cebuano, the language most people mean by "Bisaya," is spoken across Cebu, much of the Visayas and most of Mindanao, including Davao City. Here are 50 essential phrases, grouped by situation, with simple pronunciation tips.

Quick Pronunciation Tips

Cebuano is mostly pronounced as it's written, which makes it friendly for beginners.

Letter / soundHow to say itExample
a"ah" as in fathersalamat (sah-LAH-maht)
e / ioften between "eh" and "ee"ikaw (ee-KOW)
o / uoften between "oh" and "oo"unsa (OON-sah)
ngas in singerngalan (NGAH-lahn)
mgasaid "mah-NGA"mga tawo (people)

Stress often falls on the second-to-last syllable, but not always. Listening and copying locals is the best teacher. Don't worry about perfection; Cebuanos and Davaoeños appreciate any effort.

Tip: Add Day (for a woman) or Dong (for a man) to sound warm and local, as in "Salamat, Day!" These are friendly, informal terms used widely in Cebu and Davao.

Rice is central to every Cebuano meal, and in Cebu you'll often get it as puso, rice cooked in a woven coconut-leaf pouch. Practise lami at a street-food stall; see our Cebu street food guide.

Shopping and Haggling (33-40)

Hangyo is polite bargaining, common at markets but not in malls or fixed-price shops. Keep it friendly and smile; a small discount or an extra piece of fruit is often the result.

In daily life, especially for prices and time, many people use Spanish-derived numbers instead: uno, dos, tres, and prices like singkwenta (50) or siyento (100). Don't be surprised to hear both systems in the same sentence.

Bisaya vs Tagalog: Watch Out for False Friends

Bisaya and Tagalog share many words, but some look the same and mean different things. Pila means "how much" in Bisaya but "queue" in Tagalog, and nalibog means "confused" in Bisaya but has a very different meaning in Tagalog. Read our article on nalibog and other false friends before you try out your new phrases in Manila.

Tips to Learn Bisaya Faster

  • Use it daily. Greet your guard, driver and tindera (shopkeeper) in Bisaya.
  • Listen to Bisaya music. Bisrock and Bisaya pop are full of everyday phrases; start with our list of Bisrock bands.
  • Learn expressions, not just words. Phrases like ambot (I don't know) and sige (okay) unlock conversations.
  • Ask locals to correct you. Most people will happily help and might teach you some slang.
  • Read about the languages of the region in the trouble with Filipino tongues.

Kaayo means "very," and it attaches to almost anything: lami kaayo (very delicious), gwapa kaayo (very beautiful), mahal kaayo (very expensive). If you want to give compliments, see our guide to gwapo and gwapa.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Bisaya the same as Cebuano?

In everyday use, "Bisaya" usually refers to Cebuano, the language of Cebu and much of Mindanao. Technically, "Bisayan languages" is a wider family that also includes Hiligaynon, Waray and others.

Do people in Davao speak Bisaya?

Yes. Cebuano is the main everyday language in Davao City, although Tagalog and English are also widely understood, and indigenous languages are spoken in some communities.

How long does it take to learn Bisaya?

You can learn basic phrases in a few days and hold simple conversations after a few months of regular practice. Daily use with locals speeds things up.

What does "salamat" mean?

Salamat means "thank you" in both Bisaya and Tagalog. Say daghang salamat for "thank you very much" in Bisaya.

What is "lugar lang"?

Lugar lang is what you say to a jeepney or taxi driver when you want to stop and get off. It roughly means "just here, please."
