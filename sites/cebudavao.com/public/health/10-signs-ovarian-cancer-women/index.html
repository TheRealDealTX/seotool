10 Warning Signs of Ovarian Cancer Every Woman Should Know

Bloating, pelvic pain and feeling full quickly can be early signs of ovarian cancer. Learn the 10 warning signs, risk factors and when to see a doctor.

The 10 warning signs of ovarian cancer include persistent bloating, pelvic or abdominal pain, feeling full quickly, urinary urgency, changes in bowel habits, back pain, fatigue, pain during sex, unexplained weight change and abnormal vaginal bleeding. These symptoms are common and usually caused by something less serious, but if they are new, frequent and last more than a few weeks, see a doctor. Ovarian cancer is often called a "silent" disease because early signs are easy to dismiss, which is why knowing them matters.

This article is for general information, not medical advice. If you have symptoms or concerns, please see a doctor.

What Is Ovarian Cancer?

Ovarian cancer is a cancer that begins in the ovaries, the fallopian tubes or the nearby lining of the abdomen (peritoneum). Because the ovaries sit deep in the pelvis, small tumours rarely cause obvious symptoms, and many cases are found at a later stage. When it is found early, treatment is more likely to be successful.

1. Persistent bloating

Most women experience bloating sometimes. The warning sign is bloating that is there most days, doesn't go away, and may make clothes feel tighter around the waist.

2. Pelvic or abdominal pain

A dull, persistent ache or pressure in the lower abdomen or pelvis, especially if it is new, deserves attention.

3. Feeling full quickly or trouble eating

Feeling full after only a few bites, or losing your appetite, can happen when a growth or fluid presses on the stomach.

4. Urinary urgency or frequency

Needing to urinate suddenly or more often, without signs of a urinary tract infection, can be caused by pressure on the bladder.

5. Changes in bowel habits

New, persistent constipation or other bowel changes can be a symptom, though they have many common causes.

6. Back pain

Lower back pain that doesn't improve and has no obvious cause, such as heavy lifting, should be checked if it occurs with other symptoms.

7. Fatigue

Tiredness that is unusual for you and lasts for weeks is a vague but important sign, especially combined with others on this list.

8. Pain during sex

Discomfort or pain during intercourse that is new or getting worse should be discussed with a doctor.

9. Unexplained weight loss or abdominal swelling

Losing weight without trying, or a belly that keeps getting bigger even while you lose weight elsewhere, can signal fluid build-up.

10. Abnormal vaginal bleeding

Any bleeding after menopause, or bleeding that is unusual for you, should always be checked by a doctor.

Tip: Doctors say the key is frequency and persistence. Symptoms that happen most days for two to three weeks, or that are new for you, are worth checking even if they seem minor.

Risk Factors for Ovarian Cancer

Anyone with ovaries can develop ovarian cancer, but some factors increase the risk:

  • Age: Risk rises with age, and most cases occur after menopause.
  • Family history: Close relatives with ovarian, breast or colorectal cancer.
  • Inherited gene changes: Such as BRCA1, BRCA2 or Lynch syndrome.
  • Endometriosis.
  • Never having been pregnant.
  • Hormone replacement therapy after menopause, especially long-term use.
  • Excess body weight.

Factors linked with lower risk include having been pregnant, breastfeeding and long-term use of oral contraceptives. Talk to your doctor about your personal risk before making any decisions.

Is There a Screening Test?

There is currently no reliable screening test for ovarian cancer in women at average risk. A Pap smear checks for cervical cancer, not ovarian cancer. Blood tests such as CA-125 and transvaginal ultrasound may be used to investigate symptoms or to monitor high-risk women, but they are not recommended as routine screening for everyone.

That is why recognising symptoms and seeing a doctor promptly is so important. Women with a strong family history may be referred for genetic counselling.

When to See a Doctor

See a doctor, ideally an obstetrician-gynaecologist (OB-GYN), if you have:

  • Any of the signs above most days for two to three weeks
  • Bleeding after menopause
  • A family history of ovarian or breast cancer and new symptoms

A doctor may do a pelvic exam, ultrasound and blood tests to find the cause. Most of the time, symptoms turn out to be something less serious, but early checks give peace of mind.

Getting Help in the Philippines

In Cebu and Davao, major public and private hospitals have OB-GYN and oncology departments. Ask your barangay health centre or family doctor for a referral if you're unsure where to start.

Useful resources:

  • The Philippine Cancer Society offers education and support.
  • The National Integrated Cancer Control Act (Republic Act No. 11215) aims to improve cancer care and access to services in the country.
  • PhilHealth has benefit packages for some cancer treatments; check current coverage with PhilHealth or your hospital's billing office.

For reliable general information, see the World Health Organization and the American Cancer Society.

Looking After Your Health

Regular check-ups, knowing your family history and listening to your body are the best tools you have. Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of ovarian cancer?

Early signs are often persistent bloating, pelvic or abdominal pain, feeling full quickly and needing to urinate more often. They are easy to dismiss, so see a doctor if they last more than two to three weeks.

Can a Pap smear detect ovarian cancer?

No. A Pap smear screens for cervical cancer. There is no routine screening test for ovarian cancer in average-risk women, so watching for symptoms is important.

Who is most at risk of ovarian cancer?

Risk increases with age and is higher in women with a family history of ovarian or breast cancer, certain inherited gene changes such as BRCA1 or BRCA2, or endometriosis.

Is bloating always a sign of ovarian cancer?

No. Bloating is very common and usually due to diet, digestion or hormones. It becomes a concern when it is persistent, happens most days and is new for you.

Which doctor should I see for ovarian cancer symptoms?

Start with an OB-GYN or your family doctor, who can examine you and order tests such as an ultrasound or blood work if needed.
