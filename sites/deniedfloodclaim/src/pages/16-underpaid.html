Flood claim underpaid: finding the gap between the check and the repair bill

When a flood claim is underpaid, the cause is usually not one big mistake. It is dozens of small ones: missed rooms, low unit prices, depreciation, and items placed in the wrong coverage. Here is where to look, and the tools the policy gives you to fix it.

Treat a short payment as a partial denial

When an NFIP insurer pays part of a claim and refuses the rest, the letter that explains the payment is usually a written denial "in part." Under SFIP Section VII.O and 42 U.S.C. 4072, that starts the one-year period to sue over the unpaid portion, and under 44 CFR 62.20 it starts the 60-day window for a FEMA appeal. Do not let a partial payment lull you into waiting.

For a private flood policy, Florida's claim statute requires the insurer to pay or deny within 60 days of notice, and when it pays less than its own adjuster's detailed estimate, to explain the difference in writing (section 627.70131(7)(a)). Late payments of covered amounts carry statutory interest under that subsection.

Where flood estimates usually come in low

Scope gaps

The adjuster's estimate may omit areas or tasks: drywall cut higher than the water line to reach wet insulation, baseboards and trim throughout, cabinets that delaminated, flooring beneath appliances, electrical outlets below the water line, doors that swelled, and mold-prevention drying. Walk the estimate room by room against photographs.

Unit prices

Estimates are often built with pricing software that may lag local post-storm labor and material costs. A contractor's estimate in the same line-item format makes the comparison concrete.

Depreciation on contents

Under the NFIP Dwelling Form, personal property is settled at actual cash value, meaning replacement cost minus depreciation (SFIP Section VII.R.4.e). Disputes often turn on the age and condition assigned to each item. A detailed inventory with purchase dates, receipts, photos and model numbers is the remedy.

Replacement cost conditions on the building

A single-family principal residence insured to at least 80 percent of its full replacement cost (or to the NFIP maximum) qualifies for replacement cost on the building (Section VII.R.1.a). If the repair costs more than $1,000 or more than 5 percent of the building coverage, the insurer is not liable for the replacement cost portion until the repair or replacement is actually completed (Section VII.R.2.c). You may first claim actual cash value and later claim the additional replacement cost amount, provided you notify the insurer of that intent within 180 days after the date of loss (Section VII.R.2.d).

Wrong coverage or no coverage

Items may be denied as "below the lowest elevated floor," or charged to the wrong deductible. Separate deductibles apply to building and contents in each flood loss (Section VI.B). See what NFIP flood insurance covers.

Wind damage left out

If part of the loss was wind, neither the flood estimate nor the flood check will include it. That portion belongs on the homeowners or windstorm claim; read wind vs. flood damage claims.

How SFIP appraisal works

Appraisal under Section VII.M is available when you and the insurer disagree on the actual cash value or replacement cost of damaged property. It is not a way to decide whether something is covered. The steps:

  1. Written demand. Either side may demand appraisal in writing.
  2. Appraisers. Each side chooses a competent and impartial appraiser within 20 days of the other's written request.
  3. Umpire. The appraisers choose an umpire. If they cannot agree within 15 days, either side may ask a judge of a court of record in Florida to choose.
  4. Valuation. The appraisers separately state actual cash value, replacement cost and the amount of loss for each item. An agreement between them, or between either appraiser and the umpire, sets the amount.
  5. Costs. Each side pays its own appraiser and splits the umpire and other appraisal expenses equally.
Appraisal and FEMA appeal do not mix. A dispute that has been through appraisal cannot be appealed to FEMA, and once an issue is appealed it can no longer go to appraisal (44 CFR 62.20(c)). Choose deliberately.

Building a stronger supplement

  • One comparison document. List each line where your estimate and the insurer's differ, the dollar gap, and the exhibit that supports your number.
  • Proof of what was hidden. Photos taken during demolition showing wet insulation, damaged subfloor or corroded wiring.
  • Invoices. Paid invoices for mitigation, drying and repairs carry more weight than estimates.
  • Contents detail. Age, condition, original cost and current replacement cost for each item, with photos.
  • Code and ICC. If the community declares the building substantially damaged, Increased Cost of Compliance coverage of up to $30,000 may apply toward elevation or other compliance work (Section III.D).

Frequently asked questions

Should I cash the flood insurance check if I think it is too low?

Whether accepting a payment affects your right to pursue more depends on what you sign with it. Read any release, proof of loss or settlement language carefully. If a document says the payment is in full and final settlement, get legal advice before signing or depositing.

Why did NFIP pay actual cash value on my house?

Either the home did not qualify for replacement cost (not a principal residence, or insured below 80 percent of replacement cost and below the NFIP maximum), or the repair is not finished yet. Under Section VII.R.2.c, replacement cost on larger losses is paid after repairs are completed, and you must give notice of your intent to claim it within 180 days of the loss.

Can I use appraisal for an underpaid flood claim?

Yes, if the dispute is about value, not coverage. Section VII.M of the SFIP provides the procedure, and many private flood policies include a similar clause.

Does the deductible apply twice?

Under the NFIP Dwelling Form, separate deductibles apply to building and personal property in each flood loss (Section VI.B). Deductibles do not apply to loss avoidance measures or Increased Cost of Compliance.
