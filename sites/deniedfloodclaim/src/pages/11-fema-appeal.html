44 CFR 62.20

Flood insurance appeal to FEMA: the 60-day process, step by step

A flood insurance appeal lets an NFIP policyholder ask FEMA to review the insurer's claim decision without filing a lawsuit. The appeal is free and fairly informal, but it has a short filing window and some traps that can cost you the right to sue.

What a flood insurance appeal to FEMA is

The claims appeal process is set out in a federal regulation, 44 CFR 62.20. It lets a policyholder insured by a Write Your Own company or directly by FEMA appeal the insurer's "final claim determination," which the regulation defines as the insurer's written denial, in whole or in part, of the claim. Once that decision issues, you may appeal any action taken by the insurer, a FEMA employee or contractor, an insurance adjuster or an insurance agent with respect to the claim, the proof of loss or the loss estimate.

The appeal is decided by FEMA's Federal Insurance Administrator, not by the insurance company that denied the claim. That independent look is its main advantage.

The 60-day filing deadline

Under 44 CFR 62.20(e)(1), you must submit a written appeal to FEMA within 60 days from the date of the decision. The decision date is the date of the written denial letter, not the date you received or read it. Count calendar days and send the appeal well before day 60, by a method that gives you proof of delivery.

The appeal does not pause anything. Section 62.20(c) states that filing an appeal does not waive any requirement for perfecting a claim under the SFIP and does not extend any time limit in the SFIP. The one-year suit deadline in SFIP Section VII.O keeps running while FEMA reviews your appeal.

What the appeal must include

The regulation lists four requirements. To pursue an appeal, a policyholder must:

  1. Submit a written appeal to FEMA within 60 days of the decision.
  2. Attach a copy of the insurer's written denial, whether full or partial.
  3. Identify the policy and claim information and state the basis for the appeal, that is, what you believe the insurer got wrong and why.
  4. Submit documentation that supports the appeal. Send only documents that relate to your claim.

Section 62.20(e)(4) gives a long list of examples FEMA may require. For a typical Florida single-family home the most useful ones are:

  • The proof of loss you submitted to the insurer.
  • Room-by-room itemized estimates from the adjuster and from your contractor, with unit costs and quantities.
  • Clear interior and exterior photographs showing that the damage resulted from direct physical loss by or from flood.
  • Detailed damaged personal property inventories that include the approximate age of each item.
  • The declarations page and, for an elevated building, the elevation certificate.
  • Engineering reports that specifically address flood-related damage versus pre-existing damage.
  • Proof of other insurance, including homeowners or wind policies and any claim information submitted to those companies.
  • Paid receipts and invoices for work already done.

FEMA also lists letters of representation from attorneys and public adjusters, so representation is expected and allowed.

How FEMA processes the appeal

According to 44 CFR 62.20(f), FEMA acknowledges the appeal in writing and gives you a point of contact. The Administrator reviews the documents and may ask for more information. Any request must give you at least 14 calendar days to respond, and failing to provide the information or ask for an extension by the due date may get the appeal dismissed. FEMA may also re-inspect the property.

The regulation says the Administrator will provide a written appeal decision to you and the insurer within 90 days from the date all information has been submitted, stating the grounds for the original denial and specific information for resolving the appeal. There is no further administrative review. If you disagree with the result, section 62.20(f)(4) points you back to the SFIP's suit clause and repeats that the one-year period starts with the insurer's written denial and is not extended by the appeal.

What an appeal cannot do

  • It cannot add coverage. Section 62.20(c) says the process is meant to resolve claim issues, not to grant coverage or limits the SFIP does not provide. An appeal will not, for example, create coverage for finished basement walls.
  • It cannot revisit appraisal. Disputes that are or have been in appraisal under the SFIP cannot be appealed (62.20(c)(1)).
  • It closes off appraisal. Once you appeal an issue, that issue is no longer available for appraisal or other pre-litigation remedies (62.20(c)(2)).
  • It ends if you sue. A policyholder who files suit on the claim issue cannot appeal, and pending appeals are terminated when litigation begins (62.20(d)).

Writing the basis for the appeal

A focused appeal letter usually does better than a long one. Quote the sentence in the denial you dispute, cite the SFIP section, and explain why the facts or the policy wording lead to a different result. Then point to the exhibit that proves each fact: the photo of the water line, the neighbor's flooding, the contractor's line item. Avoid arguments about fairness; the Administrator applies the policy as written.

If the denial says the water was not a flood, address each element of the SFIP definition: two or more acres of normally dry land or two or more properties, a general and temporary condition, and one of the listed sources. If the denial cites earth movement or pre-existing damage, an engineer's report addressing that specific point is often the most persuasive exhibit.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a fee to file a flood insurance appeal with FEMA?

44 CFR 62.20 does not impose a filing fee. You may hire a lawyer or public adjuster at your own expense, and FEMA's document list expressly includes letters of representation.

Does a FEMA appeal extend the time to sue?

No. Section 62.20(c) says an appeal does not extend any time limitation in the SFIP, and the SFIP requires suit within one year after the written denial. Track that date separately.

Can I appeal if I have a private flood policy?

No. The FEMA appeal is available only to NFIP policyholders. Private flood policies are governed by their own terms and Florida law, which may provide mediation or appraisal instead.

What happens after FEMA decides the appeal?

FEMA sends a written decision to you and the insurer with specific information for resolving the appeal. No further administrative review is available. If the result does not resolve the dispute, the remaining route is a federal lawsuit within the one-year period that began with the insurer's written denial.
