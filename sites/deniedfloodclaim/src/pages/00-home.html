Why a denied flood claim is not like a denied homeowners claim

Standard Florida homeowners policies exclude flood. Flood damage is insured separately, usually under the National Flood Insurance Program's Standard Flood Insurance Policy (SFIP), which is a federal regulation printed word for word at 44 CFR Part 61, Appendix A. A private insurer sells and services most of these policies under FEMA's Write Your Own program, but the contract terms, the appeal route and the court are federal.

So if your flood insurance claim was denied in Florida, the first question is which rules apply. That has practical consequences. Florida's homeowner claim statutes, such as the 60-day pay-or-deny rule in section 627.70131 and the pre-suit notice in section 627.70152, are written for state-regulated property policies, and federal courts have long held that federal law controls NFIP claims. The NFIP's own clock is shorter and stricter, and courts generally enforce it.

A private flood policy is different again. It is a state-regulated contract governed by section 627.715 of the Florida Statutes and the policy wording, so Florida claim-handling rules and the state statute of limitations generally apply. Knowing which kind of policy you hold is step one.

Common reasons

Why insurers deny or cut flood claims

Most denial letters cite one of a short list of policy provisions. Knowing the provision tells you what evidence answers it.

  • Late or missing proof of loss. The SFIP makes a timely, signed proof of loss a condition of payment. It is the single most litigated issue in NFIP cases.
  • "Not a flood." The SFIP defines flood narrowly: a general and temporary inundation of two or more acres of normally dry land or two or more properties. Water that only affected your house may not qualify.
  • Wind, not water (or the reverse). After a hurricane, the flood carrier may blame wind and the homeowners carrier may blame flood. See wind vs. flood damage claims.
  • Earth movement. Settlement, erosion and soil shifting are excluded even when flood water caused them.
  • Basement and below-elevated-floor limits. Coverage below the lowest elevated floor of many post-FIRM buildings is limited to a short list of items.
  • Mold and long-term seepage. Mold from failing to dry and maintain the property after the water recedes is excluded.
  • Pre-existing damage. The adjuster attributes cracks or rot to age rather than the flood.

The full list of what is and is not covered is in what NFIP flood insurance covers.

Before you call anyone

Five things to do this week after a denied flood claim

  1. Find the date on the denial letter. Write the 60-day appeal date and the one-year suit date on the letter itself.
  2. Get the whole claim file. Ask the insurer in writing for the adjuster's report, the estimate, photos and any engineer report.
  3. Locate your proof of loss. Confirm what you signed, when, and for how much. If none was filed, that is urgent.
  4. Check for a second policy. A homeowners, windstorm or excess flood policy may cover what the flood policy denied.
  5. Keep documenting. Photos, receipts, contractor estimates and moisture readings. Do not throw out damaged items before they are recorded.

Questions about a flood claim denial

Can I appeal a denied flood claim?

Yes, if it is an NFIP policy. Under 44 CFR 62.20 you may file a written appeal with FEMA within 60 days of the date of the insurer's written denial. The appeal is optional, does not extend any SFIP deadline, and ends if you file suit. A private flood policy has no FEMA appeal; its options come from the policy and Florida law.

How long do I have to sue over an NFIP flood claim?

The SFIP (Section VII.O) and 42 U.S.C. 4072 require suit within one year after the date of the written denial of all or part of the claim, filed in the federal district court for the district where the property is located. Courts treat this as a firm deadline.

Does my homeowners policy cover flood?

Standard homeowners policies exclude flood. They usually do cover wind and wind-driven rain damage under the policy terms, which is why a hurricane loss often involves two separate claims.
