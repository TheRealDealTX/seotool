Your flood policy is a federal regulation

When you buy NFIP flood insurance, whether through FEMA directly or through a Write Your Own (WYO) company such as the carrier that also writes your homeowners policy, you receive the Standard Flood Insurance Policy. Its text is published as a federal regulation: the Dwelling Form is 44 CFR Part 61, Appendix A(1). The insurer cannot change the wording, and claims are paid with federal money.

Three consequences follow for a Florida homeowner whose NFIP claim was denied:

  • Federal law controls. Federal courts, including the Eleventh Circuit, which covers Florida, have generally held that disputes over NFIP claims are governed by the policy and federal law, and that state-law claims about claim handling are largely preempted.
  • Strict compliance is expected. Because the government pays the claim, courts generally require policyholders to meet the policy's conditions, especially the proof of loss requirement, exactly as written.
  • The court is federal. Suit must be filed in the United States District Court for the district where the property is located (SFIP Section VII.O; 42 U.S.C. 4072). Florida has three districts: Northern, Middle and Southern.

Decode the denial letter

A denial letter under an NFIP policy should identify the claim, the policy provisions relied on, and your options. Read it with the policy open. Look for these items and write them down:

  1. The date on the letter. This is the "decision" date for the FEMA appeal and the "written denial" date for the one-year suit limit.
  2. Full or partial. A payment for some items with a refusal of others is a partial denial. It starts the same clocks as to the denied part.
  3. The cited section. SFIP references look like "Section V.C" or "Section III.A.8." Each one points to a specific exclusion or limit.
  4. Reports referenced. If an engineer or the adjuster's report is mentioned, request a copy in writing.
  5. Appeal language. WYO companies are expected to tell you about the FEMA appeal right. Keep that page.
Two clocks start on the letter date. You have 60 days to file a FEMA appeal (44 CFR 62.20(e)) and one year to file suit (SFIP VII.O). Filing the appeal does not stop the one-year clock (44 CFR 62.20(c)).

The reasons NFIP claims are denied most often

No timely proof of loss

Section VII.G.4 of the Dwelling Form requires a signed, sworn proof of loss within 60 days after the loss. Section VII.J.2.c allows an amended proof of loss, but it too must be filed within 60 days of the date of loss. FEMA sometimes extends this period by bulletin after major events; after Hurricanes Helene and Milton in 2024, for example, FEMA first extended it to 120 days and later extended it further for Milton. If no extension applies and no proof of loss was filed, the denial may rest on that alone. See the flood proof of loss deadline guide.

The water was not a "flood"

The SFIP covers only "direct physical loss by or from flood." Flood means a general and temporary condition of partial or complete inundation of two or more acres of normally dry land, or of two or more properties (one of them yours), from overflow of inland or tidal waters, unusual and rapid accumulation or runoff of surface waters, or mudflow (SFIP Section II.B). Water that entered only your home from rain or a broken pipe is generally not a flood under this definition.

An exclusion applies

The common ones are earth movement (Section V.C), which applies even when flood caused the movement; water, moisture, mildew or mold damage from conditions within your control, including failure to inspect and maintain after the flood recedes (Section V.D.4); and sewer or drain backup and seepage unless a flood in the area was the proximate cause (Section V.D.5).

Coverage limits, not exclusions

A basement, or the area below the lowest elevated floor of an elevated post-FIRM building in certain high-risk zones, has limited coverage. Only listed items are covered there, such as furnaces, water heaters, electrical panels, central air units and washers and dryers. Finished walls, flooring and most contents in those areas are not. This is a frequent source of partial denials. Details are in what NFIP flood insurance covers.

Wind, not flood

After a hurricane the flood adjuster may attribute roof, window or upper-floor damage to wind. That damage belongs under a homeowners or windstorm policy. The flood carrier is right to exclude it only if the evidence supports the split. Read wind vs. flood damage claims.

Pre-existing or long-term damage

Adjusters may attribute cracking, rot or settlement to age, prior events or poor maintenance. Older photos, inspection reports from a recent sale, and repair invoices are the usual answer.

NFIP claim denied: your three paths forward

Section VII.J.2 of the SFIP says that if the insurer rejects your proof of loss in whole or in part, you may accept the denial, exercise your rights under the policy, or file an amended proof of loss within the 60-day window. In practice the realistic options are these.

PathDeadlineGood forWatch out
Supplemental or amended claim with the insurerAmended proof: 60 days from loss unless extendedMissed items, new estimates, hidden damage found during repairsDoes not stop the suit clock
FEMA appeal (44 CFR 62.20)60 days from the denial letterCoverage disputes and claim handling errors, at no filing costCannot be used for issues already in appraisal; ends if you sue

The SFIP also contains an appraisal clause (Section VII.M), but it applies only when you and the insurer disagree on the actual cash value or replacement cost of covered property, not on whether something is covered. That route is covered in the underpaid flood claim guide.

Evidence that tends to matter

  • The complete proof of loss you signed, with the date it was sent and proof of delivery.
  • The adjuster's room-by-room estimate with unit prices and quantities.
  • Your own contractor's estimate prepared in a comparable format.
  • Water line photos inside and outside, plus neighborhood flooding photos showing the general condition of flooding.
  • The declarations page and your elevation certificate if the building is elevated.
  • Any homeowners or windstorm claim file for the same storm. The SFIP asks for details of other insurance on the proof of loss (Section VII.G.4.d), and FEMA lists it among appeal documents.

Frequently asked questions

Is a partial payment a denial?

For deadline purposes it can be. The SFIP's suit clause runs from "the written denial of all or part of the claim," and FEMA's appeal rule treats a written denial "in whole or in part" as the decision. Treat a letter that refuses any item as starting the clocks for that item.

Can the insurer reopen my NFIP claim after a denial?

Insurers can review supplemental requests with new documentation, and many claims are adjusted further after a denial. That review does not, by itself, extend the one-year suit deadline from the original written denial, so keep the original date in mind.

Do Florida's homeowner claim laws apply to my NFIP policy?

Generally not. Statutes like section 627.70131 (pay or deny within 60 days) and section 627.70152 (pre-suit notice) govern state-regulated property policies. Federal courts generally apply the SFIP and federal law to NFIP claims. A private flood policy is the exception; see the private flood guide.

What if I never received a denial letter?

The one-year suit clock under the SFIP runs from a written denial. If you have had no written decision, ask the insurer in writing for one and keep a copy. A lawyer can advise on timing in that situation because the analysis depends on the facts.
