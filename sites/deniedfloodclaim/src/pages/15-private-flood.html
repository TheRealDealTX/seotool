Confirm that your policy is private

A private flood policy is issued by an insurance company on its own form, not the federal Standard Flood Insurance Policy. Clues on the declarations page: the insurer's own form numbers, coverage for additional living expenses or replacement cost on contents (which the NFIP Dwelling Form does not offer), or limits above the NFIP maximums. A flood endorsement added to a homeowners policy is also private coverage. If the declarations page says "National Flood Insurance Program," you have an NFIP policy even if a private company services it; see NFIP claim denied instead.

Private flood coverage can come from an authorized (admitted) Florida insurer or, through a surplus lines agent, from an eligible surplus lines insurer (section 627.715(4)). The distinction affects things like guaranty-fund protection, but Florida's main claim-handling statutes discussed below are written to reach both.

The five kinds of Florida flood policies

Section 627.715 of the Florida Statutes lets authorized insurers write personal lines residential flood coverage in several forms. Knowing which one you have helps explain a denial:

TypeWhat the statute requires
StandardThe same coverage as an NFIP standard flood policy, including deductibles and loss adjustment
PreferredStandard coverage plus water intrusion from outside the structure not otherwise within "flood," additional living expenses, and replacement cost on repaired or replaced contents
CustomizedCoverage broader than standard flood insurance
FlexibleFlood coverage with one or more limiting terms, such as a limit tied to the mortgage balance, a hurricane-size deductible, actual cash value settlement, principal-building-only coverage, or no contents coverage
SupplementalFills gaps in an NFIP or other flood policy, for example jewelry, art, deductibles or living expenses

The statute defines "flood" using essentially the same two-acre-or-two-properties language as the NFIP. A "flexible" policy may lawfully pay much less than you expected, so read its limiting terms before assuming a denial or low payment is wrong.

Florida deadlines that apply

  • Notice of claim. Section 627.70132 bars a property insurance claim unless notice is given under the policy terms within 1 year after the date of loss; a supplemental claim must be noticed within 18 months. For hurricanes and other weather events, the date of loss is the date of landfall or the date NOAA verifies the event.
  • Policy duties. The policy may separately require a sworn proof of loss within a set time after the insurer requests it, an inspection, records and an examination under oath. Missing these can support a denial.
  • Insurer timelines. For residential claims, section 627.70131 requires the insurer to acknowledge communications within 7 days, begin investigating within 7 days of a proof of loss, physically inspect (if it inspects) within 30 days of the proof of loss, send you its detailed estimate within 7 days of generating it, and pay or deny within 60 days of notice, absent factors beyond its control. A denial or partial denial must include a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy.
  • Time to sue. Section 95.11 sets the limitation period for an action for breach of a property insurance contract, running from the date of loss, and section 95.03 makes a contract clause shortening the statutory period void. Do not rely on the outer limit: notice and pre-suit requirements come first.
No FEMA appeal. The 60-day FEMA appeal and the one-year federal suit rule are NFIP features. A private flood denial is challenged under the policy and Florida law, usually in state court.

Private flood insurance claim denied: ways to dispute it

  1. Get the reasons and the file. Ask for the written explanation required by section 627.70131(7)(a), the adjuster's detailed estimate, photos and any engineer report.
  2. Supplement or reopen. If new damage or new estimates exist, present a supplemental claim within the 18-month window.
  3. Mediation. Florida's Department of Financial Services runs a mediation program for residential property claims under section 627.7015. Ask DFS whether your flood claim is eligible; the program does not apply to commercial coverages.
  4. Appraisal. If the policy has an appraisal clause and the dispute is about the amount of loss, either side may be able to invoke it.
  5. Pre-suit notice, then suit. Section 627.70152 requires a written notice of intent to initiate litigation, filed through DFS at least 10 business days before suit, and only after the insurer has made a coverage determination. The insurer must respond in writing within 10 business days. After a coverage denial it must accept coverage, continue to deny, or assert a right to reinspect; for other disputes it must make a settlement offer or require appraisal or another alternative dispute resolution method.
  6. Civil Remedy Notice. Where the insurer's handling itself is unreasonable, a Civil Remedy Notice under section 624.155 gives the insurer 60 days to cure and can be a prerequisite to a later bad-faith claim.

Attorney fees after the 2022 and 2023 reforms

Florida's 2022 special session law (SB 2-A) and 2023 tort reform law (HB 837) removed the one-way attorney fee statute that once required insurers to pay a policyholder's attorney fees after a successful suit. For most current property policies, each side now generally bears its own attorney fees, subject to other fee rules such as offers of judgment. That is one reason contingency arrangements and early, well-documented claims matter more than they used to. Ask any lawyer you speak with how fees and costs work under the written fee agreement.

Common private flood denial reasons

  • Late notice under the policy or section 627.70132.
  • The water does not meet the policy's definition of flood, or a "flexible" policy limits coverage to the principal building.
  • Wind or wind-driven rain, which the flood insurer says belongs to the homeowners carrier. See wind vs. flood damage claims.
  • Failure to cooperate: missed examination under oath, missing records.
  • Exclusions modeled on the NFIP form, such as earth movement and mold from failure to dry the property.

Frequently asked questions

Do Florida's claim-handling laws apply to private flood insurance?

Generally yes. Private flood is property insurance regulated by the state, and statutes like 627.70132 (notice), 627.70131 (handling timelines) and 627.70152 (pre-suit notice) are written for property insurance policies, including surplus lines policies in several cases. The exact fit depends on the policy type, so have the policy reviewed.

Can I appeal a private flood denial to FEMA?

No. FEMA's appeal process under 44 CFR 62.20 is only for NFIP policies.

Is a private flood policy required to match the NFIP?

Only the "standard" type under section 627.715 must match NFIP coverage. Preferred and customized policies are broader; flexible policies can be narrower. Lenders separately decide whether a private policy satisfies a mortgage flood requirement under 42 U.S.C. 4012a(b).

How long do I have to give notice of a private flood claim?

Under section 627.70132, generally within 1 year after the date of loss for an initial or reopened claim and within 18 months for a supplemental claim, in addition to any "prompt notice" duty in the policy.
