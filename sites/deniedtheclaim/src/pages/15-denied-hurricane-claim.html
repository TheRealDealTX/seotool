Denied hurricane claim: how Florida storm denials happen and how to answer them

A denied hurricane claim usually rests on one of four arguments: the water was flood, the damage was already there, it is below the hurricane deductible, or you reported too late. Each has Florida-specific rules. Here they are, in the order homeowners tend to run into them.

Wind versus flood

A standard Florida homeowners policy covers windstorm (unless wind was excluded and written separately, as with some coastal policies) and excludes flood. Flood insurance is a separate policy, most often through the federal National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP). After a hurricane that brought both wind and surge, the homeowners insurer may attribute damage to flood, and the flood insurer may attribute it to wind.

The flood exclusion in many policies includes anti-concurrent causation language: if flood contributed to the loss “concurrently or in any sequence,” the loss is excluded. That language is not a blanket excuse. Damage that wind caused on its own — roof covering torn off, windows broken by debris, rain driven in through a wind-created opening — is a separate loss from water rising from outside. The evidence that separates them:

  • Water lines inside and outside the house, and whether damage sits above them.
  • Roof, gutter, soffit and window damage that flood water could not reach.
  • Timing: wind arrives before surge; neighbors, cameras and storm data can establish the sequence.
  • Ceiling stains and wet insulation, which point to water from above.

If you also have flood insurance

NFIP flood claims follow federal rules, not Florida’s. The Standard Flood Insurance Policy generally requires a signed proof of loss within 60 days after the loss unless FEMA extends it, and a lawsuit over a denied flood claim must generally be filed within one year after the date the insurer mailed the written denial (42 U.S.C. 4072). Those are much shorter than the Florida deadlines below. This site focuses on homeowners (non-flood) coverage; ask a lawyer about the flood side early.

“Pre-existing” and “prior storm” damage

Florida homes often go through several named storms in a few years. Insurers frequently deny a hurricane claim by saying the damage came from an earlier storm (sometimes one that was claimed with a different insurer) or from long-term deterioration. That turns on proof of the property’s condition just before the storm:

  • A recent wind-mitigation or four-point inspection, often done for an insurance application.
  • Photos from a real-estate sale, refinance appraisal or prior claim.
  • Roof replacement permits and invoices after an earlier storm.
  • Prior claim files showing the earlier damage was repaired.

When the denial rests on age and condition rather than another storm, see wear and tear denials.

The hurricane deductible

Florida residential policies usually carry a separate hurricane deductible, often a percentage of the dwelling limit. Under s. 627.701(3)(a), insurers must offer hurricane deductible options of $500 and 2%, 5% and 10% of the dwelling limit (with some exceptions by coverage amount). A few points that matter when the result is “below your deductible”:

  • It is annual. Under s. 627.701(5), the hurricane deductible applies on a calendar-year basis to all covered hurricane losses that year. If you already absorbed it for an earlier hurricane in the same year, the insurer generally applies the greater of the remaining hurricane deductible or the regular deductible to the next storm.
  • It applies only to hurricane losses. Reporting deadlines after a hurricane

For hurricane losses governed by the current version of s. 627.70132, the date of loss is the date the hurricane made landfall, and notice of a new or reopened claim must be given within 1 year after that date (18 months for a supplemental claim). Roof leaks from hurricane damage sometimes appear months later; report as soon as you see them. See late notice claim denials for how insurers argue prejudice from delay.

Roof claims after a hurricane

Roof claims are the core of most denied hurricane claims. Insurers often pay for a handful of shingles or tiles and deny the rest as age-related, or deny everything as below the deductible. Before accepting a spot repair, ask whether the remaining roofing can be matched and repaired to code, and get a licensed roofer’s written opinion. Florida’s roof rules have changed in recent years, and how they apply depends on your policy’s issue date, roof age and any roof payment schedule endorsement, so read those terms carefully.

What to do after a denied hurricane claim

  1. Confirm the date of loss the insurer used matches the storm that caused the damage.
  2. Get the estimate, photos and any engineer report (s. 627.70131(3)(e) requires the insurer to send any detailed estimate within 7 days of generating it).
  3. Separate wind damage from flood damage with photos and measurements.
  4. Check the deductible math, including whether it was already met this calendar year.
  5. Request reconsideration in writing with your evidence.
  6. Consider DFS mediation, appraisal or suit; see how to appeal a denied homeowners insurance claim in Florida.

FAQ: denied hurricane claim

Can I claim wind damage if my house also flooded?

Generally yes. Wind damage and flood damage are different losses covered by different policies. The homeowners insurer may still deny the parts it attributes to flood, so evidence separating the two matters.

Why was my hurricane claim denied as below the deductible?

Because the insurer valued the covered damage at less than your hurricane deductible, which is often a percentage of the dwelling limit. An independent, itemized estimate is the way to test that number.

Do I pay the hurricane deductible again for a second storm in the same year?

Generally not in full. Under s. 627.701(5), the hurricane deductible applies annually to all covered hurricane losses in a calendar year, with any remaining balance or the regular deductible (whichever is greater) applied to later storms.

How long do I have to report hurricane damage?

For current-law claims, 1 year from the hurricane’s landfall for a new or reopened claim and 18 months for a supplemental claim, plus any prompt-notice condition in your policy.
