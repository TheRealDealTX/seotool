How to appeal a denied homeowners insurance claim in Florida

Florida has no single “appeal” form for a denied homeowners claim. Instead there is a ladder of options, from a written reconsideration request to state mediation, appraisal, a Civil Remedy Notice and a lawsuit. This guide explains how to appeal a denied homeowners insurance claim in Florida by choosing the right rung.

The ladder at a glance

  1. Reconsideration — ask the insurer to reverse itself, with new evidence.
  2. DFS consumer assistance — a regulator asks the insurer to respond.
  3. DFS mediation — free, nonbinding settlement conference (s. 627.7015).
  4. Appraisal — binding valuation of the amount of loss under the policy.
  5. Neutral evaluation — for sinkhole claims only (s. 627.7074).
  6. Notice of intent to litigate — mandatory DFS filing before suit (s. 627.70152).
  7. Lawsuit — a court decides coverage and amount.
  8. Civil Remedy Notice — the step toward a statutory bad-faith claim (s. 624.155).

You do not have to climb every rung, and some rungs are not available for every denial. The biggest fork is whether the insurer said the loss is not covered (a coverage dispute) or the covered damage is worth less than you think (an amount dispute).

1. Reconsideration

Send a written request that names the claim number and the denial letter’s date, disputes the specific reason given, and attaches evidence: an independent estimate, a licensed professional’s cause opinion, pre-loss photos, weather data. Ask for a written response and for any report the denial relied on. Reconsideration costs nothing, but it does not pause any deadline.

2. DFS consumer assistance

The Florida Department of Financial Services’ Division of Consumer Services takes requests for assistance through its website and its helpline, 1-877-693-5236. DFS contacts the insurer and asks it to respond. It cannot order payment, but it creates a regulatory paper trail.

3. DFS mediation under s. 627.7015

Florida’s residential property mediation program is designed for exactly this situation: a homeowner and insurer who disagree about the cause of damage or the amount. Key points from the statute and DFS’s program description:

  • Who can use it: personal lines residential and commercial residential policyholders. Commercial (non-residential) coverages, auto and liability coverages are excluded.
  • Cost: the insurer pays the mediator’s fee (currently $350 per DFS). If you miss the conference, you pay to reschedule.
  • Eligibility limits: the dispute generally must be $500 or more after the deductible, and claims are excluded where the insurer has a reasonable basis to suspect fraud, where agreed facts show no coverage, where the insurer reasonably believes a material misrepresentation justifies a full denial, or where notice did not comply with s. 627.70132.
  • Timing: mediation is a pre-appraisal, pre-suit option. DFS generally schedules the conference within 21 days after assigning a mediator.
  • Nonbinding: nobody can force a settlement. If you sign one, you generally have 3 business days to rescind it, unless you have already cashed or deposited the settlement check.
  • Effect on appraisal: if either party rejects the mediation result, the policyholder generally is not required to complete contractual appraisal before suing for breach of contract.

Request it online through DFS, with form DFS-I0-2082, or through the helpline. If you hire a lawyer, DFS asks that you notify the mediator and the department at least 14 days before the conference.

4. Appraisal

Most Florida homeowners policies contain an appraisal clause: if you and the insurer disagree on the amount of loss, either side can demand appraisal. Each side picks an appraiser; the two pick an umpire; an award agreed by any two is generally binding as to the amount. Typically each side pays its own appraiser and splits the umpire and other costs, which can run into the thousands.

Appraisal is usually a poor fit for a full denial on coverage grounds, because appraisers value losses; they do not decide whether a loss is covered. Florida courts have allowed some causation questions to go to appraisal where the insurer admits there is a covered loss but disputes how much of the damage it caused. If the insurer demands appraisal after a partial denial, have someone review whether it narrows or helps your case before you agree.

5. Sinkhole neutral evaluation

For disputed sinkhole claims, s. 627.7074 provides a separate neutral evaluation process once a sinkhole report has been issued. Either the policyholder or the insurer may request it, and once requested it is mandatory for both. The insurer generally pays the reasonable cost. The evaluator addresses causation, the methods of stabilization and repair, and their cost; the process is nonbinding, and both sides keep access to the courts.

6. Notice of intent to initiate litigation

Before suing under a residential or commercial property policy, s. 627.70152 requires you to file a notice of intent to initiate litigation with DFS on its form at least 10 business days before filing suit. It cannot be filed before the insurer makes its coverage decision. After a denial, the notice includes an estimate of damages if known; for other disputes, it includes a pre-suit settlement demand and the disputed amount. The insurer has 10 business days to respond, and after a denial it may accept coverage, keep denying, or demand a reinspection that gives it 14 more business days. If you sue without proper notice, the court must dismiss the case without prejudice.

7. Lawsuit

The limitations period for breach of a property insurance contract is five years from the date of loss under s. 95.11(2)(e). In a coverage lawsuit, a court decides whether the loss is covered and, if needed, how much is owed. After the 2022 and 2023 reforms, a homeowner who wins generally cannot recover attorney fees from the insurer under the old one-way fee statute for most policies issued on or after December 16, 2022, and the offer-of-judgment statute can apply to these cases. Those changes are a big reason to get a lawyer’s view of the numbers before you file.

8. Civil Remedy Notice

A Civil Remedy Notice (CRN) under s. 624.155 is filed with DFS on its form and gives the insurer 60 days to cure an alleged violation, such as failing to attempt in good faith to settle a claim. It must identify the statute violated, the facts, the people involved and the relevant policy language. For residential property claims, a CRN generally cannot be filed within 60 days after appraisal is invoked. The statute now states that mere negligence alone is not bad faith, and that the policyholder’s own good-faith conduct may be considered. Bad-faith claims generally require the underlying coverage dispute to be resolved in the policyholder’s favor first, so a CRN is usually a parallel step, not a substitute for the contract claim.

FAQ: how to appeal a denied homeowners insurance claim in Florida

Is there a deadline to appeal a denied claim in Florida?

There is no single appeal deadline. The key outside limit for suing on the policy is generally five years from the date of loss (s. 95.11(2)(e)), but notice deadlines, policy conditions and pre-suit requirements can matter much sooner.

Does DFS mediation cost the homeowner anything?

Generally no. The insurer pays the mediator’s fee. You may have to pay if you miss the conference and need to reschedule.

Can the insurer force me into appraisal?

If your policy has an appraisal clause, either party can usually invoke it for a dispute about the amount of loss. Whether a particular dispute is appraisable, and whether mediation results affect it, depends on the facts and the policy.

Do I need a lawyer to file the pre-suit notice?

The law does not require one, but the notice has specific content requirements and is the doorway to a lawsuit, so most homeowners who reach that stage work with counsel.
