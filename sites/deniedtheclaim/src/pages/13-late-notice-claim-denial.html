Late notice claim denial: when the insurer says you waited too long

A late notice claim denial comes in two very different forms in Florida. One is a hard statutory deadline. The other is a policy condition that asks whether your delay actually hurt the insurer. Knowing which one you are facing is most of the battle.

The statutory deadlines in s. 627.70132

For losses governed by the current version of s. 627.70132, a property insurance claim is barred unless notice was given to the insurer in accordance with the policy terms:

  • within 1 year after the date of loss for a new claim or a reopened claim; and
  • within 18 months after the date of loss for a supplemental claim — additional damage from the same peril the insurer already adjusted, or costs incurred during repairs under an open, timely claim.

For a hurricane, tornado, windstorm, severe rain or other weather-related event, the statute fixes the date of loss: for a hurricane it is the date the hurricane made landfall; for other weather events, the date the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration verifies the event. The deadlines are paused during a servicemember’s qualifying deployment, and condominium loss-assessment claims have their own rule.

Older losses

These windows have been shortened more than once. Earlier versions of the statute allowed 2 years for initial and reopened claims and 3 years for supplemental claims, and the rules before 2021 were different again. If your loss is older than December 2022, which version applies to you is a legal question worth checking.

The “prompt notice” condition and prejudice

Even inside the statutory window, most policies require “prompt” notice of a loss. Insurers deny claims reported months after a storm on the theory that the delay made it impossible to tell what caused the damage or how much was there originally.

Florida courts have long treated late notice as a two-step question. First, was notice actually late, given when you knew or reasonably should have known about the damage? Second, if it was late, the insurer is generally presumed to have been prejudiced — but the policyholder can rebut that presumption with evidence that the insurer could still investigate and determine the cause and extent of the loss. How strong the presumption is, and what evidence overcomes it, can vary by court and by facts, so this is a place where a lawyer’s read matters.

Facts that often matter

  • When you discovered the damage. Hidden roof damage that shows up as a ceiling stain months later is different from a missing section of roof you saw the next morning.
  • What changed in between. If the damage is still there, unrepaired and photographed, the insurer can still inspect it.
  • Contemporaneous proof. Dated photos, texts to a roofer, a neighbor’s claim from the same storm, weather data.
  • Repairs you made. Temporary repairs to prevent further damage are expected; extensive permanent repairs before notice make the insurer’s prejudice argument stronger.

How to answer a late notice claim denial

  1. Pin down the dates. Date of loss (for a hurricane, landfall), date you discovered damage, date and method of first notice. Find the claim confirmation email or call log.
  2. Check the date of loss the insurer used. Insurers sometimes tie a claim to an earlier storm than the one that caused the damage, which makes notice look late.
  3. Explain the delay in writing if there was one, with evidence: why you did not see the damage, when you did, and what you did next.
  4. Show the insurer can still investigate. Offer access, provide photos and the preserved materials, point to weather records.
  5. Do not sit on it. If more damage appears, give notice of a supplemental claim promptly; the 18-month window runs from the original date of loss.

Which dispute path fits a late notice denial

A late-notice denial is usually a coverage defense, so the main battleground is a lawsuit (after the DFS pre-suit notice) or, for eligible residential disputes, DFS mediation. Note that DFS mediation excludes losses that do not comply with s. 627.70132, so a claim that is truly outside the statutory deadline is not eligible. Appraisal sets the amount of loss and generally is not the forum to decide whether notice was late. Read the comparison in how to appeal a denied homeowners insurance claim in Florida.

FAQ: late notice claim denial

How long do I have to report a hurricane claim in Florida?

Under the current s. 627.70132, generally 1 year after the date the hurricane made landfall for a new or reopened claim, and 18 months for a supplemental claim. Your policy may also require prompt notice, so report as soon as you know about damage.

Is a claim automatically denied if I report it late?

Not automatically, if you are still within the statutory window. Late notice under the policy usually raises a presumption that the insurer was prejudiced, which you can try to rebut. Outside the statutory window, the claim is generally barred.

What counts as giving notice?

Notice must be given to the insurer in accordance with the policy terms. Reporting through the insurer’s claims line, app or portal, or in writing as the policy specifies, and keeping the confirmation, is the safest approach. Telling a contractor or your agent may not be enough under some policies.

Does the lawsuit deadline run from the denial?

For breach of a property insurance contract, s. 95.11(2)(e) runs five years from the date of loss, not from the denial. Flood claims under the federal NFIP program follow different rules.
