Wear and tear denial: when “it’s just old” is not the whole story

A wear and tear denial is the most common answer Florida homeowners get on roof and plumbing claims. The exclusion is real, but it is narrower than many letters make it sound. This guide explains what it actually excludes and how homeowners show that something sudden happened.

What the wear and tear exclusion excludes

Most Florida homeowners policies cover “direct physical loss” to the dwelling and then list exclusions. One of them typically reads something like “wear and tear, marring, deterioration” and often sits next to “mechanical breakdown, latent defect, inherent vice” and “faulty, inadequate or defective maintenance.” Insurance is meant for sudden, accidental losses; the gradual aging of a roof or a water heater is an ordinary cost of owning a house.

The key word is excludes. An exclusion takes away coverage for what it describes. It does not, by itself, take away coverage for a different, sudden event that happened to an older part of the house. A 15-year-old roof can be worn and wind-damaged. The question is which caused the damage you are claiming.

Three patterns behind wear and tear denials

1. The roof is “at the end of its life”

The adjuster sees granule loss, curling or cracked shingles and concludes the roof failed from age. What matters is whether there is also evidence of a wind event: creased or lifted tabs, missing shingles in a pattern consistent with wind direction, debris impact, damage concentrated on the windward slopes, and leaks that began right after a known storm date.

2. The pipe “deteriorated”

A cast-iron drain or old supply line fails and the insurer denies for deterioration. Many policies exclude the cost to repair the worn pipe itself but still cover ensuing water damage from a sudden and accidental discharge — and some cover the cost to tear out and replace part of the structure to reach the leak. Read the exclusion paragraph and the water-damage coverage terms together. See denied water damage claims.

3. “Lack of maintenance”

Here the insurer says the damage happened because something was not maintained: caulking, flashing, a sealant joint. Maintenance records, invoices and photos of the condition before the loss are the direct answer.

When the denial relies on an engineer

Insurers commonly hire engineering firms to write cause-and-origin reports. Ask for the report: Florida law requires the insurer to send you any detailed estimate within 7 days (s. 627.70131(3)(e)), and while engineer reports are handled differently, insurers often share the report a denial relied on when asked. Read it for:

  • How long the engineer was at the property and whether they went on the roof or into the attic.
  • Whether they tested anything (e.g. lifted shingles to check the seal strip) or only looked.
  • Whether they considered the storm data for your date of loss.
  • Conclusions phrased as possibilities rather than findings.

A short written response from your own licensed professional that addresses the report point by point is far more persuasive than a general disagreement.

Florida roof rules that sometimes come up

Florida’s recent reforms changed several roof-related rules — including how insurers may write coverage for older roofs and how roof claims are settled under some policies. Those changes depend on your policy form, its issue date and the age and type of roof, so read your policy’s roof endorsements (for example, any roof-surface payment schedule) before assuming either side is right. If a payment schedule reduced the payment rather than denying the claim outright, that is an amount dispute, not a wear and tear denial.

What to do next

  1. Get the full policy and read the exclusion paragraph, including any ensuing-loss language.
  2. Ask for the inspection photos, estimate and any engineer report.
  3. Gather the evidence in the table above.
  4. Send a written reconsideration request with your evidence attached.
  5. If the insurer will not move, compare mediation, appraisal and suit. Note that when the dispute is whether the cause is covered at all, appraisal may not be available.

FAQ: wear and tear denial

Can an old roof still be covered after a storm?

Yes, if the storm caused the damage being claimed. Age makes the insurer more likely to argue wear and tear, and some policies settle older roofs differently, but age alone does not exclude wind damage under a typical policy.

Is mechanical breakdown the same as wear and tear?

They are usually separate exclusions in the same list. Mechanical breakdown targets appliances and systems that fail on their own. Like wear and tear, it generally excludes the failed item, not necessarily the damage a sudden discharge causes elsewhere.

Do I need an engineer to fight a wear and tear denial?

Not always. A licensed roofer’s or plumber’s written opinion with photos may be enough for reconsideration or mediation. When the insurer relies on an engineer’s report, a comparable professional opinion on your side usually carries more weight.

Should I replace the roof before the dispute is resolved?

Make temporary repairs to prevent further damage. Before any permanent replacement, document the roof thoroughly and give the insurer a reasonable chance to reinspect, or you risk losing the evidence.
