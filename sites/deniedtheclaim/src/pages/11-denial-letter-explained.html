Denial letter explained: what your insurer actually said

Property insurance denial letters are written by people who draft them every day for readers who see one maybe once in their lives. This page has the denial letter explained part by part, with the phrases Florida insurers use most and what each one means for your next move.

What Florida requires the letter to say

Under s. 627.70131(7)(a), Florida Statutes, when a property insurer denies a claim it must provide a reasonable explanation in writing of the basis in the insurance policy for that decision. If it pays less than a detailed estimate it generated, it must also explain the difference in writing. In practice, a compliant letter should let you answer three questions without guessing:

  1. What did they decide? Full denial, partial denial, payment of some items only, or a finding that the covered damage falls below your deductible.
  2. Why? A factual reason — “the inspection found…” — tied to a cause of loss.
  3. Under which policy language? A quotation or citation of the specific coverage grant, exclusion or condition.

A letter that says only “your claim is not covered under the terms of your policy” does not tell you which terms. Ask, in writing, for the specific provision and the facts the insurer relied on.

Common phrases, translated

“Reservation of rights”

This is not a denial. It means the insurer is still investigating, or is paying something, while keeping the option to deny coverage later. It is a signal to document carefully, because the insurer is building a file.

“Wear and tear, marring, deterioration”

The standard homeowners exclusion for gradual aging. It excludes the worn-out condition itself; it does not automatically exclude a sudden event that happened to an older component. See fighting a wear and tear denial.

“Constant or repeated seepage or leakage of water over a period of 14 days or more”

Many Florida policies exclude water damage from a leak that continued for 14 days or more. The question becomes how long the leak actually ran, which is often an engineering judgment the insurer made from a single visit. See denied water damage claims.

“Prejudiced by late reporting”

The insurer is saying you reported too late and the delay kept it from investigating. Florida courts have generally treated late notice as creating a presumption of prejudice to the insurer that the policyholder can rebut with evidence. See late notice claim denials.

“Regardless of any other cause or event contributing concurrently or in any sequence”

This is anti-concurrent causation (ACC) language, common in flood and earth-movement exclusions. If an excluded cause (like flood) combined with a covered one (like wind), the clause tries to exclude the whole loss. Whether it applies depends on the exact wording and the evidence about what caused which damage, a frequent fight after hurricanes.

“Ensuing loss”

Many exclusions say that if an excluded event leads to a covered peril, the resulting (ensuing) damage is covered. For example, a policy may exclude the cost of fixing a worn pipe but still cover water damage that results from a sudden break. Look for the word “ensuing” or “resulting” in the exclusion the letter quotes.

“Below your deductible”

The insurer agrees some damage is covered but values it at less than the deductible, so it pays nothing. This is a dispute about scope and price, not about coverage, which is why appraisal or mediation may fit. For hurricane losses, also check that the hurricane deductible (often a percentage of dwelling coverage) was applied only once in the calendar year as s. 627.701 requires.

“Material misrepresentation” or “concealment or fraud”

The insurer is accusing you of a false statement, either in the policy application or during the claim. Under s. 627.409 a misstatement in an application prevents recovery only if it was fraudulent, material to the risk, or would have changed the insurer’s decision to issue the policy or its terms. Do not give further statements without advice.

“Examination under oath”

A sworn, transcribed interview that many policies allow the insurer to require. You generally may bring a lawyer. An EUO request after a denial, or alongside one, usually means the insurer is looking at fraud or misrepresentation issues.

“This letter does not waive any rights”

Boilerplate. It means the insurer may later rely on other policy defenses besides the one in the letter. It does not stop you from disputing the decision.

A 10-minute checklist for your own letter

  • Circle the date of loss and the date of the letter.
  • Highlight every policy provision quoted, and find each in your full policy.
  • Read the whole exclusion paragraph, including its exceptions.
  • Note who inspected and when; ask for their report.
  • Mark whether this is a full or partial denial.
  • If money was paid, check the deductible math and the depreciation (if any) withheld.
  • Note any deadline the letter mentions and any offer of mediation.
  • Write down what evidence would answer the stated reason.

Keep the original

Scan the letter, keep the original, and do not write on it. Mark up a copy. If the dispute ends up in mediation, appraisal or court, the clean original is the exhibit.

How to respond to the letter

A good response is short. Reference the claim number and the letter’s date, identify the reason you dispute, attach evidence, and ask for three things: reconsideration, a copy of any report or estimate the decision relied on, and the specific policy provisions if the letter did not name them. Send it in a way that leaves a record. Then calendar your deadlines with the worksheet on the nine-step plan, and compare your formal options in how to appeal a denied homeowners insurance claim in Florida.

FAQ: denial letter explained

Is a reservation of rights letter the same as a denial?

No. A reservation of rights letter says the insurer is still investigating or paying while keeping its right to deny coverage later. A denial is a decision. Treat both seriously and keep documenting.

What if the denial letter does not cite any policy language?

Florida law requires a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy for a denial (s. 627.70131(7)(a)). Ask in writing for the specific provisions and the facts relied on, and keep a copy of the request.

Can the insurer change its reason for denial later?

Insurers often reserve the right to raise other defenses, and whether they can depends on the facts and the law that applies. Answer the reason given, but prepare for the insurer to add others.

Does a partial denial mean I have to accept the payment?

No. You can generally deposit an undisputed payment and still dispute the rest, but read anything you are asked to sign. Avoid signing a release that says the payment is full and final unless you mean it.
