Denied water damage claim: sudden leak or “long-term seepage”?

Water claims are among the most frequently disputed homeowners claims in Florida, and a denied water damage claim almost always comes down to one question: did the water arrive suddenly, or over a long time? This guide explains how policies draw that line and how to show which side of it you are on.

Sudden and accidental versus repeated seepage

Homeowners policies generally cover an “accidental discharge or overflow of water” from a plumbing, heating or air-conditioning system or household appliance. They also generally exclude “constant or repeated seepage or leakage of water” over a period of time — in many Florida policies, 14 days or more. A burst supply line under the sink is the first kind. A slow drip behind a shower wall for months is the second.

The trouble is that most real leaks are discovered after they have been running for some unknown time. The insurer’s inspector looks at staining, rot, rust or mold and estimates duration; a denial follows. That estimate is an opinion, and it can be tested.

Plumbing leaks

For a plumbing claim, the strongest evidence is usually physical and financial. Keep the failed fitting, hose or pipe section. Pull 12 months of water bills; a sudden spike can date the failure. Ask the plumber who fixed it to write down what failed and how (a split, a cracked fitting, a corroded-through pipe). Many policies exclude the cost to repair the pipe or appliance itself but cover the resulting water damage and, in some forms, the cost to tear out and replace the part of the structure needed to reach the leak. Read the coverage and exclusion paragraphs together, and check for any water-damage limitation endorsement on your policy.

Roof leaks

Interior rain damage is often covered only when wind or hail first created an opening in the roof or walls through which rain entered. A roof leak with no storm-created opening is likely to be denied as wear and tear. A licensed roofer’s written report documenting lifted, creased or missing roofing, matched to a storm date, is the key piece. After a hurricane, see also denied hurricane claims.

Mold

Many Florida policies exclude mold, or cover it only up to a modest sublimit, and often only when it results from a covered water loss. If your claim was denied because of mold, check whether the underlying water event is covered: the water damage itself may be payable even if mold remediation is capped. Do not delay drying and remediation while you argue; mold that grows after you knew about the leak can become its own problem.

An evidence kit for a denied water damage claim

  • Photos and video of the source, the damage and the water before cleanup.
  • The failed part, bagged and labeled.
  • Plumber, roofer or leak-detection report stating cause and likely duration.
  • Water bills for the past year.
  • Moisture-mapping or drying logs from the mitigation company.
  • Receipts for emergency shutoff, drying and temporary repairs.
  • Your timeline: when you first saw signs, when you called whom.

Mitigation invoices

Water mitigation companies sometimes ask homeowners to sign an assignment of benefits. For residential and commercial property policies issued on or after January 1, 2023, Florida law generally makes post-loss assignments of benefits invalid (s. 627.7152). Read what you sign, and pay attention to who is billing the insurer.

Next steps

Ask the insurer for its estimate, photos and any leak-duration or engineer opinion; send a written reconsideration request with your evidence kit; and calendar your deadlines. FAQ: denied water damage claim

What does “repeated seepage over 14 days” mean?

It is a common exclusion for water that leaked continuously or repeatedly for 14 days or more. The insurer has to rely on evidence about how long the leak ran; your own plumber’s opinion, water bills and the failed part can contradict its estimate.

Is a slab leak covered in Florida?

It depends on the policy. Many cover sudden discharge from plumbing and the cost to access it under certain conditions, while excluding the pipe repair and long-term seepage. Read the water damage and tear-out terms of your policy.

My insurer denied my claim because of mold. Can I still recover?

Possibly, if the mold resulted from a covered water event. The water damage may be covered even if mold remediation is excluded or capped by a sublimit.

Should I let the mitigation company deal with my insurer?

You can share their documentation, but keep control of the claim. For policies issued on or after January 1, 2023, post-loss assignments of benefits are generally invalid under s. 627.7152.
