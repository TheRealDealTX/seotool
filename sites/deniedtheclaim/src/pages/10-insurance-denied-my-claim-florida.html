Insurance denied my claim in Florida. What are my rights now?

If you are typing “insurance denied my claim Florida” into a search bar, you probably have a letter in one hand and a damaged house behind you. Here is what Florida law already guarantees you, what to do in the first month, and the mistakes that make a denial harder to undo.

The rights you already have

Florida regulates how property insurers handle claims mostly through section 627.70131 of the Florida Statutes, and it requires insurers to give residential policyholders a summary of these rules — the Homeowner Claims Bill of Rights in s. 627.7142 — within 14 days after you first report a claim. If you never read that notice, the key points are below. They apply to most personal residential policies; commercial policies follow some but not all of them.

RightWhat it means for youSource
AcknowledgmentThe insurer must review and acknowledge your claim communications within 7 calendar days.s. 627.70131(1)(a)
InspectionIf the insurer physically inspects, it generally must do so within 30 days after receiving proof-of-loss statements.s. 627.70131(3)(b)
Estimate copyIf the insurer generates a detailed estimate of the loss, it must send you a copy within 7 days.s. 627.70131(3)(e)
DecisionPay or deny within 60 days after notice of the claim, in most cases, with interest if payment is late.s. 627.70131(7)(a)
Written reasonA denial must come with a reasonable written explanation of the basis in the policy.s. 627.70131(7)(a)
Adjuster listThe insurer must keep a log of each adjuster who communicated with you and provide it on request.s. 627.70131(4)(a)
Free mediationMost residential disputes qualify for state-run mediation; the insurer pays the mediator.s. 627.7015

These rights do not decide whether your loss is covered. They decide how the insurer has to treat you while it makes that decision — and a violation can matter later, both in negotiation and in any bad-faith claim.

Week one: collect, do not argue

The instinct after a denial is to call the adjuster and push back. That call rarely changes anything, and it can produce a note in the claim file you never get to see. Use the first week to gather documents instead.

  1. Save the denial letter and the envelope or email. The date it was sent can matter for later deadlines.
  2. Request the full certified policy in force on the date of loss, including every endorsement.
  3. Request the estimate, photos and reports the insurer relied on, and the list of adjusters assigned to your claim.
  4. Write down your timeline: when the damage happened, when you noticed it, when and how you reported it, every inspection and every call. Dates are the backbone of most disputes.
  5. Keep mitigating. Tarp the roof, dry the floor, shut off the water. Florida policies generally require reasonable steps to protect the property from further damage, and a denial does not suspend that.

Weeks two to four: build your side of the file

The insurer’s file has an adjuster’s estimate and often an engineer’s opinion. Yours should have something comparable. Depending on the reason for denial, that could be:

  • A licensed contractor’s written estimate with line items, not a one-line bid.
  • A written opinion on cause from a licensed roofer, plumber, leak-detection company or engineer.
  • Older photos (real-estate listing photos, a home inspection from your purchase, a wind-mitigation or four-point inspection) showing the property’s condition before the loss.
  • Weather data for the date of loss, such as National Weather Service storm reports.
  • Repair and permit records showing the roof, plumbing or other system had been maintained.

Then send the insurer a written request for reconsideration with those attachments. Name the reason in the letter you are disputing and explain, briefly, why your evidence answers it. Ask for a written response. If the denial was based on age or maintenance, the guide to wear and tear denials lists the evidence that usually matters; if it was based on timing, see late notice claim denials.

Calling the Florida Department of Financial Services

The Department of Financial Services (DFS), through its Division of Consumer Services, runs the state’s insurance consumer helpline at 1-877-693-5236 (1-877-MY-FL-CFO). DFS can do two useful things for a homeowner whose claim was denied:

  • Request Assistance: you can file a consumer request, and DFS will contact the insurer and ask it to respond. DFS is not your lawyer and cannot order the insurer to pay, but a written regulatory inquiry often produces a clearer explanation, and sometimes a second look.
  • Mediation: under s. 627.7015 you can request free, nonbinding mediation of most residential claim disputes. The insurer pays the mediator. If you settle, you generally have 3 business days to change your mind, as long as you have not cashed the check. The appeal options guide explains eligibility.

Five mistakes that make a denial harder to undo

  1. Throwing away the damaged materials. Shingles, the failed supply line, the soaked pad: these are evidence of cause. Photograph them and keep samples if you can.
  2. Signing a release with a check. A partial payment is not a problem by itself, but read anything you sign. A “full and final” release can end the claim.
  3. Guessing in a recorded statement. If you do not know when a leak started, say so. A guess that turns out to be wrong can be recast as a misrepresentation.
  4. Letting the notice clock run. Florida bars a new or reopened claim unless notice was given within 1 year after the date of loss, and a supplemental claim within 18 months (s. 627.70132), for losses governed by the current statute.
  5. Filing suit without pre-suit notice. A lawsuit under a residential or commercial property policy must be preceded by a notice of intent to initiate litigation filed with DFS at least 10 business days earlier (s. 627.70152). Skip it and the court must dismiss the case.

FAQ: insurance denied my claim in Florida

Can I reopen a claim my insurer already denied?

You can ask the insurer to reconsider at any time and send new evidence. Formally reopening a claim is subject to the same notice deadline as a new claim under s. 627.70132 — generally 1 year after the date of loss for current-law claims.

Will filing a DFS complaint hurt my claim?

No. Asking the state regulator for assistance is a normal consumer right. It does not stop any deadline from running, though, so keep calendaring your notice and limitation dates.

My insurer says it never got my documents. What should I do?

Resend them in a way that leaves a record — through the insurer’s portal with a screenshot, by email, or by certified mail — and keep a log. Insurers must acknowledge claim communications within 7 calendar days under s. 627.70131(1)(a).

Does Citizens Property Insurance follow the same rules?

Citizens is a Florida insurer subject to the claim-handling statutes discussed here, though some procedures and policy forms differ from private carriers. Read your Citizens policy for its specific conditions.
