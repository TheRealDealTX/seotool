The first 72 hours after the storm

  1. Stay safe. Wait for officials to say it is safe to return. Avoid downed lines and floodwater.
  2. Document before you touch anything. Take wide shots of each room and the exterior, then close-ups of every damaged item, water lines on walls, the roof from the ground, fallen trees and fences. Narrate a video with the date.
  3. Prevent further damage. Your policy requires “reasonable emergency measures” — tarp the roof, board broken windows, pull wet carpet and pad, run fans. Keep every receipt; these costs are usually reimbursable.
  4. Report the claim. Call or file online with your homeowners/wind insurer and, separately, your flood insurer if water came in from outside. Write down the claim numbers and the name of everyone you speak with.
  5. Start a damage log. Our free storm damage documentation tool keeps a room-by-room list you can print or export.

Understanding your hurricane deductible

Florida homeowners policies include a separate hurricane deductible that applies to damage from a named hurricane. It is usually a percentage of your dwelling coverage (Coverage A) — commonly 2%, sometimes 5% or 10% — rather than a flat dollar amount.

Dwelling coverage2% deductible5% deductible10% deductible
$250,000$5,000$12,500$25,000
$350,000$7,000$17,500$35,000
$500,000$10,000$25,000$50,000

Under Florida law the hurricane deductible applies once per calendar year: if a second hurricane strikes the same year, the remaining amount of the deductible (or your regular all-other-perils deductible, whichever is greater) applies. Run your own numbers with the hurricane deductible calculator.

Roof damage claims

Roofs are the most common — and most disputed — part of a Pensacola hurricane claim. Missing shingles, lifted tabs, creased shingles, damaged flashing and interior leaks are all signs of wind damage. Things to know:

  • Florida's building code generally requires that when more than 25% of a roof section is repaired or replaced in a 12-month period, the whole section be brought up to current code. Newer roofs (built to the 2007 code or later) are often exempt; adjusters and contractors may disagree on how this applies.
  • Insurers may pay roofs on an actual cash value basis (with depreciation) for older roofs, depending on your policy's roof-payment schedule. Read the endorsements on your declarations page.
  • Get a written estimate from a licensed Florida roofing contractor and keep photos of the roof before tarping.
  • Be wary of anyone who knocks on your door offering a “free roof.” Florida restricts contractors from soliciting insurance claims and from offering to waive deductibles.

Wind vs. flood: the most expensive distinction

A homeowners or wind policy covers wind and wind-driven rain entering through openings the wind created. It does not cover flood or storm surge. Flood damage is paid only by an NFIP or private flood policy. After a surge event like Ivan or Sally, insurers may attribute damage to flood (excluded) rather than wind (covered). Photos and timelines matter:

  • Photograph water lines on walls, and roof and window damage, separately.
  • Note when wind damage occurred versus when water rose, if you were present.
  • File with both insurers when you have both kinds of damage.

Water and leak damage claims

Wind-driven rain through a damaged roof, window or wall is typically covered wind damage. Insurers often try to recharacterize it as a long-term leak, wear and tear, or excluded mold. Strong water claims show how water got in: missing shingles or flashing directly above the stain, broken window seals, photos taken right after the storm, and moisture readings. Dry the property quickly and keep the drying company's logs.

Supplemental and reopened claims

Hurricane damage is often discovered weeks or months later — a ceiling stain after the next rain, cracked roof tiles, or a contractor's estimate far above the insurer's. Florida generally allows a supplemental or reopened claim within 18 months of the date of loss. Many underpaid Hurricane Sally and Isaias claims are fixed through properly documented supplements.

Condo, HOA and commercial claims

Pensacola Beach and Perdido Key condo owners often deal with two policies: the association's master policy for the building and the owner's HO-6 policy for interiors and assessments. Associations, landlords and businesses face larger losses, business-interruption questions and coinsurance disputes. The Lawgical Firm handles residential and commercial property claims.

Additional living expenses (ALE)

If your home is unlivable, Coverage D (Loss of Use) pays reasonable extra costs above your normal spending — hotel or rental, extra mileage, meals beyond your usual grocery bill. Keep every receipt. Renters policies include this too.

Free help with a hurricane claim

  • Florida Department of Financial Services — the Division of Consumer Services helps with insurer disputes and runs a free mediation program for property claims. Helpline: 1-877-693-5236. myfloridacfo.com
  • Appraisal — many policies let either side demand appraisal when you agree damage is covered but disagree on the amount.
  • NFIP appeals — flood claim denials can be appealed to FEMA, generally within 60 days of the written denial.
