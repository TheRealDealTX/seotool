Why Pensacola watches the Gulf so closely

Pensacola hurricane risk, explained

Pensacola sits on the northern rim of the Gulf, where storms that form in the Caribbean or the Gulf itself often curve north and slow down near the coast. That geography makes a Pensacola hurricane different from a South Florida storm in three ways:

  • Storm surge funnels into the bays. Pensacola Bay, Escambia Bay, East Bay, Santa Rosa Sound and Big Lagoon all act like funnels when a storm makes landfall to the west. That is why Ivan, which came ashore near Gulf Shores, Alabama, still flooded downtown Pensacola and wrecked the I-10 bridge.
  • Slow storms bring extreme rain. Hurricane Sally crawled ashore at about 3 mph in 2020 and dropped two feet of rain or more on parts of Escambia and Santa Rosa counties, flooding neighborhoods that had never flooded from surge.
  • The barrier islands go first. Pensacola Beach, Perdido Key and Navarre Beach are reached only by bridges, which close when sustained winds reach roughly 40 mph. Leaving early is the only safe plan.

The Pensacola Hurricane timeline is long: Spanish explorer Tristán de Luna's settlement was destroyed by a hurricane in 1559, only weeks after it was founded, and major storms have struck the area roughly every decade since. Before, during and after a Pensacola hurricane

Before the storm

Know your evacuation zone, build a supply kit for at least seven days, photograph your home and belongings, and read your insurance declarations page so you know your hurricane deductible. The Pensacola Hurricane checklist generator and calculators do the math for you.

When a watch or warning is issued

A hurricane watch means hurricane conditions are possible within about 48 hours; a warning means they are expected within about 36 hours. When Escambia or Santa Rosa County issues an evacuation order for your zone, leave — and leave early enough to cross the bridges and get north of I-10 before tropical-storm-force winds arrive.

After the storm

Stay out of floodwater, watch for downed lines, and never run a generator indoors. Document all damage before you clean up, make temporary repairs to prevent further damage, and report your claim promptly. The Pensacola Hurricane claims center explains Florida's claim deadlines and what to do if an insurer delays, underpays or denies a hurricane claim.

Hurricane insurance claims in Florida

Most Pensacola homeowners carry a percentage hurricane deductible — often 2% of the dwelling limit, sometimes 5% or more — and wind and flood are covered by different policies. Florida law also sets tight deadlines: a new hurricane claim generally must be reported within one year of the date of loss, and a supplemental or reopened claim within 18 months.

FAQ

Pensacola Hurricane questions

When is hurricane season in Pensacola?

The Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30. Pensacola's highest risk is mid-August through mid-October; Ivan, Opal, Sally and the 1926 storm all struck in September or early October.

What was the worst hurricane to hit Pensacola?

Hurricane Ivan (September 16, 2004) is the most destructive modern storm for Pensacola. It made landfall near Gulf Shores as a Category 3, pushed a 10–15 foot storm surge into Pensacola Bay and Escambia Bay, knocked spans of the I-10 Escambia Bay Bridge into the water and damaged or destroyed tens of thousands of homes.

How do I find my evacuation zone in Pensacola?

Enter your street address in the Pensacola Hurricane Evacuation Zone Checker. It geocodes the address and checks it against Escambia County and Santa Rosa County evacuation zone maps (Zones A–E), then lists the nearest designated shelter sites.

Does homeowners insurance cover hurricane flooding in Florida?

No. Standard homeowners policies cover wind damage subject to a separate hurricane deductible, but flood and storm-surge damage require a separate NFIP or private flood policy, which usually has a 30-day waiting period. See the flood risk center.
