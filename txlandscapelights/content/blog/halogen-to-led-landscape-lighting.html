If your yard still runs on halogen, you already know the routine: bulbs that burn out every season or two, fixtures that run hot enough to scorch nearby leaves, and an electric bill that creeps up every summer. Moving from halogen to LED landscape lighting solves all three, and it is one of the highest-return upgrades you can make to an existing system.

But "just swap the bulbs" is not always the right answer. This guide covers what changes, what can stay, and how to avoid the flicker and color mismatch that make some LED conversions disappointing.

Why switch from halogen to LED landscape lighting

Energy use

A typical halogen MR16 landscape lamp draws 35 to 50 watts. An LED lamp with comparable brightness usually draws 4 to 7 watts. Multiply that difference by every fixture in the yard and every night of the year, and the savings add up quickly.

Lamp life

Halogen lamps are commonly rated somewhere around 2,000 to 4,000 hours. Quality LED lamps and integrated LED fixtures are commonly rated for 25,000 hours or more. At six hours a night, that is the difference between replacing lamps every year or two and going many years without touching them.

Heat and load

Halogen lamps run very hot, which bakes gaskets, fades lenses and can damage nearby plants. LEDs run much cooler. The lower load also frees up transformer capacity, so the same transformer can often power more fixtures after a conversion.

Retrofit lamps vs full fixture replacement

There are two ways to convert, and the right one depends on the condition of what you already own.

Retrofit LED lamps

Many halogen fixtures use standard bi-pin MR16 (GU5.3) or PAR36 lamps. Drop-in LED replacements exist for both. Retrofitting makes sense when:

  • Fixtures are solid brass, copper or quality aluminum in good shape
  • Lenses, gaskets and sockets are intact and not corroded
  • The fixture has enough interior room for the LED lamp's heat sink
  • You are happy with current fixture placement

New integrated LED fixtures

Integrated fixtures have the LED and driver built in and sealed. Full replacement is the better call when:

  • Fixtures are cracked, rusted or have melted sockets from halogen heat
  • Lenses are clouded or the housing leaks water
  • The original layout has dark gaps or glare problems
  • You want adjustable beam spreads, dimming or smart color control

Watch out: Cheap retrofit lamps are the most common source of early LED failures. A lamp that overheats inside a sealed fixture or lacks a quality driver can dim, shift color or die within a couple of seasons, especially in a Texas summer.

Transformer compatibility: magnetic vs electronic

The transformer is where many DIY conversions go sideways. Before you swap a single lamp, find out which type you have.

Magnetic transformers

Most professional landscape transformers use a heavy magnetic (toroidal or core-and-coil) design. These generally work well with LED loads. One thing to watch: magnetic transformers can deliver slightly higher voltage when lightly loaded, and a halogen system converted to LED might drop from 600 watts to under 100. Measure voltage at the fixtures after the conversion and move to a lower tap if any run sits above the lamp's rated range.

Electronic transformers

Some lighter-duty and older kit transformers are electronic. Many electronic transformers have a minimum load requirement, and when a low-wattage LED load falls below it, the output can flicker, cycle on and off, or shut down entirely. If you have an electronic transformer, look for LED lamps specifically rated for it, or plan to replace the transformer as part of the upgrade.

Right-sizing after the switch

Converting often leaves a big transformer lightly loaded. That is not a problem with a quality magnetic unit, and the spare capacity is useful if you plan to add fixtures. Matching color temperature

Halogen light is warm, typically around 2800K to 3000K, with excellent color rendering. Early LEDs had a reputation for cold, bluish light, but today's warm-white options match halogen closely if you choose carefully.

  • Choose 2700K for a warm, traditional look on brick, stone and stucco, or 3000K for a slightly crisper white.
  • Look for a CRI of 90 or higher so foliage, stone and brick colors look natural.
  • Buy every lamp for a given area from the same product line so colors stay consistent.
  • Convert a whole zone at once. Mixing halogen and LED side by side makes the difference obvious.
  • Match beam spreads to the original lamps, or adjust aim if the new beam is narrower.

Common halogen to LED landscape lighting mistakes

Most disappointing conversions trace back to the same handful of shortcuts. Avoid these and your new LED system should look better than the old halogen one ever did.

Matching watts instead of light output

A 35-watt halogen lamp and a 35-watt LED are not equivalent. Compare lumens and beam angle, not wattage. Many LED packages list a "halogen equivalent" figure, which is a useful starting point, but real-world brightness varies by brand. Test one lamp before buying a case.

Ignoring the beam spread

Halogen MR16 lamps come in familiar beam codes such as narrow spot and wide flood. LED replacements often have different beam shapes, and a lamp that is too narrow can turn a softly washed column into a hard stripe. Match the beam to the job: narrow for tall palms and columns, wide for broad facades and shrub masses.

Leaving old wiring problems in place

Halogen systems drew heavy current, so years of operation may have cooked splices and corroded connections. The lower LED load hides some of that damage, but bad connections still fail. A conversion is the right time to cut out and reseal old splices.

Forgetting the timer

Many older halogen systems still run on mechanical timers that need resetting after every daylight saving change. Pair the LED conversion with an astronomical timer or smart controller so the new system runs on schedule without attention.
