Landscape lighting transformer sizing is the step most DIY systems get wrong, and it is the reason so many yards have one bright corner and a row of tired, yellowed path lights at the far end. The good news: the math is simple once you know the three things that matter. How much load you have, how far that load sits from the transformer, and how heavy the wire is between them.

This guide walks through each one, then puts it together in a worked example you can adapt for your own yard. If you just want a quick answer, our transformer sizing calculator does the arithmetic for you.

How landscape lighting transformer sizing works

A low-voltage transformer takes 120V household power and steps it down to roughly 12V for your fixtures. Every transformer has a maximum rating in watts (or VA). Your job is to add up the load, add a safety margin, and choose a size that leaves room to grow.

Step one: total the load

List every fixture and its rated wattage. Modern LED fixtures typically draw somewhere between 2 and 10 watts each: path lights on the low end, larger uplights and wall washers on the high end. Many LED spec sheets also list a VA rating, which can be slightly higher than watts because LED drivers are not perfectly efficient. When a VA figure is listed, size with that number. It is the more honest one.

Step two: apply the 80% rule

Never load a transformer to 100% of its rating. Running at or below 80% keeps the core and windings cooler, extends its life and leaves headroom for voltage adjustments. The formula is simple:

Minimum transformer size = total fixture watts ÷ 0.8

A 120-watt LED load needs at least a 150-watt transformer (120 ÷ 0.8 = 150). If you are planning to add a patio or tree feature next year, go one size up now. Swapping a transformer later costs more than buying the right one the first time.

Multi-tap transformers and why they matter

Quality landscape transformers are multi-tap, meaning they offer several output terminals, commonly 12V, 13V, 14V and 15V. The higher taps exist to compensate for voltage lost along the wire. A fixture 200 feet away may need the 14V or 15V tap so that it actually receives around 11 to 12 volts at the lamp.

Many LED fixtures accept a range of input voltage, often somewhere around 9 to 15 volts, so they are more forgiving than old halogen lamps. That does not mean you can ignore voltage drop. Check the fixture spec sheet, and aim to keep every fixture inside its rated range. Over-voltage shortens LED driver life just as under-voltage dims the light.

The voltage drop formula

Voltage drop is the voltage lost to the wire's resistance between the transformer and the fixtures. Here is the working formula used by lighting designers:

Voltage drop = amps × 2 × run length (ft) × (ohms per 1,000 ft ÷ 1,000)

Amps equal watts divided by volts, so a 60-watt run at 12V draws 5 amps. The "× 2" accounts for current traveling out and back on the two conductors of the cable. The ohms value depends on the wire gauge.

Tip: As a rule of thumb, keep voltage drop on any run under about 1.5 to 2 volts, and use the transformer tap to make up the difference. If the math says you need more than the 15V tap, split the run or go to a heavier gauge.

Hub and T wiring: delivering even voltage

How you lay out the wire matters as much as the gauge. Daisy-chaining fixtures along one long cable means the first fixture gets the most voltage and the last gets the least. Two layouts solve that.

Hub

Hub wiring

A single heavy home run (often 10 or 8 AWG) travels from the transformer to a central splice point. Short, roughly equal leads then branch out to each fixture. Every fixture sees nearly the same voltage, which is ideal for clusters around a tree, a bed or a patio.

T

T wiring

The home run lands in the middle of a fixture run instead of at one end, splitting the load in two directions. This halves the effective length on each side and works well for long path runs and driveway borders.

Power, GFCI and Texas mounting considerations

Plug-in landscape transformers should connect to a GFCI-protected outdoor receptacle with an in-use cover rated for wet locations. If there is no suitable outlet where the transformer needs to live, a licensed electrician should add one. The lighting system itself is 12V low-voltage, but the 120V side is regular household electrical work.

Texas adds a few twists:

  • Mount the transformer at least 12 inches above grade, higher in flood-prone neighborhoods, and never where sprinklers hit it directly.
  • Avoid full afternoon sun on a west wall where possible. Summer heat raises internal temperatures, and a transformer loaded near 100% suffers most.
  • Choose a stainless steel or powder-coated enclosure that resists Gulf Coast humidity and salt air.
  • Use an astronomical timer or photocell so the system tracks sunset through the year without manual resets.
  • Leave clear space around the enclosure for airflow and service access, away from fire ant mounds and dense shrubs.
