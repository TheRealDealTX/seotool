If you are weighing a new system or wondering why your bill jumped after the old halogen lights went in, the question comes up fast: how much electricity does landscape lighting use? With modern LED fixtures, the honest answer is very little. A typical Texas yard can be beautifully lit for a few dollars a month. Older halogen systems are a different story.

This guide gives you the formula, real numbers for small, medium and large systems, and the hours your lights actually run in Texas through the year.

The formula for landscape lighting electricity use

Everything comes down to three numbers: how many watts the system draws, how many hours it runs and what you pay per kilowatt-hour (kWh).

Daily cost = total watts × hours per night ÷ 1,000 × rate per kWh

Dividing by 1,000 converts watt-hours to kilowatt-hours, the unit on your electric bill. Multiply the daily cost by 30 for a monthly figure or by 365 for a yearly one.

Here is a quick example. A 100-watt LED system running four hours a night uses 400 watt-hours, or 0.4 kWh. At 15 cents per kWh, that is 6 cents a night, about $1.80 a month and $21.90 a year.

Timer strategies that keep costs low

With LED, energy cost is rarely the deciding factor, but a smart schedule still saves money, extends fixture life and keeps your neighbors happy.

01

Astronomical timers

An astronomical timer calculates sunset for your location every day, so lights come on at dusk year-round without manual resets after daylight saving time changes.

02

Dusk plus a fixed off time

The most popular schedule: on at sunset, off at 10 or 11 PM. You get full curb appeal during the hours people are awake and outside.

03

Two-stage scheduling

Run the full design in the evening, then keep only entry, path and side-yard fixtures on until dawn. An all-night security layer of 30 to 40 LED watts costs only about $20 to $26 a year at 15 cents per kWh.

04

Zones and dimming

Smart controllers let you run backyard and pool zones only when you are entertaining, and dim fixtures that do not need full output.

Other factors that change your numbers

The table above is a solid starting point, but a few details can push your real-world landscape lighting electricity use up or down.

Fixture mix

Not every fixture draws 5 watts. Small path lights and step lights may draw 2 to 3 watts, while large uplights for mature live oaks, tall palms or two-story facades can draw 8 to 10 watts or more. A system heavy on tree lighting will use more power than one built mostly around paths. When you estimate, list each fixture type separately rather than assuming one average wattage.

Dimming

Many LED fixtures and smart controllers support dimming. Running a fixture at a lower output reduces its draw, so a design that dims accent zones after 9 PM uses less energy than one that runs everything at full output until midnight.

Holiday and seasonal additions

Plugging string lights or seasonal decorations into the landscape transformer adds load. Count those watts too, and make sure the transformer stays under 80 percent of its rating.

Your electricity plan

Texas rates vary by provider, plan and season. Some plans include time-of-use pricing or free night hours that make evening lighting even cheaper. Others charge more during peak periods. Plug your actual rate into the formula rather than relying on our 15 cent example.

Old halogen leftovers

A mostly LED system with a few remaining halogen fixtures can surprise you. Four 50-watt halogen lamps draw as much as forty 5-watt LEDs. If your bill seems high, check for holdouts before blaming the rest of the system.
