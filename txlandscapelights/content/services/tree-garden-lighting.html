Four ways to light a tree

There is no single correct way to light a tree. The right technique depends on its shape, its bark, where people will see it from and what is behind it.

Uplight

Canopy uplighting

Fixtures at the base aimed into the canopy. The classic approach for oaks, elms and pecans. Wide beams fill broad canopies; narrow beams reach tall, slender trees.

Graze

Trunk grazing

A fixture close to the trunk, aimed straight up, to show bark texture. Striking on crape myrtles, sycamores and the twisting trunks of older live oaks.

Moon

Moonlighting

Downlights mounted high in the tree that cast dappled light and branch shadows across the lawn, patio or path below.

Silhouette

Silhouetting

A light behind the plant aimed at a wall or fence, so the plant reads as a dark shape. Ideal for agave, yucca and sculptural natives.

Moonlighting under big Texas canopies

Large live oaks are perfect for moonlighting. We mount one or more downlights 20 to 35 feet up, aimed through the branches, so light falls on the ground as a soft, shifting pattern. It is one of the most natural-looking effects in landscape lighting, and it lights patios and lawns without any fixtures at eye level.

Mounts use stainless hardware and loose straps or stand-off brackets that do not girdle the limb. Wire runs down the trunk in a tight, bark-colored channel so it disappears in daylight.

Garden beds, borders and native plantings

Beds are where layered lighting comes together. A few small accent lights, often 1 to 3 watts each, pick out ornamental grasses, a specimen Japanese maple in a shaded corner, a boulder or a birdbath. In Hill Country and West Texas xeriscapes, grazing an agave or sotol from a low angle creates bold shadows on gravel and stone. We keep bed fixtures low and tucked behind foliage so they are hidden by day.

Seasons change the picture. Crape myrtles bloom in summer, then drop their leaves and show sculptural bark all winter, so one set of grazing lights gives you two looks a year. Live oaks stay green, which makes them reliable anchors for year-round tree uplighting in Texas. Pecans and cedar elms look completely different bare, when the light traces every branch against the winter sky.

Texas conditions we plan for

Heat, drought and clay

Blackland clay in Dallas, Fort Worth and Austin swells after rain and cracks in drought. Fixtures on short stakes lean, and lenses end up pointed at the neighbor's window. We set uplights on longer stakes, check plumb at the final adjustment visit and leave slack in the cable so soil movement does not pull connections apart.

Roots and rock

Old oaks have dense surface roots, so we never trench across the root zone. Instead we route wire around major roots or pull it shallow under mulch where the code and the site allow. In the Hill Country, limestone and caliche often sit just below the surface, and careful routing along bed edges beats breaking rock.

Freezes and storms

A freeze like February 2021 kills tender plants and can leave a lighting design pointed at an empty bed. Hard freezes and ice can also bring down limbs that carry moonlight fixtures. After major weather we recommend a quick check of aim and mounts, which our repair team can handle.

Watch out: Cheap uplights with exposed lamps and no shielding can throw a hot spot straight into your eyes from the patio. Always check a tree light from where you will actually sit or walk, not just from the street.

Choosing the right color for foliage

Most of the trees we light look best at 2700K, which keeps leaves a natural, deep green and warms bark. Some designers use 3000K on blue-gray foliage like cenizo, Arizona cypress or silver palms. We test both during the demo. If you are curious, the color temperature guide shows how each option reads on different materials.
