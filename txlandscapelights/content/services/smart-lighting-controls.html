Why Texas sunsets make controls matter

In most Texas cities the sun sets a little after 5:20 p.m. in early December and around 8:30 p.m. in late June. That is a swing of roughly three hours across the year, plus two daylight saving time jumps. A basic plug-in timer set in winter will switch your lights on in broad daylight all summer, wasting power and annoying neighbors. Set it in summer and your yard sits dark during winter dinner parties.

Our sunset timer planner shows the exact swing for your city. For Gulf Coast homes in Sugar Land or Houston and North Texas homes in Frisco, the numbers differ slightly, but the conclusion is the same: your lights should follow the sun, not the clock.

Zones: the foundation of control

Zoning means splitting fixtures into groups that can be switched or dimmed independently. A typical home might have four zones: facade and front trees, paths and driveway, backyard trees and beds, and patio or pool. Each zone can follow its own schedule.

For example, path lights and the front facade might run from sunset until midnight, while backyard accents turn off at 10 p.m. on weeknights. Security-minded homeowners often keep a few path and side-yard lights on until sunrise at low output.

Dimming

Adjust brightness without reaiming

Dimming lets you fine-tune each zone after installation. A tree that looks perfect at 100 percent on a moonless night may be too bright when the porch light is on. Dimming to 60 or 70 percent also extends LED life and reduces energy use.

Scenes

One tap for the whole yard

Scenes store a combination of zones and brightness levels. A "dinner" scene might brighten the patio and dim the facade. An "arrival" scene might bring up the paths and driveway. "All on" is useful for security checks or when you hear a noise.

Smart home

Voice and automation

Many smart transformers and controllers work with popular voice assistants and hubs. Z-Wave and other protocols let landscape lights follow the same routines as your indoor lighting, door locks or security system.

Remote

Control from anywhere

App-based controls let you check or change your lighting while traveling, which is handy for making the house look occupied or turning lights off if a storm knocks a fixture loose.

Setting up controls that last

Smart controls are only as reliable as their installation. A few details make the difference.

Wi-Fi at the transformer

Transformers usually mount on an exterior wall, often on the far side of the house from the router. Brick and stone walls block signal. We test signal strength before choosing a location and recommend a mesh node or outdoor access point if needed. Some controllers also offer Bluetooth or a separate bridge for weak spots.

Heat and weather

Electronics inside a transformer box can bake on a west-facing wall in a Texas August. We favor shaded, ventilated locations and stainless or powder-coated enclosures rated for outdoor use. Near the coast, sealed enclosures resist corrosion from salt air.

Power and outlets

Smart transformers plug into a standard exterior GFCI outlet. If a new outlet or dedicated circuit is needed, a licensed electrician installs it. The lighting system itself stays at 12V, which keeps zoning and dimming simple and safe.

Local schedules

We choose controllers that store schedules on the device itself. If the internet drops during a storm or a power flicker, lights still follow the programmed schedule when power returns.

Weekend, holiday and vacation schedules

Smart controllers make it easy to run a different schedule on weekends, extend hours for holiday gatherings or add a temporary zone for seasonal string lights. Some homeowners keep the backyard on later on Friday and Saturday nights and shut it off at 10 p.m. during the week. Others use a vacation mode that varies the off time slightly each night so the house looks lived in. All of this runs from the same app, and none of it requires a trip outside to the transformer. That everyday convenience is the real payoff of smart landscape lighting controls.

Tip: After a long power outage, like the grid failures many Texans saw during the February 2021 freeze, check that your controller has kept its clock and location. Most modern units recover automatically, but older timers may need to be reset.
