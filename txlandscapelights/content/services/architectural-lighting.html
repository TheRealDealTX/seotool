What we light on a Texas home

A facade is not one surface. It is a collection of features, and each one wants a different treatment.

  • Stone and brick walls: grazed from close range to show texture.
  • Columns and pilasters: a narrow beam from the base to draw the eye upward.
  • Gables and rooflines: enough reach to define the top of the house against the sky.
  • Entries and porches: soft, even light so guests can find the door and see faces.
  • Windows and shutters: framed, never blasted, so interior glare does not compete.

Washing vs. grazing: choosing the right technique

The two core techniques in architectural lighting are washing and grazing, and the difference comes down to fixture distance and beam spread.

Grazing for texture

Grazing places an uplight 6 to 12 inches from the wall and aims it nearly parallel to the surface. The light skims across every ridge and mortar joint, throwing tiny shadows that make Austin chopped limestone, tumbled brick or ledgestone look three-dimensional. A 15 to 25 degree beam works well here. Grazing is unforgiving on flat stucco, though, because it exaggerates every trowel mark and patch.

Washing for smooth surfaces

Washing moves the fixture back 2 to 4 feet and uses a wider 40 to 60 degree beam to spread light evenly. It suits smooth stucco, painted siding and Hardie board, where the goal is a soft glow rather than drama. On larger homes we often combine both: grazing on a stone turret or chimney, washing on the stucco field next to it.

Accenting vertical elements

Columns, pilasters and the corners of a house look best with a single narrow beam, usually 10 to 25 degrees, centered at the base. On a two-story facade this is how we reach a gable peak without mounting anything on the roof. Spacing columns evenly and matching output on each one matters more than raw brightness. A mismatched pair is the first thing a visitor notices.

Color temperature and the materials Texas homes are built with

Color temperature has a bigger effect on architecture than most people expect. Warm 2700K light brings out the cream and gold in Texas limestone and the reds in brick. Cooler 4000K light can make the same stone look gray and flat, and it reads as commercial. For most homes we recommend 2700K, with 3000K as an option for white stucco, modern facades or crisp gray brick. Our color temperature guide shows the difference side by side, and the warm white vs. cool white article explains when to break the rule.

We also pay attention to color rendering. A fixture with a CRI of 90 or higher shows materials accurately. Cheaper LEDs at CRI 80 can make warm stone look slightly green, especially on camera.

Heat

Built for 100-degree summers

Solid brass and copper fixtures shed heat and do not chalk or fade under Texas UV. LED drivers rated for high ambient temperatures last far longer in a fixture sitting on sun-baked mulch.

Humidity

Sealed for the Gulf Coast

In Houston, Katy and Galveston-area homes, salt air and humidity attack cheap aluminum. We specify brass or marine-grade finishes with sealed lenses and gel-filled wire connectors.

Hail

Tough lenses up north

North Texas hailstorms crack thin glass. Recessed lenses, tempered glass and shrouded fixtures give uplights near walls a better chance of surviving a spring storm.

Glare, light trespass and HOA rules

The best architectural lighting is the kind where you notice the house, not the fixtures. That means every uplight gets a shroud or a glare guard, and each one is aimed so the bright part of the beam lands on the wall, not in a neighbor's bedroom window or a driver's eyes. In master-planned communities like The Woodlands or Frisco, HOA guidelines often spell this out. Dark-sky friendly design follows the same logic: aim light at surfaces, keep it below the roofline where possible and avoid bare, unshielded sources.

If your HOA requires architectural review, we can provide a written plan with fixture types, finishes and placement for the committee.

Tip: Ask to see the facade lit from the street, not just from the front walk. Most people view your house from the curb or a car, and that is where uneven columns or a hot spot on a window are most obvious.

Installation details that matter in Texas soil

Architectural fixtures sit right at the foundation line, which in much of Texas means expansive clay that swells after rain and shrinks in drought. Stakes driven straight into that soil tilt within a season. We set fixtures on longer stakes or concrete-anchored mounts where needed and leave a service loop in the wire so the fixture can move slightly without pulling a connection apart. In the Hill Country, where caliche and limestone sit a few inches below the surface, we trench carefully or route wire along bed edges instead of fighting the rock.

Wire is sized to the run. Long runs to the far corner of a house lose voltage, and an under-voltage LED can flicker or dim. We calculate load and distance for every run before choosing a transformer and wire gauge. Connections are made with waterproof, gel-filled connectors rather than the push-pierce clips that come with big-box kits, because those clips are the most common failure point we see in landscape lighting repair calls.

Finally, architectural lighting rarely stands alone. Most homeowners pair it with path and driveway lighting for safe arrival and a few uplit trees to frame the house. Everything runs on the same low-voltage system and can be grouped into zones on a smart timer.
