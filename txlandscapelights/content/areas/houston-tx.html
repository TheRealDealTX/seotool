Houston homes and the materials we light

Houston has no single architectural style, and that is part of the fun. In River Oaks and Tanglewood you will find Georgian and French-inspired brick and limestone estates with deep setbacks. The Heights is full of restored bungalows and Victorian cottages with wood siding and front porches. Memorial mixes ranch homes on wooded lots with large new builds, and Bellaire and West U have many modern and transitional rebuilds in stucco, stone and painted brick.

Each surface responds differently. Textured brick and stone look best with grazing light placed close to the wall. Smooth stucco and painted brick call for wider, softer washes so you avoid hot spots. Porch columns and gables on Heights homes reward a lighter touch that keeps the character visible without making the house glow.

Live oaks, magnolias and Gulf Coast plantings

Houston's tree canopy is the city's best lighting asset. Broad live oaks along boulevards and in front yards are ideal for uplighting and moonlighting. Southern magnolias have glossy leaves that pick up light beautifully. Loblolly pines in Memorial and along the bayous give tall vertical lines, and crape myrtles, palms, sago palms and banana plants bring the subtropical look many Houston gardens are known for.

Because everything grows fast here, we place fixtures so they can be adjusted as shrubs fill in, and we avoid burying lights where azaleas or ligustrum will swallow them within a season. Our tree uplighting guide covers the techniques we use most often on mature oaks.

Humidity

Corrosion and moisture

Constant Gulf humidity attacks cheap fixtures and twist-on connectors. We use sealed LED fixtures in solid metals and waterproof connections rated for direct burial.

Storms

Hurricanes and tropical systems

Storms like Harvey in 2017 and Beryl in 2024 showed how fast limbs fall and water rises. Transformers go up high, and cable runs are planned to avoid low spots.

Heat

Long, hot summers

Months of high heat shorten the life of plastic housings and older halogen lamps. Quality LED fixtures run cooler and use a fraction of the power.

Flooding, drainage and Houston's flat terrain

Much of Houston sits on flat clay soil that drains slowly. Neighborhoods near Brays Bayou, Buffalo Bayou and White Oak Bayou, including parts of Meyerland and The Heights, know how quickly a yard can hold water. We look at your drainage during the consultation and keep transformers, timers and splices out of areas that pond. For more on storm prep, read our guide to protecting landscape lighting from Texas storms.

Deed restrictions and civic associations

Many Houston neighborhoods have deed restrictions enforced by civic associations, and some, like River Oaks, have their own architectural review. Landscape lighting rarely needs formal approval, but it is smart to keep beams aimed onto your own property, avoid glare toward neighbors and the street, and confirm any rules before work begins. Warm color temperatures and shielded fixtures keep things neighborly.

When your lights come on

Sunset in Houston ranges from roughly 5:20 pm in early December to about 8:25 pm in late June. That is a big swing, and a basic plug-in timer will be wrong most of the year. Astronomical timers and app-based controllers follow the sunset for you. Try our sunset timer planner to see suggested on and off times by season.

Many Houston homeowners run the full system from dusk to around 11 pm, then leave a few path and entry lights on until dawn for safety. Zoning the system this way keeps the house welcoming late at night without lighting the whole yard while everyone sleeps, and LED fixtures keep the power use modest either way.
