Windows decide more about how a home feels than almost anything else: how warm or cool a room stays, how bright it is at breakfast, how much of the outdoors you get to see. They are also one of the larger investments in a remodel. This is our guide to all about windows, from choosing windows for an eco-friendly home to placing them for natural light and picking window treatments that work as hard as the glass does.

Start by reading the label

Learning all about windows starts with a small sticker most people peel off without reading.

In the United States, many windows carry a label from the National Fenestration Rating Council (NFRC). It lists a few numbers that let you compare windows across brands. These are the ones that matter most for most homeowners:

RatingWhat it measuresWhat to look for
U-factorHow quickly heat passes through the whole windowLower is better, especially in cold climates
Solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC)How much solar heat comes throughLower in hot, sunny climates; can be higher in cold climates where solar warmth helps
Visible transmittance (VT)How much daylight comes throughHigher means brighter rooms
Air leakageHow much air sneaks through the window assemblyLower is better

ENERGY STAR also certifies windows by climate zone, which is a useful shortcut. Look up the requirements for your zone on the ENERGY STAR site rather than relying on a general rule, because the targets differ between, say, Minnesota and Arizona.

Desert homes: In hot, sunny places like El Paso, Tucson and Phoenix, solar heat gain is often the bigger concern than winter heat loss. A low SHGC, especially on west-facing glass, can make a noticeable difference in summer comfort.

Choosing windows for an eco-friendly home

Glass

  • Double-pane windows with an insulating gas fill are the standard for most new and replacement windows today.
  • Triple-pane windows add another layer of insulation and sound reduction. They make the most sense in cold climates or noisy locations.
  • Low-E coatings are thin, nearly invisible metallic layers that control heat transfer. Different coatings are tuned for different climates, so ask which one a manufacturer recommends for yours.

Frames

FrameStrengthsConsiderations
VinylAffordable, low maintenance, good insulationFewer color options; quality varies widely
FiberglassStrong, stable in temperature swings, insulates wellUsually costs more than vinyl

Installation

A high-rated window installed poorly will leak air and water. Ask installers how they flash and seal openings, whether they insulate the gap between frame and wall, and what warranty covers labor. Get more than one quote, and check whether your utility or local government offers rebates for efficient windows.

Placing windows for natural light

If you are building, adding on or remodeling, the direction a window faces matters as much as its size. In the Northern Hemisphere:

  • North-facing windows give soft, even light all day with little direct sun. Artists and photographers love it for this reason.
  • South-facing windows get the most sun overall. A roof overhang sized correctly can block high summer sun while letting in lower winter sun.
  • East-facing windows bring bright morning light, which is lovely in a kitchen or bedroom.
  • West-facing windows bring strong, hot afternoon light. They need the most shading, especially in the Southwest.

Windows placed higher on a wall push light deeper into a room. Windows on two sides of a room give more balanced light and cross-ventilation. And a window at the end of a hallway can make the whole space feel larger.

Natural light, as photographers see it

As people who think about light for a living, we notice how much a window shapes a photo. The same principle that makes a north-facing window lovely for portraits makes it lovely for living: large, indirect light sources create soft shadows and flattering skin tones. If you want to take better photos of your family at home, turn people so the window is beside them rather than behind them, and shoot in the morning or late afternoon when the light is warmer. Window treatments that actually work

Treatments are where you can fine-tune light and efficiency without replacing windows.

For insulation

Cellular or honeycomb shades trap air in their pockets and are a popular choice for adding insulation. Lined drapes that hang close to the wall and reach the floor also help, especially at night in winter.

For heat and glare

Blocking sun outside the glass is more effective than blocking it inside, because the heat never enters the house. Awnings, exterior solar screens, deep overhangs, pergolas and well-placed trees all help. Inside, solar shades cut glare while preserving the view.

For light and privacy

Top-down, bottom-up shades let light in from above while keeping the lower half of the window covered. Sheer curtains soften harsh light and add privacy during the day. Frosted window film is a renter-friendly fix for bathroom and street-facing windows.

A quick window checklist

  • Note which way each window in your home faces
  • Look for drafts, condensation between panes or hard-to-open sashes
  • Compare NFRC labels when shopping, not just brand names
  • Check ENERGY STAR criteria for your climate zone
  • Ask about rebates from your utility or local programs
  • Get at least two installation quotes with details on flashing and sealing
  • Plan shading for west- and south-facing glass
  • Choose treatments for each room's needs: light, privacy or insulation

All about windows: when replacement is not the answer

Replacing every window is expensive, and older windows can sometimes be improved instead. Weatherstripping, caulking, new sash cords, storm windows and good treatments can make a real difference for much less. For historic homes, local preservation rules may also affect what you can change. A home energy audit can help you decide whether windows or something else, like attic insulation, should come first.
