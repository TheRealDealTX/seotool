A light fixture is one of the few things in a room that changes both how the space looks and how it feels to live in. Choose one that is too small or hang it too high and the room feels unfinished; hang it too low and someone will bump their head. This guide covers how to hang light fixtures so they look intentional, including the right distance between a light fixture and a dining room table, how to space pendant lights, and ideas for corner hanging lights when there is no box where you need one.

Step one: size the fixture to the room

Before you think about height, make sure the fixture is the right scale. Two rules of thumb do most of the work.

The room rule

Add the length and width of the room in feet. Use that number, in inches, as a starting diameter for a central fixture. A 12-by-14-foot room suggests a fixture around 26 inches across. This is a starting point, not a law; a very airy fixture can go larger, and a solid, heavy-looking one can go smaller.

The table rule

Over a dining table, a fixture about half to two-thirds the width of the table usually looks balanced. Keep at least 6 inches between the edge of the fixture and the edge of the table on each side so nobody hits it when they stand up. For long rectangular tables, consider a linear fixture or two or three smaller pendants instead of one round chandelier.

Step two: get the hang height right

Height is where most fixtures go wrong, and it is the heart of learning how to hang light fixtures well. Here are the common guidelines designers and lighting retailers use.

WhereTypical guideline
Over a dining table30 to 36 inches from tabletop to bottom of fixture with an 8-foot ceiling; add about 3 inches per extra foot of ceiling
Over a kitchen islandAbout 30 to 36 inches above the counter, depending on pendant size and sightlines
Open walkway or entryAt least about 7 feet from the floor to the bottom of the fixture
Two-story foyerOften hung so it sits near the level of the second-floor window or landing
Over a bed or nightstandPendants low enough to read by, high enough to clear your head when sitting up
Mock it up first: Before you commit, have someone hold a cardboard circle the size of your fixture at the planned height while you sit at the table. Can everyone see each other? Does it feel cramped? Adjust before you cut the chain.

The distance between light fixture and dining room table

This one deserves its own section because it is the question we hear most. The 30 to 36 inch range works for most homes, but think about how you use the room:

  • Tall people at the table? Go toward the higher end so the fixture does not block sightlines.
  • Glare from exposed bulbs? Either raise the fixture or choose shades and frosted bulbs.
  • Table might move? Center the fixture on the table rather than the room, and use a swag hook or a canopy offset kit if the box is in the wrong spot.

Pendant lights: spacing and count

Pendants over an island or a long table are about rhythm. Common guidelines:

  1. Measure the length of the island and subtract about 12 inches to leave 6 inches on each end.
  2. Choose pendants whose combined widths take up a reasonable share of that length without crowding.
  3. Space them roughly 24 to 30 inches apart center to center for medium pendants, more for large ones.
  4. Odd numbers, especially three, often look balanced, but two large pendants over a short island can look cleaner than three small ones.

Corner hanging lights and fixtures without a box

Many rooms, especially in rentals and older homes, do not have a ceiling box where you want light. A plug-in swag pendant is the classic workaround.

  • Corner hanging lights. Mount a ceiling hook in the corner, hang a plug-in pendant over a reading chair, and run the cord along the ceiling and down the wall with cord clips or a cord cover.
  • Swagging to a table. Use two hooks: one over the outlet side and one over the table, letting the cord drape between them.
  • Anchor properly. Use a hook rated for the fixture's weight and screw it into a ceiling joist, or use a toggle anchor rated for drywall.

How to hang light fixtures safely

Swapping a fixture on an existing box is a common homeowner project, but it is still electrical work. Local codes and your comfort level should guide you. If anything below is unfamiliar, hire a licensed electrician.

  • Turn off the circuit at the breaker, not just the wall switch
  • Confirm power is off with a non-contact voltage tester
  • Check that the ceiling box is rated for the fixture's weight; heavy fixtures need a box or brace designed to carry them
  • Connect the ground wire as well as the hot and neutral
  • Have a helper hold the fixture while you make connections
  • Adjust chain or cord length before final mounting
  • Use bulbs within the fixture's rated wattage

Bulbs: the part everyone forgets

The same fixture can look warm and inviting or flat and clinical depending on the bulb. For living and dining spaces, many people prefer warm white in the 2700K to 3000K range. A high color rendering index, often listed as CRI 90 or above, makes food, skin tones and wood finishes look natural, which matters if you ever photograph your home or your dinner. Put dining and living room fixtures on dimmers when you can, and make sure your bulbs are labeled as dimmable.

Pulling the room together

Lighting works best in layers: an overhead fixture for general light, lamps or sconces for warmth, and task lighting where you cook or read. Once the fixture is up, look at what it lights. A wall of objects or a peg rail shelf looks better with a sconce or picture light aimed at it, and the room around your table changes once you consider the natural light from your windows too.
