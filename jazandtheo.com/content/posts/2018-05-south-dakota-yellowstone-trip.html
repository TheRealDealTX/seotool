Few drives in the West pack in as much variety as a South Dakota to Yellowstone road trip. In a matter of days you move from the striped spires of the Badlands to the granite and pine of the Black Hills, across the ranch country and mountains of Wyoming, and into the geysers and canyons of the world's first national park. This guide lays out a practical route from Rapid City to Yellowstone, the stops we think are worth slowing down for, and how to come home with Badlands National Park photos you will actually want to print.

The big picture: picking your route

Most travelers base the South Dakota half of the trip around Rapid City, then head west on Interstate 90 into Wyoming. From there, you have two main ways to reach the park.

From Rapid City to Yellowstone's East Entrance by way of Cody is roughly 400 miles. That is doable in one long day, but you will enjoy it far more split across two, with a night in Buffalo, Sheridan or Cody.

Check the season: Yellowstone's entrance roads and some interior roads open and close on a seasonal schedule, and high routes like the Beartooth Highway can close for snow even in early summer. Confirm current status with the National Park Service and the relevant state road department before you lock in a route.

Day 1 and 2: Badlands and the Black Hills

Badlands National Park

Badlands National Park sits about an hour east of Rapid City, so many travelers start there and work west. The Badlands Loop Road (SD-240) runs through the park and connects a string of overlooks and short trails. A few favorites:

  • Door and Window trails. Short walks into the formations that give you a sense of scale.
  • Yellow Mounds Overlook. Soft yellow and red bands that glow in low light.
  • Pinnacles Overlook. A wide view over jagged ridges, popular at sunset.
  • Sage Creek Rim Road. A gravel road where bison, pronghorn and prairie dog towns are often seen. Ask about road conditions after rain.

Plan to be in the park at sunrise or sunset if you can. Bring more water than you think you need; there is little shade, and summer days are hot.

The Black Hills

Back west, the Black Hills hold a dense cluster of stops. Mount Rushmore National Memorial is the famous one, and it is worth seeing early in the day before crowds build. Custer State Park is a highlight for many people, with the Wildlife Loop Road for bison and the Needles Highway for granite spires and narrow tunnels. Some tunnels are tight, so check size limits if you are driving a large vehicle or towing. Custer State Park charges an entrance fee; confirm the current pass options with the park.

Day 3: Across Wyoming

The drive across northern Wyoming is easy to treat as a transfer day, but it has some of the trip's quiet surprises.

  • Devils Tower National Monument. A short detour off I-90 in northeastern Wyoming. The paved trail around the base is an easy, rewarding walk.
  • The Bighorn Mountains. Whichever pass you choose, pull over at the overlooks. The change from prairie to alpine meadows in an hour of driving is striking.
  • Cody. A practical overnight before the park, with plenty of lodging and restaurants. It also puts you a reasonable morning drive from the East Entrance.

Days 4 to 7: Yellowstone

Yellowstone is large, and the drive between its main areas takes longer than the map suggests, especially with wildlife traffic. Rather than trying to see everything, choose a base or two and explore from there.

A simple way to divide the park

  1. Lake and Canyon. Coming in from the East Entrance, you reach Yellowstone Lake first, then the Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone with its Upper and Lower Falls.
  2. Geyser basins. Old Faithful, Grand Prismatic Spring and the Upper, Midway and Lower geyser basins on the west side of the loop.
  3. Northern range. Mammoth Hot Springs and the Lamar and Hayden valleys, which are well known for wildlife watching.

Lodging inside and near the park fills early in summer. Book well ahead, or plan to stay in gateway towns.

Stay on the boardwalk: Thermal areas have thin crusts over scalding water. Keep to marked trails and boardwalks, and give wildlife plenty of room; the park publishes minimum distances for bison, elk and predators.

Photographing the trip

A road trip like this rewards a little photographic planning. A few notes that apply across the whole route:

  • Chase the edges of the day. In open landscapes like the Badlands, sunrise and sunset do almost all of the work. Our golden hour photography guide explains how to plan around them.
  • Use a longer lens for wildlife. A telephoto lets you keep a safe distance and still fill the frame. Never approach animals for a photo.
  • Put people in the landscape. A small figure on a ridge gives scale to formations that otherwise look abstract.
  • Protect your gear. Dust in the Badlands and steam near geysers can both be hard on cameras. A lens cloth and a simple rain cover help.
  • Back up nightly. Copy each day's photos to a laptop or second card in the motel room.

Packing for a South Dakota to Yellowstone road trip

  • America the Beautiful pass or plan for individual park entrance fees
  • Offline maps, since cell service drops out in parts of the route
  • Layers: hot Badlands afternoons and cold Yellowstone mornings can fall in the same week
  • Refillable water bottles and a small cooler
  • Binoculars for wildlife
  • Sun protection: hat, sunscreen and sunglasses
  • Bear spray if you plan to hike in Yellowstone, and know how to use it
  • Spare camera batteries and memory cards
