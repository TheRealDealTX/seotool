Commercial Roof Types We Service

TPO single-ply membranes

White TPO is common on newer retail and office buildings in the Katy area because it reflects heat. Its weak points are seams, flashing at curbs and walls, and punctures from foot traffic around HVAC units.

Modified bitumen

Multi-layer modified bitumen is durable and common on older low-slope buildings. Over time it can blister, crack and lose its surface granules, especially in our sun.

Metal roofs

Many warehouses, shops and light industrial buildings use metal panels. Exposed fasteners loosen, washers dry out, and seams and skylights become leak points. See our metal roofing page for more on metal systems.

Why Katy Commercial Roofs Fail

Most commercial leaks we see trace back to a few causes:

  1. Clogged drains and scuppers. Leaves, debris and nesting birds block drainage and cause ponding.
  2. Rooftop equipment. HVAC curbs, conduit and service traffic create punctures and stressed flashing.
  3. Heat cycling. Daily expansion and contraction opens seams and pulls flashing away from parapet walls.
  4. Storm damage. Hail fractures membranes and wind lifts edges, as many buildings saw after the 2024 derecho and Hurricane Beryl.
  5. Deferred maintenance. Small problems left for a season or two become wet insulation and decking damage.

Planning and Budgeting a Commercial Roof Project

Commercial roof costs vary widely with roof size, system type, insulation requirements, access and how much wet material needs replacing. Because of that, we do not publish per-square-foot ranges for commercial work. Instead, we start with an inspection and give you a written scope with clear options, such as repair now, coat within a year or plan a replacement in your next budget cycle.

For buildings with multiple tenants, we coordinate access, notify occupants about noisy days and plan staging so parking and entrances stay open. Work can often be scheduled early in the day or in phases to limit disruption.

A condition report also helps with long-term planning. Knowing that a roof has an estimated five to eight years left lets you spread maintenance and reserves across budgets instead of facing an emergency replacement after a storm. Many owners find that a modest annual maintenance plan pays for itself by catching seam and flashing failures early.
