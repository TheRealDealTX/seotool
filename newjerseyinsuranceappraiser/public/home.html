Storm record

Eight storms that taught a New Jersey Insurance Appraiser what to look for

New Jersey’s big losses rarely fit the textbook. Each of these events changed how claims are scoped, split between policies or argued.

  1. 1962

    Ash Wednesday Storm

    A three-day nor’easter reshaped the shore. Lesson: nor’easters can do hurricane-scale damage without a hurricane deductible.

  2. 1999

    Tropical Storm Floyd

    Record flooding along the Raritan basin. Lesson: inland homes need flood coverage too.

  3. Aug 2011

    Hurricane Irene

    Came ashore near Little Egg Inlet and flooded river towns. Lesson: wind and water claims arrive together.

  4. Oct 2011

    Halloween nor’easter

    Heavy snow on leafy trees across North Jersey. Lesson: debris limits rarely cover big trees.

  5. Jun 2012

    South Jersey derecho

    Straight-line winds toppled trees and roofs in minutes. Lesson: document wind damage before cleanup.

  6. Oct 2012

    Superstorm Sandy

    Landfall near Brigantine and record surge. Lesson: hurricane deductibles depend on exact trigger wording.

  7. Sep 2021

    Ida’s remnants

    Flash floods and the Mullica Hill tornado. Lesson: basement water is often a flood or endorsement question.

  8. Apr 2025

    Jones Road Wildfire

    About 15,000 acres burned in Ocean County. Lesson: smoke damage reaches homes the fire never touched.

The process

Seven steps from a short estimate to a signed award

The appraisal clause turns an open-ended argument into a defined process with a binding result. This is how a New Jersey Insurance Appraiser moves a file from first call to award.

Read the full process guide
  1. 1

    Put the disagreement on paper

    Appraisal starts with a claim that has been accepted, at least in part.

  2. 2

    Make the written demand

    If the gap remains, either side can demand appraisal in writing.

  3. 3

    Each side names an appraiser

    Under language based on New Jersey’s standard fire policy, each side commonly has 20 days from the demand to name a competent and disinterested appraiser.

  4. 4

    The appraisers choose an umpire

    The two appraisers pick a neutral umpire, often early so one is ready if needed.

  5. 5

    The appraisers work the differences

    This is where most of the work happens.

  6. 6

    The umpire decides what’s left

    Items the appraisers can’t settle go to the umpire.

  7. 7

    The award is signed and paid

    An award signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss.

Questions

What people ask a New Jersey Insurance Appraiser

Straight answers about how appraisal works, what it costs and when it’s the wrong move.

All FAQs Glossary
What does a New Jersey Insurance Appraiser do?

We value property damage when you and your insurer agree the loss is covered but disagree on what it costs. As your named appraiser we inspect, build an independent line-item estimate, work with the carrier’s appraiser and, if needed, present open items to a neutral umpire.

How is an appraiser different from a public adjuster?

A public adjuster is licensed by the state to manage and negotiate the whole claim. An appraiser works inside the policy’s appraisal clause on one question: the amount of loss. We don’t negotiate claims outside appraisal, and we don’t give legal advice.

When should I call a New Jersey Insurance Appraiser?

When coverage is accepted, you have the carrier’s estimate, and the gap between it and your contractor’s number is meaningful, usually more than about 10% or several thousand dollars.

Is an appraisal award binding?

On the amount of loss, yes, once any two of the three panel members sign. That’s why the file should be complete before anyone demands appraisal.

What does it cost?

Each side pays its own appraiser and usually half of the umpire. We put our fee terms in writing before any work starts, and the first conversation is free.

Does a New Jersey Insurance Appraiser cover the whole state?

Yes. We work in all 21 counties, from Sussex and Bergen to Cumberland and Cape May.
