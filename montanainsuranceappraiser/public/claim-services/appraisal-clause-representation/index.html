Montana Appraisal Clause Representation for Policyholders

Once you or your insurer invokes the appraisal clause, the dispute stops being a back-and-forth over phone calls and becomes a short, structured process. We act as the appraiser you name.

What appraisal clause representation means

Most Montana homeowners, farm and commercial property policies contain a paragraph headed “Appraisal.” In plain terms it says: if you and the company fail to agree on the amount of loss, either side can demand appraisal in writing. Each side picks its own appraiser, the two appraisers pick an umpire, and a written agreement signed by any two of the three generally sets the amount of loss.

Appraisal clause representation is the job of being your appraiser on that panel. We build the number, defend it line by line, and work toward a signed award. We are not a law firm and we are not a public adjuster; we don’t argue coverage, and we don’t negotiate the claim outside the appraisal process.

How the process usually runs

  1. Policy read. We start with the exact appraisal wording in your policy. Forms differ on how the demand is made, how long each side has to name an appraiser, and how the umpire is chosen if the appraisers can’t agree.
  2. Demand or response. If you are demanding appraisal, the written demand should identify the claim and the disputed amount. If the insurer demanded it, you name us within the window the policy sets.
  3. Independent inspection. We walk the property ourselves: roof, framing, interior finishes, outbuildings, whatever is in dispute. We measure and photograph rather than marking up the carrier’s estimate.
  4. Our valuation. We write a line-item estimate priced for your area. Labor in Big Sky or Whitefish does not cost what it costs in Glendive, and contractor travel to a ranch north of Havre is a real expense.
  5. Appraiser-to-appraiser work. We meet the carrier’s appraiser and try to settle items one at a time. Many panels resolve here without the umpire ever deciding an item.
  6. Umpire, if needed. Items still apart go to the umpire with documentation from both sides. Two signatures make the award.

Where Montana disputes tend to land

The disagreements we see most often are about scope and price rather than cause:

  • Whether a hail-struck roof is repairable or needs full replacement, and whether discontinued shingles or metal panels can be matched.
  • Code upgrades required by the local building department in places like Bozeman or Missoula, and whether the policy’s ordinance or law coverage pays for them.
  • Overhead and profit when a loss needs several trades, which is common after wind, fire and snow-load claims.
  • Depreciation on older roofs, siding and log-home finishes, especially under actual cash value or roof payment schedules.
  • Pricing after a big storm year, when contractors in Billings and Great Falls are booked out and the building season is short.

When appraisal is not the right tool

Appraisal decides how much a covered loss costs. It does not decide whether the loss is covered. If the insurer has denied the claim outright, says an exclusion applies, or disputes that you met a policy condition, that is a coverage question, and appraisal clause representation in Montana will not answer it. Questions about the insurer’s conduct under Montana’s Unfair Trade Practices Act (Title 33, Chapter 18 of the Montana Code Annotated) also sit outside appraisal. For those, talk to a licensed Montana attorney.

Appraisal also may not be worth it when the gap is small compared with the cost of two appraisers and an umpire. Our claim gap calculator is a quick way to size the difference first.

Before anything is signed, read our guide to the Montana appraisal clause. It walks through the demand letter, the umpire step and what an award does and does not cover.

What you get from us

You get a defensible valuation, a clear record of what was agreed and what went to the umpire, and plain answers about where your claim stands. If the carrier’s estimate has not been checked yet, an insurance estimate review is often the right first step before we talk about appraisal clause representation at all.

Questions about appraisal clause representation

Who can demand appraisal in Montana?

Under most policy forms, either you or the insurance company can make a written demand for appraisal once the two sides disagree on the amount of a covered loss. Your specific policy controls the details, including how the demand is delivered and how long each side has to name an appraiser, so the wording in your own policy is the first thing to check.

Is the appraisal award binding?

An award signed by two of the three panel members is generally binding on the amount of loss. It does not settle coverage questions, and the insurer may still apply your deductible, prior payments and policy limits to the award. If you think the process itself was unfair or improper, a licensed Montana attorney can explain what options may exist.

Do you also handle coverage denials?

No. Appraisal only decides the dollar amount of a loss the insurer accepts as covered. If your claim was denied, or the insurer says an exclusion applies, that is a coverage dispute. We are not a law firm and cannot give legal advice, so for a denial we suggest talking with a licensed Montana attorney before deciding anything about appraisal.
