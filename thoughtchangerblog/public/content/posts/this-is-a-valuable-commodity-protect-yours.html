We talk about money like it’s the ultimate prize. We chase raises. We compare salaries. We check balances. But money is renewable. You can lose it and earn it back. You can spend it and replace it.

Your most valuable commodity doesn’t work that way. Once an hour is gone, it’s gone. Once your attention has been spent on something empty, you can’t ask for a refund.

I didn’t really understand this until I looked back at a stretch of months and couldn’t remember what I’d done with them. I’d been busy the whole time. Busy, but not present. That’s when the question changed for me—from How do I get more? to What am I trading away without noticing?

This article is about that trade. And how to make it more deliberately.

What Is Your Most Valuable Commodity?

Your most valuable commodity is the combination of your time and your attention. Time is the raw material of your life—finite, non-renewable, and the same twenty-four hours for everyone. Attention is how you actually spend it. Together, they decide what your life is made of.

In economics, a commodity is a basic good that can be bought, sold, and traded. Gold. Wheat. Oil. What makes some commodities more precious than others is simple: scarcity and usefulness. The rarer something is, and the more it’s needed, the more it’s worth.

By that measure, nothing you own comes close to your time.

You can’t store it. You can’t mine more of it. You can’t borrow it from tomorrow. And every single thing you care about—relationships, health, creativity, rest, meaning—requires it.

Attention is the twin. You can be physically in a room for an hour and give it nothing. You can give five minutes your full presence and remember them for years. Time is the quantity. Attention is the quality.

Why Time and Attention Outrank Money

Money matters. Let’s not pretend otherwise. It buys security, options, and breathing room.

But notice what money is actually for. Most of the time, we use it to buy back time—a shorter commute, help with chores, a weekend away, a cushion that lets us stop worrying. Money is often a tool for reclaiming hours.

That tells you which one is the real currency.

The Roman philosopher Seneca put it bluntly in On the Shortness of Life: “It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it.” He noticed that people guard their property fiercely but hand over their time to anyone who asks.

Two thousand years later, that hasn’t changed much. We’d never let a stranger take twenty dollars from our wallet. But we’ll let a notification take twenty minutes without a second thought.

Looked at this way, protecting your time isn’t selfish. It’s basic stewardship.

Who Is Bidding for Your Attention?

Here’s the uncomfortable part. Your attention isn’t just valuable to you. It’s valuable to a lot of other people too.

Much of the modern internet runs on what’s often called the attention economy—the idea that when information is abundant, human attention becomes the scarce resource that companies compete for. Apps, feeds, and headlines are designed to hold you. That’s their business model.

None of this makes technology evil. It just means the default settings aren’t built around your priorities.

Other bidders are quieter:

  • Other people’s urgency. The meeting that could have been an email. The request that “will only take a minute.”
  • Your own worry. Rehearsing conversations that haven’t happened. Replaying ones that already did.
  • Obligation without reflection. Saying yes because saying no feels awkward.
  • Low-grade busyness. Tidying the inbox instead of doing the hard thing.

When you treat time and attention as your most valuable commodity, these bids become visible. And once you can see them, you can decide which ones deserve a yes.

Signs You’re Spending It Without Noticing

Most of us don’t waste time dramatically. We leak it. A little here, a little there, until whole days slip through.

A few signals that the leak is happening:

  • You reach the end of the week and can’t name one thing you did on purpose.
  • You pick up your phone for one task and put it down twenty minutes later without having done it.
  • You feel tired but not satisfied.
  • The things you say matter most keep getting pushed to “later.”
  • You’re rarely bored—but rarely absorbed, either.

That last one surprised me. Boredom used to be the space where ideas showed up. When every gap gets filled, that space disappears.

If any of these feel familiar, there’s nothing to be ashamed of. It’s simply information. As I wrote in what happens when you’re not present, the first step back is noticing you left.

How to Protect Your Most Valuable Commodity

Protection doesn’t mean rigid schedules or color-coded calendars. It means a handful of small, repeatable choices that keep your time pointed at what matters.

1. Know What You’re Protecting It For

You can’t guard something well if you don’t know why it’s worth guarding. Write down three to five things you want more of. Not goals—priorities. Time with your kids. Morning walks. Finishing the book. Deep work.

This list becomes your filter. If you’ve never done this exercise, what is essential to you is a good place to start.

2. Run a One-Week Time Audit

For seven days, jot down roughly how you spend your hours. No judgment. Just data. Most people discover two or three surprising leaks—often in the transitions between tasks rather than the tasks themselves.

3. Price Your Yes

Every yes is also a no to something else. Before agreeing to a request, ask: What will this cost me, and what won’t get done? Sometimes the answer is still yes. That’s fine. The point is to choose it.

4. Create Protected Blocks

Pick one block of time each day—even thirty minutes—that belongs to your priorities. Phone in another room. Door closed if you have one. Treat it like an appointment you wouldn’t cancel on a friend.

5. Reduce the Bidders

Turn off non-essential notifications. Move tempting apps off your home screen. Batch email into two or three check-ins a day. These aren’t heroic acts of willpower. They’re small design changes that make the better choice the easier one.

6. Leave Some Margin

A calendar packed edge to edge leaves no room for life to happen. Margin is where you recover, reflect, and say yes to the unexpected good things. Empty space isn’t wasted time. It’s breathing room.

7. Do One Thing at a Time

Multitasking feels efficient. It rarely is. Every time you switch between tasks, a little attention gets left behind on the last one. You end up spending more time and getting less depth.

Try single-tasking for one hour this week. One tab. One conversation. One piece of work. Notice how different it feels when your attention isn’t split into pieces. Many people find the work goes faster—and feels calmer.

The same applies to people. When someone you love is talking, put the phone face down. Full attention is one of the rarest gifts you can give, precisely because it’s so hard to come by. It costs you nothing extra, and it tells the other person they matter more than whatever might be waiting on the screen.

Common Mistakes When Guarding Your Time

Once you start protecting your time, it’s easy to overcorrect. A few traps to watch for:

Treating every minute as a productivity unit. Your time isn’t only valuable when it produces something. Rest, play, and slow conversations are some of the best things you can spend it on.

Confusing efficiency with meaning. Doing the wrong things faster isn’t progress. Speed matters less than direction.

Becoming unavailable to the people you love. Protecting your time is about choosing what deserves it. Often, the answer is people. Don’t build a fortress so tight that connection can’t get in.

Waiting for a perfect system. You don’t need the ideal app or planner. You need one honest choice today, and another tomorrow.

A Reflection to Carry With You

Try this tonight. Before you go to sleep, ask yourself three questions:

  1. Where did my attention go today?
  2. Which hour felt most like me?
  3. What’s one thing I’ll protect tomorrow?

That’s it. Two minutes. Over time, those small reviews add up to a very different relationship with your days.

Benjamin Franklin wrote in Poor Richard’s Almanack that “Lost time is never found again.” It sounds stern. But I find it freeing. If time can’t be found again, then the only time that matters is the time in front of us right now. And we still get to decide what to do with it.

Spend It Like It Matters

Your most valuable commodity isn’t locked in a vault. It’s in your calendar. It’s in the next ten minutes. It’s in where you choose to look.

You won’t guard it perfectly. Nobody does. Some days the leaks win.

But every time you notice, you get a chance to choose again. And a life built on small, deliberate choices about time is a life that feels like yours—something worth remembering, as I explored in life is fleeting.

Protect it. Not out of fear.

Out of respect for what it’s worth.
