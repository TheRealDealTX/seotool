I should be further along by now.
I’m supposed to be doing something bigger.
Everyone else seems to know where they’re going.

If you’ve ever lain awake asking, “What am I supposed to do with my life?” you are in very good company. I’ve asked it at 22, at 35, and more than once since. It tends to show up at crossroads: graduations, layoffs, breakups, birthdays that end in zero.

It’s a painful question. But I’ve come to believe it’s also a slightly broken one. Not because it doesn’t matter, but because of one small word hiding in the middle of it.

Supposed.

Let’s talk about that word, who put it there, and what happens when you start asking a better question instead.

What Am I Supposed to Do With My Life? The Short Answer

There is no single thing you are “supposed” to do with your life, and no outside authority who gets to decide it for you. Your direction is something you discover and choose over time, by paying attention to what energizes you, what you value, and what you’re willing to work hard for, and then testing it in real life.

That answer can feel unsatisfying at first. We want a clear assignment. A calling with our name on it. A sign.

But I’ve found it freeing. If there’s no single correct answer, there’s also no way to permanently miss it. You’re not late for a train that’s already left. You’re laying down track as you go.

Where “Supposed To” Comes From

Nobody is born with a list of what they’re supposed to do. Those expectations get installed, slowly and often lovingly, by the world around us.

Family

Most of us absorbed a template for success before we could question it. Get the stable job. Buy the house. Don’t take big risks. Or the opposite: be exceptional, make us proud, don’t waste your gifts. Our families usually mean well. But their hopes and fears can quietly become our script.

Culture

Society hands out a timeline. Graduate by this age. Career by that one. Partner, kids, promotion, retirement. Step off the schedule, and you can feel behind even when you’re exactly where you need to be.

Comparison

Social media turned everyone’s highlight reel into a public scoreboard. It’s easy to look at a former classmate’s new business or a friend’s perfect-looking family and conclude that you’re the one who missed the memo.

Your Younger Self

Sometimes the loudest voice is our own, from years ago. The plan you made at 19 still has opinions about your life at 40, even though you’ve changed completely since then.

None of these voices are evil. But none of them should get the final vote.

I remember the moment I realized how much of my own “supposed to” list had come from other people. I was sitting with a notebook, writing down every goal I was chasing, and next to each one I wrote whose idea it was first. My parents. A college professor. A friend I admired. A magazine article I read at 24. Only a few of the goals had my own name beside them. That page didn’t tell me what to do next. But it told me which voices I could finally stop obeying.

Who Actually Gets to Decide?

You do.

That’s both the most liberating and the most uncomfortable answer. It means no one else can hand you the answer. It also means no one else can take it away.

Deciding doesn’t mean ignoring everyone. Wise counsel from people who love you matters. Responsibilities to the people who depend on you matter. Real-world constraints, money, health, geography, matter. But within those realities, the authorship is yours.

Better Questions to Ask Instead

When the big question feels paralyzing, break it into smaller, more answerable ones:

  • What makes me lose track of time? Not what impresses people. What absorbs you.
  • What problems do I care about? The ones that make you angry or tender often point toward meaningful work.
  • What am I naturally good at that others find hard? Friends often see this before we do. Ask them.
  • What kind of life do I want, not just what job? Where do you live? How do your days feel? Who’s around you?
  • What would I try if I knew it didn’t have to be forever? This one takes the pressure off.

The mythologist Joseph Campbell famously encouraged people to “follow your bliss.” It’s often misread as “do whatever feels good.” I think it’s closer to this: notice what makes you feel most alive, and take it seriously.

How to Find Your Direction in Practice

Clarity rarely arrives through thinking alone. It comes through doing, noticing, and adjusting.

1. Run Small Experiments

Instead of trying to choose your life’s purpose in one dramatic decision, test ideas in small ways. Take a weekend class. Volunteer. Shadow someone for a day. Start a side project. Each experiment teaches you something no amount of daydreaming can.

2. Track Your Energy

For two weeks, jot down which activities leave you energized and which leave you drained. Patterns show up quickly, and they’re often more honest than our ideas about what we should enjoy.

3. Listen to the Quiet Pull

Sometimes direction shows up as a persistent nudge: an interest that keeps coming back, a longing you can’t shake. It’s worth learning to listen when your soul speaks, even if what it says is inconvenient.

4. Align Before You Leap

You don’t have to blow up your life to change direction. Often the first step is closing the gap between your values and your daily actions, a theme I explore in living in alignment. Small alignments build the confidence for bigger ones.

5. Give Yourself Seasons

Your life doesn’t need one purpose. It can have several, in different chapters. The person you are at 25 might be building skills. At 40, raising a family or leading a team. At 60, mentoring or creating. Each season can have its own answer.

The Question at Different Ages

“What am I supposed to do with my life?” sounds different depending on when it shows up.

In your twenties, it often feels like a test you’re failing in real time. Everyone around you seems to be choosing majors, careers, partners, and cities with confidence. In truth, most of them are guessing too. This is a decade for exploring, not for getting it all right.

In your thirties and forties, the question can arrive as restlessness. You did the things you were supposed to do, and now you’re wondering whether they were ever really yours. That isn’t ingratitude. It’s growth asking for room.

In your fifties and beyond, it often returns as a question of legacy and freedom. Children may be grown. Careers may be winding down or shifting. There’s a chance to ask, maybe for the first time, what you’d do if no one else’s expectations were in the way.

At every age, the question is less a sign that something is wrong and more a sign that you’re paying attention. That’s a good place to start.

Common Traps That Keep People Stuck

Waiting for certainty. Certainty usually comes after action, not before. If you wait to feel sure, you may wait forever.

Believing in “the one” perfect path. There are many good lives you could live. Choosing one doesn’t mean you failed the others.

Confusing labels with identity. “Lawyer,” “stay-at-home mom,” “creative type”: labels can be useful, but they can also box you in. There’s real freedom in loosening labels.

Comparing timelines. Someone else’s fast start doesn’t make your path wrong. Plenty of people find their truest work later in life.

Reflection Prompts

  1. Whose voice do I hear when I tell myself what I’m “supposed” to do?
  2. Which of my current goals did I truly choose, and which did I inherit?
  3. What did I love doing as a child before anyone told me what was practical?
  4. What small experiment could I try in the next month?
  5. If no one would judge me, what would I explore?

Stop Saying “Supposed,” Start Saying “Choose”

The next time you catch yourself asking, “What am I supposed to do with my life?” try rephrasing it: “What do I want to do with my life, and what’s one step I can take toward it?”

It’s a small change in words. It’s a big change in who’s holding the pen.

Your life isn’t an exam with a hidden answer key. It’s a story you get to write, revise, and keep writing. Nobody else gets to decide how it goes.

And that, I promise, is very good news.
