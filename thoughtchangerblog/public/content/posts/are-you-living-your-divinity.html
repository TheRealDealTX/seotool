Some phrases arrive with a hush around them. The divinity of her is one of those.

It sounds like something carved over a temple door. Lofty. A little out of reach. And yet when I sit with it, what comes to mind isn’t a statue or a goddess on a pedestal. It’s my grandmother’s hands kneading bread. A friend holding a room together during the worst week of her life. A woman at a crosswalk laughing so freely that strangers smiled.

That’s the sacred feminine most of us actually meet. Not in the clouds. In the kitchen, the boardroom, the hospital corridor, the mirror.

So let’s bring this phrase down to earth, where it belongs.

What “Divinity of Her” Means

The divinity of her is the idea that there is something sacred in a woman’s presence, wisdom, and creative power—not because she is perfect, but because she is whole. It honors qualities like intuition, nurture, fierceness, endurance, and the ability to bring things to life, and it treats them as worthy of reverence rather than as soft extras.

It isn’t about putting women on pedestals. Pedestals are lonely, and they’re easy to fall from. It’s about recognition. Seeing what was always there.

And it isn’t only a religious idea. For some, it’s deeply spiritual. For others, it’s simply a way of saying: she matters, all of her, including the parts the world calls too much or not enough.

The Sacred Feminine Across Cultures

Humans have been honoring the feminine as sacred for a very long time.

In Hindu tradition, Shakti is the dynamic, creative energy behind all existence. In ancient Egypt, Isis was revered as a mother, a healer, and a keeper of magic. The Greeks gave wisdom and strategy to Athena. Many Christian traditions honor Mary with profound devotion. Indigenous cultures around the world speak of Earth herself as a mother.

Different stories. A shared instinct.

What strikes me is how often these figures are not delicate. They are powerful. They protect, create, destroy what needs destroying, and rebuild. The sacred feminine was never meant to be fragile.

Somewhere along the way, many cultures kept the reverence in their temples but forgot to carry it home. We praised the goddess and overlooked the woman. Reclaiming the divinity of her is, in part, closing that gap.

Five Faces of the Sacred Feminine in Everyday Life

You don’t have to look far to find it. Here’s where I see it most.

1. The creator

Yes, some women create life. That alone is astonishing. But creation is bigger than biology. Women create homes that feel like refuge, businesses, art, traditions, friendships that outlast decades. Creation is often quiet and rarely applauded. It is still sacred.

2. The intuitive

That inner knowing—the sense that something is off before anyone says a word—has been dismissed as “just a feeling” for too long. Intuition is wisdom arriving faster than words. Honoring it doesn’t mean ignoring facts. It means letting your inner voice have a seat at the table. I wrote more about that inner voice in when your soul speaks, listen.

3. The nurturer

Care is holy work. Feeding, comforting, teaching, sitting up with someone through the night. The world runs on this kind of love, and it’s easy to take for granted. The sacred version of nurture also includes nurturing herself—which is often the hardest part.

4. The fierce one

The divine feminine is not only gentle. Think of a mother bear, or a woman saying “No” and meaning every letter of it. Fierceness is protection. Boundaries are sacred too. You can read more about that in pretty badass: strength wrapped in grace.

5. The wise woman

Every culture has her: the elder, the crone, the aunt who’s seen it all and tells the truth kindly. Age doesn’t diminish feminine power. It distills it. Some of my favorite words from wise women are collected in these wise woman quotes.

Why So Many Women Lose Touch With It

If the sacred is already within, why does it feel so far away?

A few reasons show up again and again:

  • Endless giving. When every drop goes to others, there’s nothing left to notice the self.
  • Being told to be smaller. Quieter, thinner, nicer, less emotional. Each message trims away a little more.
  • Comparison. It’s hard to feel sacred while measuring yourself against a filtered stranger.
  • Old wounds. Experiences that taught her that her body, voice, or desires were unsafe.

None of these erase the divinity. They just bury it. And what’s buried can be uncovered.

How to Honor the Divinity of Her in Yourself

This is the practical part. Sacredness isn’t just believed. It’s practiced.

Treat your body as home, not a project

Rest when you’re tired. Move in ways that feel good, not punishing. Eat with attention. Speak about your body the way you’d speak about someone you love. A body that is honored becomes easier to live in.

Make room for stillness

Ten minutes. A walk without headphones, a candle, a journal, a prayer, a cup of tea held with both hands. Intuition is quiet. It needs a little silence to be heard.

Create something just for you

Not for income or likes. A garden, a song, a messy painting, a new recipe. Creation reconnects you to the part of yourself that brings things to life.

Say one honest “no” this week

Every “no” to what drains you is a “yes” to what’s sacred in you. Start small. Notice how the sky does not fall.

Gather with other women

Circles of women have held each other up for as long as there have been women. Coffee with a friend. A book club. A walking group. Being seen by others helps you see yourself.

Reflection prompts

  • When do I feel most fully myself?
  • Which part of me have I been taught to hide?
  • What would change if I treated my own needs as sacred?
  • Which women in my life have shown me what strength really looks like?

Honoring the Sacred Feminine in Others

This isn’t only inner work. It shows up in how we treat each other—and it’s an invitation for everyone, not just women.

  • Listen fully when she speaks. Don’t finish her sentences.
  • Notice the invisible work: the planning, remembering, caring. Say thank you out loud.
  • Credit her ideas by name.
  • Respect her intuition, even when it can’t be explained in a spreadsheet.
  • Share the load so care doesn’t become depletion.

Men who honor the feminine don’t lose anything. Often they discover their own capacity for tenderness, patience, and intuition—qualities that belong to all of us.

A Simple Ritual for Ordinary Days

Big retreats are lovely. But the sacred is mostly kept alive in small, repeated gestures. Here’s a five-minute ritual you can try each morning or evening. Adjust it until it feels like yours.

  1. Arrive. Sit somewhere quiet. Put a hand on your heart or your belly. Take three slow breaths and feel your own warmth.
  2. Acknowledge. Name one thing your body did for you today. Carried you. Healed a scrape. Hugged someone. Say thank you, silently or out loud.
  3. Ask. “What do I need?” Wait for the first honest answer. It might be rest, a phone call, a cry, a walk, a boundary.
  4. Affirm. Choose a simple phrase. I am allowed to take up space. My voice matters. I am enough as I am. Repeat it a few times.
  5. Act. Do one small thing that answers your need before the day swallows it.

It sounds simple because it is. The point isn’t the ritual itself. It’s the message underneath it, repeated daily: I am worth tending to.

Over weeks, that message starts to change how you stand, how you speak, and what you’re willing to tolerate. That’s the sacred feminine growing roots.

Some women like to mark the ritual with an object—a stone from a favorite beach, a candle, a piece of jewelry passed down from a mother or grandmother. Others keep it entirely invisible. Both work. What matters is the returning.

A Few Misunderstandings to Let Go Of

“It means women are better than men.” No. Honoring one doesn’t diminish another. Sacredness isn’t a competition.

“It means always being soft and serene.” No. Anger, grief, and fire are part of the feminine too.

“It’s only for spiritual or religious people.” Not at all. You can hold this as faith, as metaphor, or simply as respect.

“It’s only about motherhood.” Motherhood can be a profound expression of it. So can a hundred other ways of creating and caring.

She Was Always Sacred

Here’s what I keep coming back to.

The divinity of her isn’t something a woman has to earn, prove, or perform. It isn’t awarded for being good enough, thin enough, calm enough. It was there in the beginning. It’s there now, on the tired days and the radiant ones.

The work isn’t to become sacred.
The work is to remember.

And then to live like it—a little more each day. If you need a nudge in that direction, you might enjoy be your own light.
