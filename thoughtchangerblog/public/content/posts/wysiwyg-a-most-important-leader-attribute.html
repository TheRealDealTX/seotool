Think about the best leader you’ve ever worked with. Chances are, you never had to wonder which version of them was going to walk through the door. They were the same in the board meeting as in the hallway. The same when things were going well as when everything was on fire.

There’s a phrase for that. WYSIWYG—what you see is what you get.

Most people know the WYSIWYG meaning from computers. But I think it describes one of the most underrated traits a leader can have. Not charisma. Not brilliance. Consistency. The kind that lets people relax, because they know where they stand.

Let’s look at what the phrase means, where it came from, and why it matters so much for anyone who leads—at work, at home, or in their community.

WYSIWYG Meaning: What the Acronym Stands For

WYSIWYG stands for “What You See Is What You Get.” It’s usually pronounced “wiz-ee-wig.” In computing, it describes software where what you see on screen while editing closely matches the final result. In everyday language, it describes a person who is exactly as they appear—no hidden agenda, no performance, no surprises.

When someone calls a leader “very WYSIWYG,” they mean that leader is transparent, consistent, and genuine. What they say matches what they do. How they act in public matches how they act in private.

Where the Phrase Came From

The phrase “what you see is what you get” was already part of popular speech before computers made the acronym famous. Comedian Flip Wilson helped popularize it in the early 1970s through his character Geraldine, whose catchphrase it became.

The acronym took off as personal computers moved from typed commands to visual interfaces. Before that, formatting a document meant typing codes and only discovering what the result looked like after printing it. WYSIWYG editors changed that. Suddenly, the screen showed you the truth.

That shift is a useful metaphor. Before WYSIWYG, there was a gap between what you saw and what you got. You had to guess. You had to wait. Sometimes you got an unpleasant surprise.

Plenty of people have worked for leaders like that.

Why WYSIWYG Is a Leader’s Greatest Trait

Teams can work with almost any leadership style. Demanding, gentle, detail-oriented, big-picture. What they struggle to work with is unpredictability.

When a leader is one way on Monday and another on Thursday, people spend energy figuring out the weather instead of doing the work. They hold back ideas. They manage up. They brace.

A WYSIWYG leader removes that tax. Here’s what that consistency gives a team:

  • Trust. People believe what you say because your words and actions line up.
  • Safety. People speak up, disagree, and admit mistakes because they know how you’ll respond.
  • Speed. Less guessing means fewer misunderstandings and quicker decisions.
  • Loyalty. People stay with leaders they don’t have to decode.

None of this requires being perfect. It requires being real.

What a WYSIWYG Leader Looks Like

It helps to see the contrast side by side.

Notice that the WYSIWYG leader isn’t flawless. They still get frustrated. They still make mistakes. The difference is that you can see it, and so you can work with it.

WYSIWYG Is Not the Same as “Brutal Honesty”

Here’s a common misunderstanding. Some people hear “what you see is what you get” and take it as permission to say whatever they think, however harshly. “I’m just being honest. That’s who I am.”

That’s not the WYSIWYG meaning in leadership. That’s just a lack of filter.

Real transparency includes care. It means being truthful and considerate. It means sharing your real view without humiliating anyone. Being the same person everywhere doesn’t mean being careless everywhere.

It also doesn’t mean oversharing. A WYSIWYG leader can keep confidences, protect private information, and choose not to share every passing feeling. What matters is that nothing they show you is a mask covering something opposite underneath. As I explored in are you wearing masks that hide the real you, the masks we wear for protection often cost us the very connection we want.

How to Become a More WYSIWYG Leader

Consistency isn’t a personality type. It’s a practice. Here are a few ways to build it.

1. Get Clear on Your Values

You can’t be consistent if you don’t know what you stand for. Write down three to five values that guide how you want to lead. Honesty, fairness, curiosity, courage—whatever is true for you. When you face a hard call, check it against the list.

2. Close the Say–Do Gap

Every promise you keep builds trust. Every one you break quietly withdraws it. Make fewer commitments, and keep the ones you make. If something changes, say so early.

3. Be the Same Person in Every Room

Pay attention to how you treat people with less power than you. The receptionist, the new hire, the server at lunch. People notice. Consistency downward says more about your character than charm upward ever could.

4. Narrate Your Thinking

When you make a decision, share the reasoning where you can. “Here’s what I weighed and why I landed here.” It invites people into your process and removes the mystery that breeds rumor.

5. Admit What You Don’t Know

“I’m not sure yet” and “I got that wrong” are two of the most trust-building sentences a leader can say. They show people that what they see from you is real, even when it isn’t polished.

6. Ask for Honest Feedback

Ask a few trusted people: Am I consistent? Where do I seem different from how I describe myself? The gap between how we see ourselves and how others experience us is often where the real work lives.

When It Matters Most: Leading Through Hard Moments

Anyone can be consistent when things are calm. The real test comes when they’re not.

A missed target. A round of layoffs. A public mistake. A personal crisis bleeding into work. These are the moments when people watch their leaders most closely—and when the gap between the person they thought they knew and the person in front of them either closes or cracks wide open.

Picture a manager who is warm and encouraging in good quarters and cold, even suspicious, in bad ones. Before long, the team learns to read the numbers before reading her emails. Nobody feels safe bringing her problems early, so problems arrive late. The inconsistency doesn’t just hurt morale. It hurts results.

A WYSIWYG leader handles hard moments differently:

  • They tell the truth sooner. Even when the news is incomplete, they share what they know and when they’ll know more.
  • They stay anchored to the same values. The pressure changes. The principles don’t.
  • They let people see their humanity. Saying “this is hard for me too” isn’t weakness. It’s honesty, and it builds solidarity.
  • They follow through afterward. What they promised in the storm, they deliver when it passes.

This takes discernment—knowing what to share, when, and how. It’s a skill built over time, one choice at a time. If you want to sharpen it, examples of discernment in life offers a helpful starting point.

When people later describe a leader they deeply trusted, they rarely talk about the easy days. They talk about how that person showed up when everything was uncertain. That is where the WYSIWYG meaning becomes more than a nice phrase.

WYSIWYG Beyond the Workplace

You don’t need a title to lead. Parents lead. Friends lead. Neighbors, coaches, volunteers—all of them shape the people around them.

In every one of those roles, the same principle holds. Children trust parents whose reactions are predictable. Friendships deepen when nobody has to wonder what the other person really thinks. Communities rally around people whose actions match their words.

Living this way has a personal payoff too. Keeping up different versions of yourself is exhausting. When the inside and outside line up, there’s a kind of ease that’s hard to describe until you feel it. I wrote more about that alignment in living and leading from congruence.

A Few Questions to Reflect On

  1. Would the people who work with me describe me the same way I’d describe myself?
  2. Is there a situation where I become someone noticeably different?
  3. What’s one promise I’ve made recently that I need to keep or renegotiate?
  4. Where am I performing instead of being real?

Sit with whichever question makes you a little uncomfortable. That’s usually the one with the most to teach.

Be the Leader People Don’t Have to Decode

The WYSIWYG meaning started as a promise from software: the screen won’t lie to you. It’s a pretty good promise for a leader to make, too.

You don’t need to be impressive. You need to be reliable. Clear. Honest. The same on a hard day as on an easy one.

When people know what they’re getting, they can stop guessing and start growing.

That’s what you see. And that’s what they get.
