You know the feeling. You sit down to start the thing that matters—the proposal, the hard conversation, the first page, the first workout in months—and something pushes back. A heaviness. A sudden urge to check your phone. A very reasonable voice explaining why tomorrow would be better.

That pushback is resistance. And most of us treat it like an enemy to defeat or a sign to stop.

But what if we treated resistance as an ally instead? What if that friction was pointing somewhere useful, rather than blocking the way?

I’ve come to believe it usually is. Not always. But far more often than we give it credit for.

What Does It Mean to See Resistance as an Ally?

Seeing resistance as an ally means treating friction—fear, hesitation, pushback, difficulty—as information and training rather than as proof that you should quit. Resistance often shows up right before something meaningful, and working with it, rather than against it, is how strength is built.

Think about a muscle. It doesn’t grow without load. The weight is the resistance. Take it away and nothing changes. Our inner lives work in a surprisingly similar way. Patience grows when something tests it. Courage grows when something frightens us and we act anyway.

Resistance isn’t the opposite of growth. It’s often the shape growth takes while it’s happening.

Where Resistance Comes From

Resistance tends to come from two directions.

The Resistance Inside

This is the quiet kind. It sounds like:

  • “I’m not ready yet.”
  • “Who am I to do this?”
  • “It has to be perfect before anyone sees it.”
  • “I’ll start when things calm down.”

Inner resistance often disguises itself as prudence. Research. Preparation. Waiting for the right moment. It’s convincing because part of it is true—preparation does matter. But somewhere along the line, preparation becomes a place to hide.

The Resistance Outside

This is friction from the world. Criticism. Rejection. Limited time or money. A door that doesn’t open on the first knock.

Outer resistance tests your commitment. Inner resistance tests your identity. Both have something to teach.

Why Resistance Shows Up Right Before Growth

Here’s a pattern worth noticing. Resistance rarely shows up when you’re about to do something easy. Nobody feels deep dread before scrolling a feed.

It shows up when something matters.

The novelist Steven Pressfield built a whole book, The War of Art, around this idea. His observation was that the more important an action is to our growth, the more resistance we’ll feel toward doing it. In that sense, resistance works almost like a compass. The needle swings hardest toward the thing you most need to do.

That doesn’t mean every hesitation is a hidden calling. Sometimes your gut says no for good reason. But it does mean the presence of resistance isn’t automatically a red flag. Often, it’s a green one in disguise.

The Stoics knew this too. Marcus Aurelius wrote in his Meditations: “The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way.” The obstacle isn’t separate from the path. It’s part of it.

How to Use Resistance as an Ally

Changing your relationship with friction doesn’t happen through one big decision. It happens through small, practical shifts.

1. Name It Out Loud

Resistance loses some of its grip once you see it clearly. When you notice yourself avoiding something, say it plainly: This is resistance. Not laziness. Not a character flaw. Just a normal human response to something that feels significant.

2. Ask What It’s Protecting

Resistance usually guards something. Your pride. Your comfort. A fear of being seen. A fear of failing in front of others. Ask yourself, gently: What am I afraid will happen if I do this? The answer is often smaller in daylight than it felt in the dark.

3. Shrink the First Step

Resistance is strongest at the threshold. So make the threshold tiny. Don’t write the chapter—write one sentence. Don’t overhaul your health—walk for ten minutes. Don’t plan the entire conversation—send the text that says “Can we talk this week?” Momentum is easier to keep than to start.

4. Separate Discomfort from Danger

Your body can’t always tell the difference between a speech and a threat. Racing heart, tight chest, sweaty hands—they feel the same either way. Remind yourself: uncomfortable is not the same as unsafe. You can feel nervous and still move forward.

5. Mine Criticism for Value

External resistance often arrives as feedback. Even unfair criticism can contain something useful. Look for the part that’s true, take it, and leave the rest. That’s how pushback becomes refinement.

6. Track the Pattern

For one week, jot down each moment you hesitate. What were you about to do? What did you tell yourself? What did you do next? Patterns show up quickly. And once you can see the pattern, you can interrupt it.

What Working With Resistance Looks Like in Real Life

Ideas are easy to agree with. Living them is harder. Here are a few everyday places where the shift shows up.

At Work

You’ve been asked to present to senior leadership. Your first instinct is to over-prepare, rewrite the slides five times, and secretly hope the meeting gets cancelled. Working with resistance looks different. You notice the dread, admit it’s there because the stakes feel high, prepare well enough, and then rehearse out loud with one trusted colleague. The nerves don’t vanish. But they turn into focus instead of avoidance.

In Relationships

There’s a conversation you’ve been putting off. Maybe a friend hurt you, or a partner keeps crossing a line. Every time you think about raising it, you feel your stomach drop. That drop is resistance—and it’s also a sign the relationship matters to you. Instead of waiting for the perfect moment, you choose a calm one. You lead with care. The conversation is awkward. It’s also the reason the relationship gets stronger.

With Your Health

You want to move more, but every evening the couch wins. Rather than fighting yourself with grand promises, you set out your walking shoes by the door and commit to just putting them on. Most nights, once the shoes are on, you go. Some nights you don’t. Either way, you’ve made friends with the friction rather than declaring war on it.

In Creative Work

The blank page might be the purest form of resistance there is. Writers, artists, and makers of every kind feel it. The people who keep creating aren’t the ones who stopped feeling it. They’re the ones who showed up anyway, at the same time, in the same place, until showing up became part of who they are.

When Resistance Is Telling You Something Else

Treating resistance as an ally doesn’t mean pushing through every no your body or mind gives you. Sometimes friction is wisdom.

A few questions help tell the difference:

  • Does this go against my values? Resistance to something that violates who you are deserves to be heard.
  • Am I exhausted, or afraid? Rest is a real need. Fear dressed as tiredness is a different thing.
  • Is the resistance about the action, or about the outcome? Fear of how it might go is normal. A deep sense that the action itself is wrong is worth listening to.
  • Would I regret not trying? If the answer is yes, that’s usually a sign to lean in.

Not every road is meant to be uphill. Sometimes the right move really is the easier one—I explore that balance in finding the path of least resistance. The skill is learning which kind of friction you’re facing.

Common Mistakes People Make With Resistance

Waiting until it disappears. It rarely does. Most people who do brave things still feel the fear. They just stop waiting for it to leave first.

Fighting it with shame. Calling yourself lazy or weak tends to add more resistance, not less. Curiosity works better than criticism.

Expecting one victory to end it. Resistance returns. Every new level brings new friction. That’s not failure. It’s a sign you’re still growing.

Mistaking perfection for progress. Polishing forever is a sneaky form of avoidance. Done and imperfect beats perfect and imaginary. If that one hits home, progress, not perfection is worth a read.

A Reflection Prompt

Take five minutes with a notebook and answer these honestly:

  1. What am I resisting most right now?
  2. What might it be pointing me toward?
  3. What is the smallest possible step I could take today?

You don’t have to act on the answers immediately. Just seeing them clearly is a start.

Let the Friction Work for You

Resistance will keep showing up. At the start of new projects. At the edge of new relationships. Every time you step into the unknown.

You can keep treating it as a foe and spend your energy fighting it. Or you can start seeing resistance as an ally—a signal that something matters, and a force that makes you stronger every time you meet it.

The weight doesn’t get lighter.

You get stronger.
