There’s a moment right before you jump. Your feet are still on solid ground. You’ve done the thinking. You’ve made the lists. And still, nothing in you feels certain.

That moment is where the leap of faith meaning really lives. Not in the jump itself. In the decision to go when the evidence runs out.

I’ve stood in that moment. I stood there longer than I’d like to admit, walking away from something that looked safe on paper toward something that felt truer but came with no guarantees. I waited for a sign. I waited to feel ready. Eventually, I realized readiness wasn’t coming. I jumped anyway.

Here’s what the phrase actually means, why it’s so hard to do, and the three things that leap taught me that I still carry.

Leap of Faith Meaning: A Simple Definition

A leap of faith is a decision to believe in something or act on it without proof that it will work out. It’s choosing to move forward when you’ve gathered all the information you reasonably can and certainty still isn’t available.

It isn’t recklessness. It isn’t ignoring risk. It’s what happens after logic has done all it can. The spreadsheets are finished. The pros and cons are written. And there’s still a gap between where you are and where you hope to be. Faith, in this sense, means trust—in your preparation, your values, and your ability to handle what comes.

Where the Phrase Comes From

The idea of a leap of faith is closely associated with the 19th-century Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard. He argued that some of life’s deepest commitments—especially religious faith—can’t be reached by reason alone. At some point, you have to commit personally, without complete proof.

Kierkegaard also wrote one of my favorite reflections on uncertainty, often quoted from his journals: life can only be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards. That captures the tension perfectly. We want to understand before we act. Life asks us to act first and understand later.

Over time, the phrase moved beyond philosophy and religion into everyday speech. Today we use it for career changes, moves across the country, new relationships, creative projects, and any choice where the outcome can’t be known in advance.

What a Leap of Faith Looks Like in Everyday Life

We often picture a leap of faith as something dramatic—quitting a job overnight, selling everything, moving abroad. Sometimes it is. More often, it’s quieter.

  • Saying “I love you” first, without knowing what you’ll hear back.
  • Applying for the role you’re not sure you’re qualified for.
  • Sharing your writing, art, or idea with people whose opinion matters to you.
  • Ending something that no longer fits before you know what comes next.
  • Asking for help when you’ve always been the one who handles everything.

Each of these carries the same core leap of faith meaning: you act before you can know how it will turn out. Big or small, the inner experience is surprisingly similar. The racing heart. The second-guessing. The strange calm that sometimes arrives once the choice is made.

Often, a leap begins as a small idea that won’t leave you alone. I wrote about that early stage in I had an epiphany—the slow courtship between noticing a possibility and finally acting on it.

A Leap of Faith Is Not the Same as a Blind Jump

This distinction matters, because the phrase gets misused both ways. Some people use “leap of faith” to justify impulsive decisions. Others avoid every leap because they think it means being careless.

A real leap of faith usually has these qualities:

  • You’ve done your homework. You’ve gathered information and thought it through.
  • It’s aligned with your values. It moves you toward who you want to be.
  • You’ve considered the downside. You know what you’d do if it doesn’t work out.
  • The remaining uncertainty can’t be removed by more thinking. Only action will tell you what’s next.

A blind jump skips the first three. A leap of faith honors them—and then goes anyway.

The 3 Things I Learned When I Finally Jumped

Here’s what stayed with me after I stopped waiting and took the leap.

1. Readiness Comes After the Leap, Not Before

I kept waiting to feel ready. To feel confident. To feel like the kind of person who does this sort of thing.

That feeling never came first. It came after.

Confidence, I learned, isn’t a prerequisite for action. It’s a result of it. You become the person who can handle the new situation by being in the new situation. The first weeks were wobbly. Then, slowly, they weren’t. The skills I thought I needed before I started were the skills I built by starting.

If you’re waiting to feel ready, you may be waiting for something that only shows up once you’re already moving.

2. The Net Appears in Unexpected Ways

There’s an old saying: “Leap, and the net will appear.” I used to find it a little too neat. Life doesn’t hand out safety nets on request.

But here’s what I found. The net didn’t appear the way I expected. It wasn’t a single rescue. It was a series of small supports I hadn’t been able to see from the edge. A friend who made an introduction. A skill from an old job that turned out to be exactly what I needed. A quiet kind of resilience I didn’t know I had.

When you’re standing still, you can only see the risks. Once you move, you start to see the resources too.

3. Staying Put Was Never Actually Safe

This was the hardest lesson, and the most important.

I had framed my choice as risk versus safety. Leap and risk everything, or stay and keep what I had. But that framing was wrong. Staying carried its own risks—they were just quieter. The risk of shrinking. Of growing resentful. Of looking back years later and wondering what might have happened.

Not leaping is also a decision. It just doesn’t feel like one because nothing visibly changes. But something does change. Slowly, the life you’re living drifts further from the life you meant to live. If that sounds familiar, the signs I describe in what are the signs of having a calling might resonate.

How to Know If You’re Ready to Take a Leap

There’s no perfect test. But a few honest questions can help you tell the difference between a leap worth taking and a jump worth reconsidering.

  1. Have I done the reasonable research? Not endless research—enough to make an informed choice.
  2. Does this align with my values? Would the person I want to become choose this?
  3. Can I survive the worst realistic outcome? Not comfortably, maybe—but survive and recover?
  4. Am I waiting for information, or for courage? If it’s courage, more time probably won’t help.
  5. Will I regret not trying? Picture yourself five years from now. What does that version of you wish you’d done?

If most of your answers point forward, the remaining fear may simply be the normal cost of doing something that matters.

Practical Ways to Make the Leap Easier

A leap of faith doesn’t have to be one giant, terrifying jump. You can build a smaller bridge.

  • Test before you commit. Try the new career on the side. Visit the city before you move. Volunteer before you switch fields.
  • Build a cushion. Savings, a backup plan, or a support network can turn a terrifying leap into a manageable one.
  • Set a date. Open-ended “someday” plans tend to stay someday. Pick a real date to decide or act.
  • Tell someone you trust. Saying your intention out loud makes it more real and gives you a source of encouragement.
  • Expect a wobble. The first stretch after any leap is usually uncomfortable. That’s not a sign you were wrong. It’s a sign you’re learning.

Every leap has an edge you can’t see past. I wrote more about that threshold in going into the unknown, where so much of our growth begins.

Reflection Prompts Before You Jump

If you’re standing at the edge of something right now, take a few quiet minutes with these:

  • What leap keeps coming back to my mind, no matter how often I push it away?
  • What am I most afraid of losing?
  • What might I gain that I can’t see yet?
  • What’s one small step I could take this week to test the water?

You don’t have to answer them all at once. Sometimes just writing the question down is the beginning of the leap.

The Edge Is Where It Starts

The leap of faith meaning, in the end, is simple: you won’t get certainty first. You’ll get it, if at all, on the other side.

That’s uncomfortable. It’s also freeing. Because it means you can stop waiting for a feeling that was never going to arrive on schedule.

Do the thinking. Honor the risks. Listen to your values.

And then, when there’s nothing left to figure out from where you’re standing—jump.
