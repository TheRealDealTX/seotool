A friend once asked me a question over coffee that I couldn’t answer: “If you lost everything tomorrow, what would you actually rebuild first?”

I started listing things. My house. My work. My routines. Then I stopped, because none of those were really the answer. The answer was quieter. A handful of people. My health. A sense that my days meant something.

That conversation sent me looking at what is essential in life, not in theory, but in my own calendar, closet, and heart. What I found surprised me. Most of what filled my days wasn’t essential at all.

If you’ve ever felt busy but unfulfilled, full but somehow empty, this question is worth sitting with.

What Is Essential in Life?

What is essential in life is the small set of things you couldn’t lose without losing yourself: the relationships, values, health, and sense of purpose that make everything else worth having. Beyond basic needs like food, shelter, and safety, essentials are deeply personal. They’re what remains when you strip away everything you do out of habit, obligation, or comparison.

In The Little Prince, Antoine de Saint-Exupéry gives the fox a line that has stayed with readers for generations: “It is only with the heart that one can see rightly; what is essential is invisible to the eye.”

I think that’s exactly right. The essentials are rarely the things we can photograph or buy. They’re the things we feel.

Why the Essentials Get Buried

Nobody sets out to neglect what matters most. It happens slowly.

A new commitment here. A subscription there. A few more hours at work because it’s “just this season.” A habit of scrolling that started as a break and became a default. Each choice seems harmless. Together, they bury the essentials under layers of the merely urgent or the merely available.

There are a few reasons this happens to almost all of us:

  • Everything is marketed as essential. Products, apps, opportunities. When everything claims to be necessary, it gets hard to tell what truly is.
  • Urgent beats important. The ringing phone wins over the quiet walk with your partner, almost every time.
  • We inherit other people’s priorities. We chase what our parents, peers, or culture told us mattered without checking whether it matters to us.
  • We assume there will be time later. “Someday” is where many essentials go to wait.

If you’ve been feeling stretched thin, it may help to look honestly at your life’s bandwidth. The essentials need room, and room doesn’t appear on its own.

How to Find What Truly Matters to You

There’s no universal list. Your essentials won’t look exactly like mine. But a few questions can help you uncover them.

1. Imagine Losing It

Go through the major parts of your life: people, work, home, health, habits, possessions. For each one, ask: If this disappeared tomorrow, how would I feel?

Relief? It’s probably not essential. Mild inconvenience? Probably nice, maybe important. Devastation? You’ve found an essential.

2. Look at Your Best Days

Think back to a few days in the past year that felt genuinely good. Not impressive. Good. What were you doing? Who were you with? What was missing?

Patterns emerge quickly. For many people, the best days involve connection, nature, meaningful work, or creative play, and they rarely involve the things we spend the most money on.

3. Ask What You’d Protect

If you had to cut your weekly commitments in half, what would you fight to keep? That instinct is information. What you’d protect under pressure is usually what you value most.

4. Follow the Ache

Sometimes our essentials show up as longing. The friend you miss. The instrument gathering dust. The faith practice you drifted from. The sense of calm you remember from years ago. An ache often points to something essential that’s been neglected.

5. Write Your Short List

Once you’ve reflected, write down no more than five to seven essentials. Keep the list short on purpose. If everything is essential, nothing is. A short list is a decision-making tool. A long list is just a wish.

Essentials Across the Areas of Your Life

It can help to look at what is essential in life one area at a time. Big questions get easier when you make them smaller.

Relationships

Most of us have a few people who are truly load-bearing. The ones you’d call at 2 a.m. The ones who knew you before and will know you after. Name them. Then ask honestly: do they get my time, or just my good intentions?

Work

Work may not feel essential, but what it gives you might be: security, growth, contribution, a sense of craft. Separate the job from the need. If the need is meaning, you may find it in more than one place.

Home

A home doesn’t need to be large or perfect. It needs to be a place where you can rest. Look around your space and notice what supports rest and what quietly demands attention. Often, the essential things in a home are a good bed, a table where people gather, and a little order.

Your Inner Life

This is the area we neglect first and miss most. Quiet. Reflection. Faith or spiritual practice, if that’s part of your life. Time to think your own thoughts. It doesn’t take much, but it does take intention. Without it, even a full life starts to feel thin.

Living From Your Essentials

Knowing what is essential in life is the first step. Living from it is the harder, more rewarding one.

Here are a few ways to let your short list actually shape your days:

  • Schedule the essentials first. Put the walk, the call, the family dinner on the calendar before anything else claims the space.
  • Use your list as a filter. Before saying yes to a new commitment, ask: Does this serve one of my essentials, or compete with them?
  • Do a gentle subtraction. Each month, remove one thing that isn’t essential: a subscription, a commitment, a cluttered drawer, a habit that drains you.
  • Protect your energy, not just your time. Give your best hours to your essentials, not your leftovers.
  • Revisit the list seasonally. What’s essential at 30 may shift at 50. Let the list grow with you.

Sometimes this means doing less overall. Less of this, more of that. It’s an idea I return to often in more of this, less of that, and it’s really the practical heart of essential living.

Common Mistakes When Simplifying

Confusing essentialism with deprivation. Focusing on essentials isn’t about owning nothing or enjoying nothing. It’s about making room for what matters most. Joy is often essential.

Starting with stuff instead of values. Decluttering a closet feels productive, but if you haven’t clarified your values, the clutter just comes back. Start inside, then work outward.

Copying someone else’s list. Minimalist influencers, productivity gurus, and well-meaning friends can offer ideas. But only you can decide what’s essential to you.

Treating it as a one-time project. Non-essentials creep back in constantly. Living from your essentials is an ongoing practice, not a weekend makeover.

A Simple Truth About Simple Things

When I finally wrote my own short list, it was almost embarrassingly simple. A few people. My health. Work that feels meaningful. Time outside. Quiet mornings.

None of it was new information. I had always known these things mattered. What changed was that I started treating them like they did.

There’s real power in the significance of simple things. The essentials are rarely complicated. They’re just easy to overlook.

Reflection Prompts

  1. What would I rebuild first if I lost everything?
  2. Which part of my life gets my best energy, and does it deserve it?
  3. What’s one non-essential thing I could remove this month?
  4. What essential thing have I been postponing until “someday”?
  5. If my calendar reflected my true values, what would change?

So, What Is Essential to You?

It’s a question worth asking more than once. Ask it when you’re overwhelmed. Ask it at the start of each year. Ask it on a quiet Sunday when the house is still.

You don’t need to get it perfect the first time. Your first short list might be messy. That’s fine. The act of asking, honestly and regularly, is what slowly brings your days back into line with your heart.

The answer probably won’t be something you can buy. It might not even be something you can see.

But once you know it, you’ll find it easier to say no to what doesn’t matter, and a wholehearted yes to what does.
