Six lines that decide most sanctuary claims — and the six that nobody checks until the roof is already open.

The valuation problem

The building was never worth what the schedule says.

Most Texas churches are insured on a statement of values that was assembled once, at the time of a refinance or a building campaign, and then rolled forward with a small annual inflation factor. Meanwhile the congregation added a family life center, enclosed a breezeway, bought the building next door for the pre-school, and replaced the sound and lighting package twice.

That gap does not matter at all until there is a loss. Then it matters in two ways at once. It caps what the policy can pay, and — if the form carries a coinsurance clause, which most church forms do — it reduces the payment on every loss by the ratio of the limit carried to the limit required. A partial roof claim on a badly scheduled sanctuary gets cut by the same percentage as a total loss would.

The first thing we do on a church file is establish what the replacement value actually is, on today's costs, for a building of that construction. Sometimes that strengthens the claim. Sometimes it tells the board something uncomfortable they needed to know two years ago. Either way it is better established by us in week one than by the carrier's consultant in month seven.

Functional replacement cost

Older sanctuaries are frequently written on a functional replacement cost basis rather than true RCV. That wording lets the carrier rebuild with modern equivalent materials instead of matching plaster, millwork or leaded glass. It is not automatically wrong — but it is a completely different number, and it should be a decision the church made, not one it discovered.

Denominational programs

Churches insured through a denominational or church-specialty program often have better wording than the open market — agreed value, replacement cost on contents, generous debris removal — and no one at the church has ever read it. The endorsement list is where the money is.

What we do

From the first tarp to the last release.

Read the policy with the trustees

Declarations, forms, endorsements and the statement of values, in one sitting, with whoever on the board will have to answer for the decision. Most churches have never had the policy explained to them by someone without a commission on the renewal.

Document before the volunteers clean

Congregations are helpful, and that is the problem. Within seventy-two hours of a loss the wet carpet is in a dumpster and the ceiling tiles are gone. We photograph, measure and moisture-map first, then tell you what is safe to move.

Build a sanctuary-specific estimate

Line-item, in the same platform the carrier uses, with specialist trades quoted rather than guessed and code work carried as its own schedule so it cannot be folded into the general allowance.

Negotiate, and report to the board

Every position in writing, every deadline cited, and a plain-English summary the church can put in front of a business meeting without a translator.

Also relevant

Church schools, camps and parsonages sit on the same policy — and get forgotten.

If the organization owns it, the claim should scope it. We routinely find outbuildings, portable classrooms, playground structures, fellowship halls and church-owned housing on the schedule that nobody inspected after the storm.

  • Church-operated schools, pre-schools and mother’s-day-out programs, including their revenue interruption.
  • Gymnasiums and family life centers — floors, backstops, HVAC and roof systems.
  • Parsonages and church-owned residential property, which may be scheduled under a different valuation basis.
  • Camps, retreat centers and cemetery structures, frequently in a different county from the main campus.
  • Detached storage, bus barns and maintenance buildings where hail damage shows earliest.
