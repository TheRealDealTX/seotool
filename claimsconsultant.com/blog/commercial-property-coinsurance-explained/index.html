Policy wording

Coinsurance: the
clause nobody reads

A church discovers it after a hailstorm. A district discovers it after a fire. The damage is covered, the limit is nowhere near exhausted, and the payment is still cut by a third. This is how that happens.

Published June 11, 2026 9 min read By the Claims Consultant claims desk

Coinsurance is the most consequential clause in commercial property insurance that almost no policyholder can describe. It sits on the declarations page as a percentage next to each building’s limit — 80%, 90%, sometimes 100%, and it does nothing at all until there is a claim.

What it actually does

The clause requires you to carry insurance of at least the stated percentage of the property’s value. If you do, losses are paid normally. If you do not, every covered loss is reduced by the ratio of what you carried to what you should have carried.

The arithmetic is unforgiving and simple:

The coinsurance formula
StepCalculation
Required insuranceProperty value at the date of loss × coinsurance percentage
Recovery ratioLimit carried ÷ required insurance
Loss payableAmount of loss × recovery ratio, less the deductible

Work an example. A sanctuary would cost $24 million to rebuild today. The policy carries a $16 million limit with a 90% coinsurance clause. Required insurance is $21.6 million; the ratio is 74%. A $3.2 million hail and water loss (well within the limit, entirely covered) pays $2.37 million before the deductible. The other $830,000 is uninsured, not because of anything to do with the damage, but because of a number on a schedule.

The critical detail

The test is applied at the date of loss, using the property’s value then. Not the value when the policy was bound, not the value in the appraisal from the last refinance. Construction costs move; the required amount moves with them, and the limit does not.

Why Texas institutions fail it so consistently

Three reasons, and none of them involve negligence.

Statements of value go stale

Most institutional schedules were assembled once, for a specific reason, a bond issue, a refinance, a merger, and have been rolled forward with a small annual inflation factor ever since. That factor has not matched what happened to commercial construction costs in Texas since 2020.

Buildings change and schedules do not

A congregation adds a family life center. A district encloses a breezeway and buys the adjacent property. A city converts a warehouse into a maintenance facility. Each of those changes the replacement value; none of them automatically reaches the statement of values.

Nobody owns the number

The broker reports what the client supplies. The client assumes the broker checks. The board approves a renewal summary that shows premium, not adequacy. The value sits unexamined until a claim tests it, at which point the carrier examines it very carefully indeed.

Three ways out, in order

Agreed value

An agreed value endorsement suspends the coinsurance clause for the policy term in exchange for a signed statement of values the insurer accepts. It is the cleanest solution available, it is commonly less expensive than people expect, and a surprising number of institutions already have it and do not know. Look at your declarations page before you do anything else.

Revalue and raise the limits

A professional valuation, or at minimum a current cost-per-square-foot review by construction class and occupancy. Then adjust the limits at renewal. It costs premium, and it costs far less than a penalty applied to every claim for the next five years.

Contest the value at claim time

The fallback, and a legitimate one. The required amount depends on the property’s actual value at the date of loss, which is a question of evidence instead of the carrier’s assertion. Where a penalty is being applied on a valuation that overstates what the building would really cost to reproduce, that valuation is contestable, and on older or unusual buildings it is often wrong.

Two adjacent traps

Blanket coverage feels like the answer, and mostly it is: a blanket limit responds anywhere on the schedule instead of trapping recovery at one building’s value. But blanket cover is usually conditioned on the accuracy of the reported values, and a margin clause or occurrence limit of liability endorsement can cap recovery at a fixed percentage, commonly 110% or 115%, of the value reported for the affected location. Understate the value and the blanket limit above it becomes decorative.

The second trap is functional replacement cost, which appears often on older churches and civic buildings. It permits the carrier to rebuild with modern equivalent materials rather than matching what was there. It is not automatically wrong — for some buildings it is sensible and cheaper, but it should be a decision the organization made knowingly.

The cheapest hour in commercial insurance is the one spent reading the declarations page while it can still be changed.From the claims desk

What to do this week

  • Find the declarations page. Look at the figure next to each building’s limit. If it says 80, 90 or 100, the clause is live.
  • If it says agreed value, confirm the signed statement of values is current. That is the condition of the suspension.
  • Get a current replacement cost per square foot for your construction class and occupancy, and multiply it out.
  • Compare that figure against the limit, and apply the percentage. Now you know your ratio.
  • If there is a shortfall, raise it with your broker at least 90 days before renewal, when something can still be done about it.

This is the rare insurance problem with a simple fix, available only in advance. After a loss, all that remains is the argument.
