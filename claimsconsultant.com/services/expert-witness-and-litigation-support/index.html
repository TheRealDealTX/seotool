The standard

Reliability is a method question, not a credentials question.

Texas Rule of Evidence 702 lets a qualified expert give opinion testimony where it will help the trier of fact, and Texas courts test that opinion for reliability and relevance. In practice the challenge is rarely aimed at whether the witness is qualified. It is aimed at the gap between the data and the conclusion — whether the method can be described, whether it was followed, and whether someone else applying it would reach the same place.

That has consequences for how a file is built long before anyone files anything. Measurements have to be recorded, not remembered. Assumptions have to be stated as assumptions. Photographs have to be indexed to locations. Where a conclusion rests on something we did not personally observe, the report has to say so.

The corollary is that we will not write an opinion we cannot defend. If the evidence does not support the position the retaining party wants, we say so in the first conversation, in writing if it helps, and the engagement ends there. That is a cheaper outcome than a withdrawn expert in month nine.

The credibility ledger

Every expert accumulates a record: who retained them, what they concluded, how often those two correlate. Ours is mixed, because a witness whose opinions always favor the paying side is an advocate with a CV. Prior engagements and testimony are disclosed on request, and to opposing counsel as a matter of course.

Working through counsel

On matters heading to litigation, engagement through counsel instead of directly through the party is common. How that affects privilege, work product and discoverability is a question for the attorney; we follow whatever structure they set and keep our file accordingly.

Scope of opinion

What we will and will not opine on.

  • Will: the extent of physical damage, the scope of work required to repair it, the reasonable cost of that work, and whether an opposing estimate is complete.
  • Will: causation within our competence, hail versus wear, wind versus water, freeze versus long-term seepage, impact versus settlement.
  • Will: the period reasonably required to repair, and the documentary basis of a time-element calculation.
  • Will not: interpret the policy or opine on coverage. That is counsel’s work and a court’s.
  • Will not: opine on structural engineering, industrial hygiene or accounting where those require a separate licensed discipline. We identify what is needed and work alongside it.
  • Will not: accept an engagement where the conclusion is specified in advance.
