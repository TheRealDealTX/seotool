Depreciation is straight-line against the service life you entered and capped at 75%, which is roughly where carriers stop sustaining it on maintained commercial property. This is a planning figure, not an estimate: a real scope is built line by line.

Reading the output

Three numbers, and the gap between them.

Every replacement cost policy pays a large loss in at least two installments. The first is actual cash value: the cost to repair, less depreciation, less the deductible. The second is the recoverable depreciation, released after the work is finished and documented. Institutions get into trouble when they budget from the first check and discover the rebuild costs the full amount.

The third number, the one this tool cannot compute, is what the carrier will offer. That depends on how the scope is documented, whether overhead and profit are included, how code upgrades are treated and whether anyone has argued for the parts of the building the adjuster did not inspect.

Inputs that matter most

Two fields move the answer more than the rest combined.

  • Replacement cost per square foot. Using an outdated figure understates everything downstream, including the coinsurance test. Current Texas commercial construction costs vary enormously by occupancy, a warehouse shell and a hospital wing are not comparable.
  • Deductible basis. A flat $50,000 and a 2% of values deductible look similar in a conversation and are wildly different in a claim. Switch the selector and watch the bottom line move.
  • Code upgrade percentage. On any building older than about twenty years, this is real and often substantial. It is also capped by your ordinance and law limit, which is often far lower than the exposure.
  • Service life. Depreciation is straight-line here. Real adjusters argue effective age against condition, and a well-maintained system deserves credit for it.
