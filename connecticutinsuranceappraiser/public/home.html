Second opinion, first-hand measurements

The Connecticut Insurance Appraiser behind the second opinion

Most short claims aren’t denials. They are estimates that skipped a roof plane, priced plaster as drywall, or left the crane out of a tree removal. Connecticut Insurance Appraiser exists for the space between “covered” and “paid in full.”

We start from our own inspection, never from an edited copy of the carrier’s file. Then we price what we can defend, because an umpire rewards credible numbers and discounts inflated ones. When a claim needs an attorney or a public adjuster instead, a Connecticut Insurance Appraiser conversation will tell you that on the first call.

  • Independent scope. Measured and photographed by us.
  • Connecticut pricing. Line items priced for your ZIP code.
  • Plain reporting. Every difference explained in writing.

Storm almanac

A storm almanac for Connecticut property owners

A Connecticut Insurance Appraiser reads every file against the storm that caused it. These are the events that still shape how carriers, contractors and panels think about damage here.

  1. 1938

    The Great New England Hurricane

    Struck without warning on September 21 and reshaped the shoreline from Old Saybrook to Stonington. Still the benchmark for Connecticut wind and surge.

  2. 1955

    The August floods

    Remnants of Connie and Diane sent the Naugatuck, Farmington and Quinebaug rivers through Waterbury, Torrington, Winsted and Putnam.

  3. 1979

    Windsor Locks tornado

    An October tornado tore through Windsor and Windsor Locks, one of the strongest ever recorded in New England.

  4. 1985

    Hurricane Gloria

    Crossed the coast near Milford in September, toppling trees and knocking out power across most of the state.

  5. 1989

    Hamden tornado

    A July tornado cut through Hamden and parts of New Haven, damaging hundreds of homes.

  6. 2011

    Irene, then the October nor’easter

    Irene battered the shoreline in August. Two months later, heavy snow on leafed-out trees caused record outages and tree damage inland.

  7. 2012

    Superstorm Sandy

    Record surge flooded shoreline neighborhoods from Greenwich to East Haven and beyond.

  8. 2013

    February blizzard

    Two to three feet of snow buried much of the state; Hamden measured about 40 inches.

  9. 2018

    May 15 tornadoes

    Several tornadoes and a macroburst crossed New Haven and Fairfield counties in one afternoon.

  10. 2020

    Tropical Storm Isaias

    Gusts brought down trees and wires in nearly every town, with outages lasting a week in places.

  11. 2021

    Henri and Ida

    Henri threatened the coast in August; weeks later Ida’s remnants caused flash flooding inland.

The process

From demand letter to two signatures

The appraisal clause turns an open-ended argument into eight defined steps with a binding result. This is how a Connecticut Insurance Appraiser file moves.

Read the process guide
  1. 1

    Pin down the disagreement

    Confirm coverage is accepted and that the dispute is about the amount of loss: scope, price, matching or the value of contents.

  2. 2

    Read the appraisal condition

    Find the appraisal paragraph in your policy and note who may demand it, how, and how many days each side has to name an appraiser.

  3. 3

    Send the written demand

    Demand appraisal in writing, identify the claim and the disputed amount, and keep proof of delivery.

  4. 4

    Name the appraisers

    Each side names a competent and disinterested appraiser within the policy’s window and tells the other side who it is.

  5. 5

    Select the umpire

    The two appraisers agree on a neutral umpire. If they can’t, either side can usually ask a court to appoint one.

  6. 6

    Inspect, compare and negotiate

    The appraisers inspect, exchange estimates and resolve as many line items as they can between themselves.

  7. 7

    Submit open items to the umpire

    Anything still in dispute goes to the umpire with photos, measurements and pricing support from both appraisers.

  8. 8

    Sign the award and get paid

    An award signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss. The carrier then pays under the policy’s terms.

FAQ

Questions people ask a Connecticut Insurance Appraiser

Straight answers on how appraisal works, what it costs and when it is the wrong move.

All FAQs Claim glossary
What does a Connecticut Insurance Appraiser do?

A Connecticut Insurance Appraiser values property damage when you and your insurer agree the loss is covered but disagree about the cost. As your named appraiser we inspect, write an independent line-item estimate, work with the carrier’s appraiser and, if needed, present open items to a neutral umpire.

How is an appraiser different from a public adjuster?

A public adjuster manages and negotiates the whole claim, usually for a percentage. An appraiser works inside the policy’s appraisal clause on one question: the amount of loss. We don’t negotiate outside appraisal and we don’t give legal advice.

When is it worth calling a Connecticut Insurance Appraiser?

When coverage is accepted, you have the carrier’s estimate, and the gap between it and a qualified contractor’s number is meaningful, often more than about 10% or several thousand dollars.

Is the appraisal award binding?

On the amount of loss, yes, once two of the three panel members sign. That is why documentation should be in place before appraisal is demanded.

What does it cost?

Each side pays its own appraiser and usually half of any umpire fee. We put our fee in writing before any work begins, and the first conversation is free.

Do you cover my town?

Yes. We work in all 169 Connecticut towns and cities, in every one of the state’s eight counties.
