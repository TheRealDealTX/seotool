Each of those storms produced a wave of insurance claims. And in each one, I've seen the same pattern: the homeowners who knew the process going in recovered faster and more completely than the ones learning it on the fly.

This guide walks through a hurricane claim in order, from the weeks before a storm to the final payment. It's written for Sugar Land homeowners, but most of it applies anywhere in Fort Bend County.

Every policy is different, so treat this as a practical roadmap, not legal advice.

Step 1: Prepare Before the Storm Arrives

The best time to work on a hurricane claim is before there's anything to claim. A little preparation in May or June saves real money in September.

Read your declarations page and deductibles

Pull out your declarations page and look for your deductibles. Many Texas homeowners policies carry a separate deductible for named storms or for wind and hail, and it is often a percentage rather than a flat dollar amount.

That percentage is usually calculated on your Coverage A (dwelling) limit—not on the size of your loss. So a 2% wind/hail deductible on a home insured for $400,000 means you absorb the first $8,000 of covered wind damage. Plenty of homeowners don't realize this until the adjuster's letter arrives.

Build a home inventory

If a storm opens up your roof and soaks the contents of your home, you'll be asked to list what was damaged, when you bought it, and what it cost. That's nearly impossible from memory. Walk through each room with your phone and record video, open closets and drawers, and save receipts for larger purchases. Store your documents somewhere safe

Keep a digital copy of your full policy (not just the declarations page), your inventory, and photos of the home's current condition in cloud storage. If you have to evacuate, you want access from anywhere.

Step 2: Know Which Policy Covers What

This is where Fort Bend County differs from the coast, and it matters.

Wind is usually on your homeowners policy

Fort Bend County is outside the coastal territory served by the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA). That means wind damage here is generally covered by your regular homeowners policy, subject to that wind/hail or named-storm deductible. You typically won't have a separate windstorm policy to deal with.

Flood is a separate policy

Standard homeowners policies exclude flood—rising water from rivers, bayous, or surface water. Flood coverage comes from the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood insurer. If Harvey taught this region anything, it's that homes outside mapped flood zones flood too.

Timing matters here: new NFIP policies generally have a 30-day waiting period before coverage starts. Buying flood insurance when a storm appears in the Gulf is usually too late. If you need it, buy it well before the season heats up. Our flood damage claims page explains how those claims differ from wind claims.

Step 3: The First 72 Hours After the Storm

Once it's safe, the first three days set the tone for the entire claim.

  1. Put safety first. Watch for downed lines, gas smells, and compromised structures. Don't go into standing water near electrical panels.
  2. Photograph before you touch anything. Wide shots of each elevation of the house, then close-ups. Get the roof from the ground, the fence, the yard, and every interior room with damage.
  3. Start mitigation. Your policy almost certainly requires you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Tarp the roof, board broken windows, pull out wet carpet and pad, and get fans or a water mitigation company running.
  4. Keep every receipt. Tarps, plywood, fans, generator fuel, a mitigation company invoice, hotel nights—all of it.
  5. Save damaged items when practical. Don't throw out damaged property until it's documented. If something has to go for health reasons, photograph it first, with a label or tag showing the brand and model.

Step 4: Document the Damage Like You'll Need to Prove It

Because you will.

Insurers pay what they can see and verify. A dated, organized record beats a few blurry photos every time.

  • Use date-stamped photos and video, and keep the original files.
  • Photograph the cause and the result: missing shingles on the roof and the stained drywall below them.
  • Measure and note water lines if flooding occurred—this matters for separating flood from wind-driven rain.
  • Write a short timeline: when the wind picked up, when you noticed the leak, when the power went out.
  • Save weather data for your address and the storm's dates.

Hidden damage—wet insulation, swelling subfloors, moisture in wall cavities—often shows up weeks later. Document it as you find it.

Step 5: Report the Claim and Handle the Recorded Statement

Report the claim promptly. Most policies require "prompt" notice, and delays give an insurer an argument you don't want them to have. Write down your claim number, the name of everyone you speak with, and the date of each call.

About recorded statements

The insurer may ask for a recorded statement early in the process. Your policy may require you to cooperate, but you can prepare. Before the call:

  • Review your timeline so dates are accurate.
  • Stick to facts you actually know. "I don't know" is a perfectly good answer.
  • Don't guess about how long a leak existed or speculate about causes.
  • Ask for a copy of the recording or transcript.

Step 6: Prepare for the Adjuster Inspection

After a major hurricane, carriers bring in large numbers of field adjusters, often from out of state, who inspect a lot of houses in a short time.

Be there for the inspection. Walk the adjuster through every area of damage, including the attic and any rooms with moisture. Hand over a copy of your photos and receipts. Ask what they're including and what they're not, and ask when you'll receive the written estimate.

When the estimate arrives, read it line by line. Look for missing rooms, missing items like drip edge or underlayment, and quantities that don't match your house.

Step 7: Navigate Wind vs. Water and Other Common Disputes

The single most common fight in a Fort Bend County hurricane claim is wind versus water. If your home took both wind damage and flooding, the homeowners carrier pays for wind and the flood carrier pays for flood. Each one has an incentive to attribute damage to the other.

Good documentation is your defense: roof openings, wind-driven rain patterns, water lines, and the timeline of events. Interior ceiling damage below a damaged roof is a very different story than a water line two feet up the drywall.

Other frequent issues

  • Additional Living Expense (ALE): If a covered loss makes your home unlivable, ALE generally covers the increase in living costs—hotel or rental, extra mileage, extra meal costs. Keep receipts and compare against your normal spending.
  • Recoverable depreciation: Many replacement cost policies pay actual cash value first and release the withheld depreciation after you complete repairs. Track your repair invoices and submit them so you don't leave that money on the table.
  • Code upgrades: Repairs may trigger current building code requirements. Check whether your policy includes ordinance or law coverage.

Step 8: Understand Texas Claim Deadlines

Texas has rules that set the pace for insurers handling claims, found in the Texas Prompt Payment of Claims Act (Texas Insurance Code chapter 542). In general terms:

StageGeneral timeline
Acknowledge the claim, begin the investigation, and request needed itemsWithin 15 days after receiving notice of the claim
Accept or reject the claimWithin 15 business days after receiving all requested items; the insurer can extend by up to 45 days if it notifies you why it needs more time
Pay an accepted claimWithin 5 business days after notifying you it will pay

Those deadlines may be extended after a weather-related catastrophe or major natural disaster, which is exactly when hurricane claims are filed. Your policy also has its own deadlines, including time limits for reporting claims and for recovering depreciation. Calendar it. Write down the date you reported the claim, the date you sent each requested document, and the date the insurer received it. Those dates are your leverage if the claim stalls.

Step 9: Know When to Call a Public Adjuster

Many hurricane claims settle fairly without outside help. Others don't. It's worth bringing in a licensed public adjuster when:

  • The insurer's estimate is far below your contractor's bid
  • Part of the claim was denied as flood, wear and tear, or pre-existing damage
  • Your home has both wind and water damage and the carriers are pointing at each other
  • Contents losses or ALE are significant and you're overwhelmed with paperwork
  • Weeks pass without meaningful progress

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my homeowners policy cover hurricane damage in Sugar Land?

Wind damage from a hurricane is generally covered by a standard Fort Bend County homeowners policy, subject to its wind/hail or named-storm deductible. Fort Bend County is outside the TWIA coastal territory, so you typically won't need a separate windstorm policy. Flood damage is excluded and requires a flood policy.

How is a percentage hurricane deductible calculated?

Percentage deductibles are usually calculated on your Coverage A dwelling limit, not on the amount of damage. A 2% deductible on a home insured for $350,000 is $7,000. Check your declarations page for the exact deductible that applies.

Can I buy flood insurance right before a hurricane?

You can buy it, but new NFIP policies generally have a 30-day waiting period before coverage begins, so a policy purchased when a storm is already approaching usually won't cover that storm. Private flood policies have their own waiting periods.

Should I make repairs before the adjuster inspects?

Make temporary repairs to prevent further damage—tarping, boarding, drying out—and keep receipts. Hold off on permanent repairs until the damage has been documented and inspected, unless safety requires otherwise.

What is recoverable depreciation?

Many replacement cost policies first pay the actual cash value of the damage and hold back depreciation. Once repairs are completed and documented, you can generally claim the withheld amount. Policies often set a time limit for doing so.

How long does the insurer have to pay a hurricane claim in Texas?

Under the Texas Prompt Payment Act, insurers generally must acknowledge a claim within 15 days, accept or reject it within 15 business days after receiving requested items (with a possible extension of up to 45 days), and pay within 5 business days after accepting it. These deadlines may be extended after a weather catastrophe.

When should I hire a public adjuster for a hurricane claim?

Consider one when the estimate seems low, part of the claim is denied, wind and flood carriers disagree, or the claim is too large and complex to manage on your own. Earlier involvement usually gives more room to shape the claim.
