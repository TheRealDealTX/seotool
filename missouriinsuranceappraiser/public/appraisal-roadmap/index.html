Process guide

The Missouri Appraisal Roadmap, Stage by Stage

8 stages from the first disagreement to payment: what happens, who does what, and where claims most often go wrong.

Most Missouri homeowners and commercial property policies contain an appraisal clause: a short paragraph that gives both sides a structured way forward when they agree a loss is covered but can’t agree on what it costs to fix. Instead of trading line items for months, each side picks an appraiser, the two appraisers pick an umpire, and a written award signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss.

The stages below follow the order a typical Missouri claim moves through appraisal, from the first sign of a stalled negotiation to the check clearing. Your own policy’s wording controls every detail, including deadlines, who pays for what and whether appraisal applies to your claim at all, so keep a copy of the full policy handy as you read.

We are appraisers, not attorneys or public adjusters. Appraisal settles the dollar amount of a covered loss. It does not decide coverage, and nothing on this page is legal advice. For questions about denials, bad faith or lawsuits, talk with a Missouri attorney.

The 8 stages

  1. Pin down the disagreement
  2. Read the appraisal clause
  3. Send the written demand
  4. Each side names an appraiser
  5. Choosing the umpire
  6. Inspection and competing estimates
  7. Negotiation and the award
  8. Payment and what follows

Stage 1Pin down the disagreement

Appraisal answers one question: how much is the covered damage worth? Before you invoke it, make sure that’s really what you and the insurer are arguing about. Line up your contractor’s estimate beside the adjuster’s and mark every place they differ.

Amount disputes that fit appraisal

  • Different unit prices for the same materials or labor
  • How many squares of roofing, how much drywall or which rooms need paint
  • Repair versus replace calls on damaged components
  • Whether overhead and profit belongs in the estimate

Disputes that usually don’t fit

If the insurer says the damage isn’t covered at all, blames wear and tear, or has denied the claim outright, that’s a coverage question. Appraisal panels generally don’t decide coverage, and pushing a coverage fight into appraisal can waste months. A written denial letter is a good signal to reread your policy and consider talking with a Missouri attorney before going further.

Stage 2Read the appraisal clause

Pull the full policy, not just the declarations page, and find the section titled “Appraisal,” usually among the conditions. Read it slowly. Wording varies between insurers and even between editions of the same company’s form.

What to look for

  • Who can demand appraisal: either party, or only both by agreement
  • How the demand must be made, usually in writing
  • How many days each side has to name its appraiser, and how long the appraisers have to agree on an umpire
  • What happens if they can’t agree on an umpire, often a request to a court in the county where the property sits
  • How appraisal costs are split
  • Whether the insurer keeps the right to deny the claim after an award

Also check the conditions on proof of loss, duties after loss and the time limit for filing suit. Appraisal doesn’t always pause those clocks, so write the dates down now. If anything in the clause is unclear, a Missouri attorney can tell you how it’s likely to be read.

Stage 3Send the written demand

Once you’ve confirmed the dispute is about amount, put your request for appraisal in writing. A short, plain letter works better than a long argument. Send it to the adjuster and to the claim address in your policy or claim correspondence, and keep proof of delivery.

What a demand usually includes

  • Your name, the property address, the policy number and the claim number
  • The date of loss and the cause, such as hail or wind
  • A statement that you dispute the amount of loss and are invoking the appraisal provision
  • The name and contact details of your appraiser, if you’ve already chosen one
  • A copy of your estimate or a short summary of the gap

Stay factual and polite. The demand usually starts a clock that the policy defines, so note the date it’s received. Insurers can demand appraisal too; if you receive a demand, read the clause and respond within the stated time.

Stage 4Each side names an appraiser

Each party picks its own appraiser within the window the policy sets. Many policies say something like 20 days after the written demand is received, but yours may say something different. Missing that window can cause real problems, so treat it as a firm deadline.

Choosing yours

Most clauses require each appraiser to be “competent” and also “independent,” “impartial” or “disinterested.” Those words are read differently from case to case, but at a minimum your appraiser should understand construction pricing and avoid any stake in the outcome your policy prohibits. Ask candidates directly:

  • How they’re paid, and whether the fee depends on the size of the award
  • Whether they’ve worked on this property or for your contractor
  • How they build estimates and which pricing data they use
  • Whether they’ve handled claims in your part of Missouri

The insurer names its appraiser in the same window. That may be an outside appraiser or someone the carrier works with often.

Stage 5Choosing the umpire

Next, the two appraisers try to agree on an umpire: a neutral third person who only steps in if the appraisers can’t settle the number between themselves. Umpires are often experienced appraisers, contractors, engineers or former adjusters.

When the appraisers can’t agree

Most clauses say that if the appraisers can’t agree on an umpire within a set number of days, either side may ask a court to appoint one. In Missouri that generally means the circuit court for the county where the property is located, whether that’s one of the 114 counties or the independent City of St. Louis. That request is a court filing, so many people bring in an attorney for that step.

What a good umpire brings

  • No ties to either party or either appraiser
  • Working knowledge of local repair costs
  • A clear, even-handed way of receiving each side’s position
  • A willingness to look at the property, not just the paperwork

Stage 6Inspection and competing estimates

With the panel in place, the appraisers inspect the property, often together. On a roof that means walking the slopes, measuring and photographing damage. Inside, it can mean moisture readings, room measurements and a close look at finishes that may need to match.

Building the estimates

Each appraiser prepares a line-item estimate, usually in software such as Xactimate with a price list for the local market. The two estimates are then compared line by line. Expect discussion about:

  • Quantities: squares, linear feet and square footage
  • Repair methods and whether code upgrades are triggered
  • Matching siding, shingles or flooring that can’t be sourced
  • Labor minimums, steep and high charges, and overhead and profit

Your role

Your part is usually to give access, share receipts and photos, and answer questions about the property’s condition before the loss. Keep a copy of everything you hand to the panel.

Stage 7Negotiation and the award

Many appraisals finish without the umpire ever weighing in. The two appraisers work through their differences, trade documentation and reach a number. When they agree, they sign a written award and send it to both parties.

If the umpire is needed

When the appraisers can’t agree on certain items, they submit just those differences to the umpire. The umpire reviews both positions, may visit the property, and then joins one appraiser on each disputed figure or sets a figure in between. Under most clauses, a written agreement signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss.

What the award usually shows

  • Replacement cost value and actual cash value, often split by dwelling, other structures and contents
  • Depreciation, where the policy allows it
  • Notes on any items the panel didn’t value because coverage is disputed

An award typically doesn’t subtract the deductible or prior payments. The insurer applies those when it pays.

Stage 8Payment and what follows

After the award, the insurer applies the policy terms: it subtracts the deductible and any earlier payments and issues payment, often with your mortgage company named on the check. The timing is usually set in the policy’s loss payment condition, so read that section and follow up in writing if payment runs late.

Common next steps

  • Collect recoverable depreciation by finishing repairs and sending invoices within the policy’s time limit
  • Endorse and deposit lender-named checks through your mortgage company’s process
  • Pay your appraiser and your share of the umpire’s fee as the policy describes
  • File the award, estimates and photos with your permanent records

If problems continue

An award usually settles the amount, but it may not end every issue. If the insurer won’t pay the award, raises a new coverage defense or delays without explanation, that’s a legal matter. Talk with a Missouri attorney. You can also file a complaint with the Missouri Department of Commerce and Insurance.

When appraisal is the wrong tool

Appraisal is good at one job, and forcing it into others usually costs time and money. Think twice before demanding it when:

  • The claim has been denied outright, or the insurer says the cause of loss is excluded
  • The insurer is alleging misrepresentation or fraud
  • The gap between estimates is small enough that appraisal fees would eat most of it
  • A suit limitation deadline is close and your policy doesn’t clearly pause it
  • The insurer hasn’t yet had a fair chance to see the damage or your documentation

In those situations, a Missouri attorney or a simple reinspection request is often the better first move. If you’re not sure which kind of dispute you have, an independent estimate review can help separate amount questions from coverage questions before you commit to anything.
