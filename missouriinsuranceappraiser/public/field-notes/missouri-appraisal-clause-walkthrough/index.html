How the Appraisal Clause Works in a Typical Missouri Homeowners Policy

Follow a typical appraisal clause from the written demand to the signed award, and learn which questions the panel can’t answer.

By the Missouri Insurance Appraiser team · Published September 3, 2026 · 8 min read

Most Missouri homeowners never read the appraisal clause until a claim stalls. Then it suddenly matters a great deal. This walkthrough follows a typical clause from the demand to the award, explains what each step means in practice, and flags the places where your own policy may say something different.

Where the clause lives and what it says

In a typical homeowners policy, appraisal appears in the conditions section, often near “Loss Settlement” and “Suit Against Us.” The language usually runs only a paragraph or two, but every sentence does work. A common version says that if you and the insurer fail to agree on the amount of loss, either may demand appraisal in writing; each party then chooses a competent, impartial appraiser within a set period; the appraisers choose an umpire; and an award agreed to by any two sets the amount of loss.

That’s the typical shape, not a promise about your policy. Some forms require both parties to agree before appraisal can begin. Some describe the appraisers as “disinterested” or “independent.” Some add language preserving the insurer’s right to deny the claim afterward. Read the exact words in your policy before relying on any summary, including this one.

Step one: a real disagreement over amount

The clause is triggered by a failure to agree on the amount of loss. That usually means the insurer has accepted at least part of the claim and issued an estimate, and you have a competing estimate showing a higher cost. If the insurer hasn’t finished investigating, or has denied the claim entirely, there may not yet be an amount dispute to appraise.

Before you demand appraisal, it helps to have:

  • The insurer’s estimate and any payment letters
  • Your contractor’s or appraiser’s line-item estimate
  • Photos, measurements and any engineering or inspection reports
  • A short list of the main differences, such as roof quantities, matching or overhead and profit

An independent review of the insurer’s estimate can show whether the gap is big enough to justify the cost of appraisal.

Step two: the written demand and the clock it starts

The demand should be in writing and sent in a way you can prove. It identifies the claim, states that you dispute the amount of loss and invokes the appraisal provision. Many people name their appraiser in the same letter.

The demand typically starts the first deadline. Policy language often gives each side 20 days to name an appraiser, though some policies use a different number, and many measure it from when the other side receives the demand rather than when it was sent. Standard wording then commonly gives the appraisers another set period to agree on an umpire. Put these dates on a calendar the day the demand goes out. Our claim calendar builder can help you lay them out next to proof-of-loss and suit deadlines.

Appraisal doesn’t necessarily stop other policy deadlines from running, including the time limit for filing suit. If a deadline is close, talk with a Missouri attorney before relying on appraisal.

Step three: appraisers, the umpire and the inspection

Each appraiser should meet the clause’s standard, usually “competent” plus “impartial,” “independent” or “disinterested.” Ask any candidate how they’re paid and whether they have ties to you, your contractor or the insurer. Those questions matter if the award is ever challenged.

The two appraisers then pick an umpire. If they can’t agree in time, the clause usually lets either party ask a court of record in the state where the property is located to appoint one. In Missouri that generally means the circuit court for the county where the home sits, or the City of St. Louis if the property is there.

Next comes the inspection. The appraisers measure, photograph and test, then build line-item estimates. Expect them to exchange those estimates and talk through the differences item by item, with each side supplying receipts, photos and contractor input as needed.

Step four: the award and what “binding” means

If the two appraisers agree, they sign an award. If they can’t, they send their differences to the umpire, and agreement by any two of the three sets the amount. The award often lists replacement cost and actual cash value separately, and it may break the numbers out by coverage, such as dwelling, other structures and contents.

Most clauses describe the award as binding on the amount of loss. In practice, that means neither side can simply reopen the numbers because it dislikes them. Awards can sometimes be challenged on narrow grounds, such as a panel member who wasn’t impartial or a panel that stepped outside its role. Those are legal questions for a Missouri attorney.

The award usually doesn’t subtract your deductible or prior payments. The insurer applies those when it pays. Our award net payout estimator shows how those deductions typically work.

What the clause does not decide

Appraisal is about amount. In general, it doesn’t decide:

  • Whether a cause of loss is covered or excluded
  • Whether you met your duties after loss, such as prompt notice
  • Whether the insurer acted in bad faith or refused to pay without reasonable cause
  • How policy language should be read when the two sides interpret it differently

Some disputes blur the line, such as whether damage came from the storm or from age. Panels and courts handle those mixed questions in different ways, and policy wording matters. When coverage is the main fight, appraisal may be the wrong first step.

A practical checklist

  1. Read the full appraisal clause and the suit limitation clause.
  2. Confirm the insurer has accepted coverage for at least part of the loss.
  3. Gather competing estimates and supporting photos.
  4. Send a dated written demand and keep proof of delivery.
  5. Name a qualified, impartial appraiser within the policy’s window.
  6. Track the umpire deadline and every other policy date.
  7. Keep copies of everything you give the panel.

Nothing here is legal advice, and your policy controls. If you’d like help with the demand or want an appraiser on your side, see our appraisal demand representation page.
