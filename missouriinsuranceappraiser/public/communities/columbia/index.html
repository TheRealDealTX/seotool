Housing mix

Columbia combines early twentieth-century homes near downtown and the university, a large stock of student rentals and duplexes, and newer subdivisions spreading south and east of town.

Three kinds of Columbia properties

Ask a Columbia roofer about the last big hailstorm and they will tell you the claims split three ways. Owner-occupied homes in Old Southwest and around the parks. Rentals in East Campus, Benton-Stephens and the duplex blocks near the university. And newer houses in the subdivisions south toward Rock Bridge and east along the outer roads. Same storm, very different disputes.

Older homes near downtown

Old Southwest and East Campus have brick and frame houses from the early 1900s onward: steep roofs, plaster walls, wood windows and porches that have been rebuilt more than once. When hail or a falling tree damages one of these, the cost of matching trim and plaster is the usual sticking point.

Rentals and small landlords

Rental claims bring their own questions. Was the loss under a landlord or dwelling-fire form rather than a homeowners policy? Is there loss of rents coverage while units are empty? Did a tenant’s delay in reporting a leak change anything? Those are policy and coverage questions. Once they are sorted out, we can help with the part appraisal covers: what the repair actually costs, including turnover-season pricing when every contractor in town is booked. Owners of several buildings may also want to read about commercial and HOA appraisal.

Newer subdivisions

Newer homes usually mean asphalt roofs, vinyl or fiber-cement siding and builder-grade finishes. Disputes tend to center on partial roof approvals and siding that no longer matches once one elevation is replaced.

Water: creek, pipe, or roof

Hinkson Creek and its tributaries can rise fast in a heavy rain. Floodwater from a creek is generally excluded from homeowners coverage and needs a separate flood policy. Water from a burst supply line in a cold snap is a different story, and so is rain coming through a storm-torn roof. Our page on burst pipe and water damage appraisal explains how source, timing and drying records feed into the estimate.

Student rentals left empty over winter break are a classic frozen-pipe setup. Many policies have conditions about heat in vacant or unoccupied buildings, so read those before you assume a claim will be paid.

Permits, pricing and the appraisal decision

Most structural and roofing work inside city limits goes through the City of Columbia’s building and site development office, and work outside the city goes through Boone County. Code-driven items can be legitimate parts of a repair estimate when your policy includes ordinance or law coverage.

Pricing is the other local factor. Columbia’s contractor pool tightens in late summer, when rental turnover and storm work overlap. An estimate based on off-season pricing can look reasonable on paper and still fail to get the job done.

Appraisal is worth considering when the insurer agrees the loss is covered, you have tried to resolve the difference, and the gap is big enough to justify the cost of an appraiser and a share of the umpire. It decides the amount of loss, not coverage. If you are not sure you are there yet, our appraisal roadmap lays out the steps.

Questions from Columbia property owners

I own a rental near campus. Can I use appraisal on a landlord policy?

Many dwelling and landlord policies include an appraisal clause, just as homeowners policies do. Check your specific form; if the clause is there and the dispute is about the amount, appraisal may apply.

A pipe froze in my vacant rental over winter break. Is that covered?

Possibly, but many policies limit coverage for freezing in vacant or unoccupied buildings unless heat was maintained or the water was shut off. That is a coverage question to resolve before appraisal.
