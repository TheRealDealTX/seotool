What Does an Insurance Appraiser Do?

  • August 11, 2026

When an insurance claim turns into a disagreement over repair costs, measurements, materials, or the extent of property damage, you may start hearing about appraisal. That usually leads to one important question: what does an insurance appraiser do? An insurance appraiser evaluates the amount of a disputed property loss, develops or reviews estimates, examines damage, and works with another appraiser to determine an appropriate valuation.

That sounds simple. In practice, there can be a lot more involved.

A roof estimate may be missing several components. Interior repairs may require more labor than the original estimate allows. One contractor may believe a building component must be replaced, while another believes it can be repaired. Even small disagreements can become expensive when they affect hundreds or thousands of square feet.

That is where the insurance appraisal process can become important.

For Alabama property owners, understanding the appraiser’s role before entering appraisal can help you know what to expect, what documents to prepare, and which questions to ask.

What Is an Insurance Appraiser?

An insurance appraiser is a person selected to evaluate the amount of loss when there is a disagreement involving the value or scope of a property insurance claim.

The exact authority of an appraiser comes from the insurance policy.

Many property policies contain an appraisal clause describing when appraisal may be requested, how each side selects an appraiser, how an umpire may be chosen, and how an appraisal award is reached. Because wording varies from policy to policy, the actual appraisal provision should always be reviewed before assuming how the process will work.

An appraiser isn’t simply another adjuster assigned to reopen the entire claim.

Their job is generally narrower.

They examine the disputed loss and develop a reasoned position regarding its amount. Depending on the property and dispute, that may require reviewing photographs, measurements, estimates, invoices, material specifications, contractor proposals, damage reports, and other documentation.

In Alabama, the policy language is especially important because courts have treated appraisal as a contractual process distinct from arbitration. The Alabama Supreme Court reaffirmed this distinction in Great American Insurance Company v. Crystal Shores Owners Association, involving an appraisal provision addressing disagreement over the amount of loss.

What Does an Insurance Appraiser Do?

So, what does an insurance appraiser do once appointed?

The work normally starts with understanding exactly what is being disputed.

Suppose an insurance carrier estimates a storm-related loss at $35,000 while a contractor estimates the necessary work at $62,000. The difference alone doesn’t explain the problem. An appraiser needs to dig into the estimates and determine why they are different.

Maybe one estimate includes only spot repairs while another includes replacement.

Maybe quantities differ.

Maybe one estimate omits roofing accessories, interior finishes, debris removal, or required labor.

The appraiser’s job is to identify those differences and evaluate them.

Typical responsibilities can include:

  • Reviewing the insurer’s estimate
  • Reviewing contractor or policyholder estimates
  • Inspecting damaged property when appropriate
  • Taking or verifying measurements
  • Reviewing photographs and claim documentation
  • Identifying differences in repair scope
  • Evaluating materials and quantities
  • Reviewing labor and pricing
  • Preparing an independent estimate or valuation
  • Discussing disputed items with the opposing appraiser
  • Identifying areas where the two appraisers agree
  • Submitting remaining disagreements to an umpire when required

The objective is not simply to produce the highest or lowest number possible.

A strong appraisal position should be supportable.

Every major line item should have a reason behind it.

Insurance Appraiser vs. Insurance Adjuster

These roles are easy to confuse, particularly because both professionals may inspect property and work with estimates.

They serve different purposes.

The distinction matters.

If you’re wondering what does an insurance appraiser do differently from an adjuster, think of the appraiser as someone brought into a specific valuation dispute rather than someone handling every aspect of the insurance claim.

Insurance Appraiser vs. Public Adjuster

An insurance appraiser and a public adjuster aren’t automatically the same thing either.

A public adjuster generally represents a policyholder during the insurance claim process, subject to applicable licensing and regulatory requirements. Their involvement can include documenting a loss, preparing estimates, communicating with the insurance carrier, and negotiating aspects of the claim.

An appraiser operates within the appraisal process.

The appraiser’s assignment is tied to determining the amount of loss being appraised according to the policy.

That narrower role is important because appraisal should not be treated as a substitute for every other part of claim handling.

When Might an Insurance Appraiser Be Needed?

Not every property claim needs appraisal.

If the policyholder and insurance company agree on the amount of loss, there may be nothing for appraisers to resolve.

Appraisal becomes more relevant when a legitimate valuation disagreement remains.

For example, consider a commercial building damaged during a severe Alabama storm. The insurance estimate allows for repair of several roof sections. The property owner’s contractor believes the damage is more extensive and prepares a substantially larger replacement estimate.

The parties exchange documentation.

Another inspection takes place.

Supplements are submitted.

But a major difference remains.

That is the type of situation where the appraisal clause may become relevant, assuming the policy and circumstances permit it.

Common disputes can involve:

  • Roofing systems
  • Hail and wind damage
  • Water damage
  • Fire-related repairs
  • Interior reconstruction
  • Exterior siding
  • Windows and doors
  • Commercial property damage
  • Material quantities
  • Labor pricing
  • Repair-versus-replacement scope
  • Replacement cost estimates

The size of the disagreement is not the only consideration. The nature of the disagreement matters too.

How Does the Insurance Appraisal Process Work?

The process depends on the actual insurance contract, so there is no single procedure that applies identically to every policy.

Still, many appraisal provisions follow a similar general structure.

1. A Disagreement Develops

The policyholder and insurer disagree over the amount of loss.

This is the starting point.

Older Alabama decisions discussing appraisal provisions have likewise described the process in the context of an actual disagreement over the amount of loss.

2. The Policy Is Reviewed

Before doing anything else, read the appraisal provision carefully.

Look for information addressing:

  • How appraisal can be requested
  • Whether the demand must be written
  • Deadlines for selecting appraisers
  • Qualifications required of appraisers
  • Umpire selection
  • Payment of appraisal expenses
  • How an award becomes effective

Never assume another insurance policy uses identical language.

3. Each Side Selects an Appraiser

Typically, the policyholder selects one appraiser and the insurer selects another.

The two appraisers independently evaluate the disputed loss.

4. The Appraisers Compare Their Positions

Here’s where the detailed work matters.

The appraisers may compare measurements, repair methods, estimates, material quantities, pricing, and other disputed components.

They may agree on many items.

That’s useful!

Every item resolved between the appraisers reduces what remains contested.

5. An Umpire May Become Involved

If the appraisers cannot agree on certain disputed items, those differences may be presented to an umpire according to the appraisal clause.

The exact mechanism depends on the contract.

Alabama cases discussing appraisal provisions have included language in which the appraisers select an umpire and submit unresolved differences to that umpire.

6. An Appraisal Award Is Reached

The policy determines how an award is formed and what effect it has.

This is one reason property owners shouldn’t rely on a generic description from the internet when deciding whether to invoke appraisal. Read your policy first.

What Does an Insurance Appraiser Inspect?

If you’re researching what does an insurance appraiser do during an inspection, the answer depends heavily on the type of claim.

A roof claim looks very different from a fire-damaged commercial interior.

For an exterior storm loss, an appraiser might evaluate:

  • Roofing materials
  • Roof measurements
  • Ridge and hip components
  • Flashing
  • Vents
  • Gutters and downspouts
  • Siding
  • Windows
  • Exterior finishes

For an interior loss, the review could include drywall, flooring, cabinetry, trim, paint, insulation, fixtures, ceilings, and other affected components.

The inspection is only part of the process.

Documentation matters just as much.

A good appraiser may need to compare the physical property against photographs taken soon after the event, prior estimates, contractor proposals, invoices, engineering reports when applicable, and the insurer’s scope.

That creates a much clearer picture than looking at one estimate in isolation.

How Does an Insurance Appraiser Determine the Amount of Loss?

This is where experienced estimating becomes valuable.

Imagine two estimates with a $40,000 difference.

Looking only at the totals tells you almost nothing.

You need to compare the details.

An appraiser may examine:

Measurements and Quantities

Are the roof dimensions correct?

How many squares are involved?

How much drywall needs replacement?

How many linear feet of trim, flashing, gutters, or fencing are included?

Incorrect quantities can distort an estimate quickly.

Materials

The specific material matters.

Replacing standard three-tab shingles is not the same calculation as evaluating architectural shingles, metal roofing, tile, specialty flooring, custom cabinetry, or commercial roofing components.

Labor

Some work involves considerably more labor than a basic unit price suggests.

Access, height, demolition, installation complexity, sequencing, and other project conditions can affect the evaluation.

Repair Scope

This is often where significant differences appear.

One estimate may include replacement of a damaged assembly. Another may allow only a partial repair.

A skilled appraiser should be able to explain the reasoning behind the position rather than simply pointing to a larger estimate.

Related Work

Repairs rarely happen in isolation.

Replacing one component can affect another.

For example, roof work may involve vents, flashing, underlayment, drip edge, accessories, removal, disposal, and adjacent materials. Interior reconstruction can involve demolition, insulation, drywall, texture, painting, flooring removal, trim work, and cleanup.

Missing related items can add up quickly.

What Does an Insurance Appraiser Not Do?

Knowing what does an insurance appraiser do is only half of the equation.

You should also understand what appraisal is not intended to accomplish.

An appraiser generally should not be treated as someone who can:

  • Rewrite your insurance policy
  • Guarantee a particular claim payment
  • Decide every dispute between you and the insurer
  • Automatically establish insurance coverage
  • Promise that every item in an estimate will ultimately be paid
  • Replace legal advice when a legal dispute exists

The distinction between valuation and broader coverage questions can be significant.

In Alabama, courts have recognized appraisal as a process governed by the insurance contract rather than simply treating it as ordinary arbitration.

If your disagreement centers on whether the policy provides coverage at all rather than how much a covered loss is worth, that may involve issues beyond the appraiser’s role.

What Does an Insurance Appraisal Umpire Do?

An umpire becomes important when the two appraisers cannot fully agree.

Think of a roofing loss.

The policyholder’s appraiser believes the appropriate amount of loss is $92,000. The carrier’s appraiser calculates $67,000.

But that $25,000 disagreement may actually involve only five disputed categories.

The appraisers might agree on:

  • Roof measurements
  • Shingle quantity
  • Gutters
  • Interior ceiling repairs
  • Debris removal

Yet disagree on additional roofing components, repair methodology, labor requirements, or certain pricing.

Instead of sending the entire claim to the umpire, the unresolved differences may be presented according to the policy’s procedure.

The umpire reviews the competing positions and participates in reaching the appraisal determination as provided by the contract.

A Practical Insurance Appraisal Example

Let’s make this more concrete.

An Alabama homeowner experiences significant storm damage.

The insurance company’s initial estimate totals $31,500.

A restoration contractor later prepares an estimate for $58,750.

That’s a $27,250 gap.

But immediately invoking appraisal without understanding the difference would be premature.

First, compare the estimates.

Perhaps the contractor included complete replacement of damaged roofing while the insurer estimated repair.

Maybe one estimate includes gutter replacement while the other allows for repair.

The contractor may include interior water damage that isn’t present in the insurer’s scope.

There may also be differences in measurements, labor, materials, or demolition.

After documentation and supplemental estimates are exchanged, the parties still cannot agree.

If appraisal is available and properly invoked under the policy, each side appoints an appraiser.

The appraisers inspect the property, review the estimates, compare disputed items, and work toward agreement.

Suppose they eventually agree on $49,500 but remain divided over $5,000 of roofing-related work.

That unresolved portion may then be addressed through the umpire process required by the policy.

This hypothetical shows why appraisal is much more detailed than simply choosing between two totals.

What Should You Look for in an Insurance Appraiser?

Understanding what does an insurance appraiser do also helps you choose one intelligently.

Don’t focus entirely on price.

Ask what the appraiser actually knows.

A property appraiser dealing with a complex roof loss should understand roofing components and estimating. Someone evaluating a large commercial fire loss should be comfortable analyzing extensive reconstruction scopes.

Look for experience involving:

  • Property damage
  • Construction or restoration
  • Detailed estimating
  • Insurance appraisal
  • The specific type of loss involved
  • Documentation review
  • Measurements
  • Negotiation between appraisers

Communication matters too.

An appraiser may have an excellent technical understanding but still struggle if they cannot clearly explain why an item belongs in the valuation.

Documentation plus reasoning is much stronger than a number alone.

Questions to Ask Before Selecting an Appraiser

Before making a decision, ask direct questions.

You might start with:

  1. How much insurance appraisal experience do you have?
  2. Have you worked with losses similar to mine?
  3. Will you personally inspect the property?
  4. How do you evaluate differences between competing estimates?
  5. What documents should I provide?
  6. How are your fees calculated?
  7. What happens if you disagree with the other appraiser?
  8. How will you communicate updates during the appraisal?

Pay attention to the answers.

Someone promising a specific award before properly reviewing the claim should raise questions. Appraisal requires evaluation first.

How Long Does Insurance Appraisal Take?

There is no universal timeline.

A straightforward residential property dispute with clear documentation may move differently from a large commercial claim involving hundreds of line items.

Timing can depend on:

  • Property size
  • Complexity of damage
  • Number of disputed items
  • Availability of both appraisers
  • Inspection scheduling
  • Quality of documentation
  • Estimate complexity
  • Whether an umpire becomes necessary
  • The procedures established by the policy

You can help reduce avoidable delays by organizing your claim documents before the appraisal begins.

Create one folder containing your policy, insurer estimates, contractor estimates, photographs, invoices, repair proposals, correspondence, reports, measurements, and other relevant material.

Don’t make the appraiser hunt for everything.

Is Insurance Appraisal the Same as Arbitration?

No. They should not automatically be treated as interchangeable processes.

The distinction has specific relevance in Alabama. In 2023, the Alabama Supreme Court concluded in Great American Insurance Company v. Crystal Shores Owners Association that the appraisal provision before it did not constitute an arbitration clause.

For a property owner, the practical takeaway is straightforward: appraisal is a process created by the insurance contract, and the wording of that contract matters.

Always start there.

When Should an Alabama Property Owner Consider Appraisal?

Appraisal may be worth investigating when there is a substantial, unresolved disagreement regarding the amount of a property loss.

For example:

Your contractor estimates $80,000.

The insurer estimates $42,000.

You’ve provided documentation explaining the difference.

The insurer has reviewed the supplement.

And a significant amount-of-loss dispute remains.

At that point, reviewing the policy’s appraisal provision can make sense.

But appraisal isn’t automatically the correct response to every difficult claim.

First determine what the disagreement actually involves.

Is it about pricing?

Measurements?

Repair scope?

Damage extent?

Or is the insurer saying the damage isn’t covered at all?

Those are not necessarily the same type of dispute.

Understanding the problem before choosing the process can save time, expense, and frustration.

Frequently Asked Questions About Insurance Appraisers

What does an insurance appraiser do?

An insurance appraiser evaluates the amount of a disputed property loss. This may include inspecting damage, reviewing estimates and documentation, calculating repair costs, and working with the other party’s appraiser.

Who hires the insurance appraiser?

In a typical appraisal process, the policyholder selects an appraiser and the insurance company selects another, subject to the requirements of the insurance policy.

Does an insurance appraiser work for the insurance company?

Not necessarily. Each side normally chooses an appraiser during the appraisal process, so you should determine who appointed the appraiser and what qualifications the policy requires.

Can an insurance appraiser determine coverage?

Appraisal is generally focused on determining the amount of loss rather than resolving every question about insurance coverage. The exact scope depends on the policy and applicable law.

What is an appraisal clause in insurance?

An appraisal clause is a provision within an insurance policy establishing a process for resolving certain disputes concerning the amount of loss.

Who pays for an insurance appraiser?

The answer depends on the policy. Many appraisal provisions address how each appraiser and the umpire are paid, so review the specific language of your contract before proceeding.

What happens if the insurance appraisers disagree?

If the two appraisers cannot resolve certain differences, an umpire may become involved under the procedure established by the insurance policy.

What does an insurance appraisal umpire do?

The umpire considers unresolved differences between the two appraisers and participates in determining the appraisal award according to the policy’s terms.

How long does insurance appraisal take?

There is no fixed timeframe. Claim complexity, documentation, scheduling, the number of disputed items, and whether an umpire is required can all affect the process.

Is an insurance appraisal binding?

The effect of an appraisal award depends on the specific insurance contract and applicable law. Review your policy carefully and obtain appropriate professional advice when necessary.

Understanding the Appraiser’s Role Before You Begin

So, what does an insurance appraiser do?

At its core, the job is about evaluating a disputed property loss in detail.

An appraiser looks beyond the bottom-line number. Measurements are checked. Materials are reviewed. Repair methods are evaluated. Estimate differences are identified. Documentation is examined. Then the appraiser works with the opposing appraiser—and, when required, an umpire—to address the disputed amount.

For Alabama homeowners and commercial property owners, that can provide a structured way to address certain valuation disagreements when an insurance policy includes an appraisal provision.

The important part is knowing what you’re getting into.

Read the appraisal clause. Understand what is actually disputed. Organize your documentation. And choose an appraiser who understands both property damage and detailed estimating.
