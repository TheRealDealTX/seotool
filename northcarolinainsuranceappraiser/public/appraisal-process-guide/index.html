Process guide

The Insurance Appraisal Process in North Carolina, Stage by Stage

8 stages, from the first disagreement to payment: what happens, who does what, and where claims most often go wrong.

Panel

Binding
Any 2 of 3 signatures

Appraisal is a dispute-resolution tool written into most North Carolina property policies. It exists for one narrow problem: you and your insurer agree there is a covered loss, but you cannot agree on what it costs to make it right. Instead of arguing indefinitely or heading to court, each side names an appraiser, the two appraisers pick a neutral umpire, and an award signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss. The stages below describe how that usually unfolds, from the first disagreement to the check. Your policy form controls the details, so read its appraisal clause alongside this guide, and talk to a North Carolina attorney about any question of coverage, deadlines or legal rights.

The 8 stages

  1. The disagreement takes shape
  2. Reading the appraisal clause
  3. Sending the written demand
  4. Both sides name appraisers
  5. Selecting the umpire
  6. Inspection, exchange and negotiation
  7. Umpire review and the award
  8. Payment and closing out the claim

Stage 1The disagreement takes shape

Appraisal starts after the carrier has inspected, written an estimate and paid or offered something. The question is no longer whether there was damage, but how much it costs to repair or replace. Before invoking anything, pin down where the gap actually is.

  • Compare the carrier’s estimate with your contractor’s line by line.
  • List missing items, wrong quantities and pricing differences separately.
  • Ask in writing for a re-inspection or supplement review if obvious items were missed.

Many disputes shrink at this point once both sides see the same list. If the remaining gap is still meaningful, you have a clear, documented amount-of-loss disagreement, which is exactly what appraisal is designed to resolve. A settlement gap estimate can help you size it before you decide.

Stage 2Reading the appraisal clause

Every policy words appraisal a little differently, so the clause itself is your rulebook. North Carolina’s standard fire policy provisions in Chapter 58 of the General Statutes include an appraisal framework, and most homeowners forms follow a similar pattern: two appraisers, an umpire, and an award agreed to by any two.

What to look for

  • Who may demand appraisal and whether the demand must be written.
  • How many days each side has to name an appraiser (20 days is common).
  • How appraiser and umpire costs are split.
  • Any requirement to submit a sworn proof of loss first.
  • The policy’s suit-limitation period, which may keep running during appraisal.

If anything is unclear, ask the carrier for a certified copy of the full policy.

Stage 3Sending the written demand

Either party can usually start appraisal with a written demand. Keep it short and factual. A typical demand identifies the policyholder, property address, policy and claim numbers and date of loss; states that the parties disagree on the amount of loss; invokes the appraisal provision; and names your appraiser or commits to naming one within the policy’s time frame.

Send it by a method you can prove, such as certified mail plus email to the adjuster, and keep copies of everything. Avoid arguing coverage in the demand. Appraisal is about the dollar value of damage, and mixing in legal arguments can invite objections or delay. If the carrier responds that a coverage question must be resolved first, that is a point to discuss with a North Carolina attorney.

Stage 4Both sides name appraisers

Each party selects its own appraiser, and most policies require that person to be competent and disinterested. In practice that means someone who understands construction costs and estimating software and who has no financial stake that would compromise independence.

Once retained, your appraiser typically:

  • Reviews the policy, the carrier’s estimate, photos and correspondence.
  • Inspects the property, measures and documents damage.
  • Builds an independent, line-item estimate of the amount of loss.
  • Contacts the carrier’s appraiser to set an exchange schedule.

Choose carefully. An appraiser whose independence is questioned can give the other side grounds to challenge the eventual award. Ask any candidate to disclose relationships and fee terms in writing.

Stage 5Selecting the umpire

The two appraisers choose a neutral umpire, ideally early, before any impasse forms. A good umpire has deep estimating experience, no ties to either side and the patience to work through detailed line items.

Appraisers usually exchange a short list of candidates, disclose any prior dealings and agree on one name in writing. If they cannot agree within the time the policy allows, many policy forms let either party ask a court of record in the county where the property is located to appoint an umpire. That step involves a court filing, so a North Carolina attorney should handle it.

The umpire generally stays in the background while the appraisers negotiate. Only the items they cannot resolve are submitted, which keeps the process focused and usually reduces the umpire’s fee.

Stage 6Inspection, exchange and negotiation

This is where most of the work happens. The appraisers inspect the property, sometimes together, and exchange their estimates. Then they go line by line through the differences.

Typical sticking points

  • Roof square counts, waste factors, pitch and steep charges.
  • Whether damaged materials can be repaired or must be replaced.
  • Unit pricing for the local market at the time of loss.
  • Overhead and profit where multiple trades are involved.
  • Matching of adjoining materials that cannot be blended.

Good documentation settles most of these questions: clear photos, measurements, manufacturer specifications and contractor quotes. The appraisers agree on whatever they can and narrow the rest to a short, well-defined list for the umpire.

Stage 7Umpire review and the award

If the appraisers resolve everything, they sign an award together and the umpire may never be needed. Otherwise, each appraiser presents the disputed items to the umpire with supporting evidence. The umpire may inspect the property, ask questions and request more documentation before deciding.

The award usually states the amount of loss, often split into replacement cost value and actual cash value, and sometimes broken out by coverage part such as dwelling, other structures or contents. Under the typical clause, an award signed by any two of the three becomes the amount of loss.

Read the award carefully before anyone treats it as final. Confirm that the categories line up with your policy, that the arithmetic is right and that it is limited to amount of loss rather than coverage decisions.

Stage 8Payment and closing out the claim

After the award, the carrier applies the policy terms: it subtracts your deductible and any prior payments, applies limits, and pays the balance. Coverage questions the award did not decide may still be open, and the insurer may reserve its rights on them.

  • Track whether payment is for actual cash value now, with recoverable depreciation released after repairs.
  • Note any deadline for completing repairs to claim the holdback.
  • Keep invoices and photos of completed work.
  • Watch the suit-limitation period if any issue remains unresolved.

Knowing how replacement cost, actual cash value and holdback interact helps you anticipate each payment. If payment stalls, the North Carolina Department of Insurance accepts consumer complaints.

Mistakes that weaken an appraisal

Most problems in appraisal come from preparation, not from the panel itself. The ones we see most often are avoidable:

  • Invoking too early. If the carrier has not finished its estimate, there may not yet be a real disagreement to appraise.
  • Thin documentation. Without dated photos, measurements and receipts, the panel has to rely on whatever the carrier recorded.
  • Missing deadlines. Appraiser selection windows and suit-limitation periods can run regardless of negotiations.
  • Choosing an appraiser with a conflict. A relationship or fee arrangement that looks like a stake in the outcome can be used to attack the award.
  • Letting mitigation slide. Damage that grows because the property was not protected may be excluded from the amount of loss.

What appraisal can’t do

Appraisal sets the amount of loss. It generally does not decide whether a policy covers a cause of loss, whether an exclusion applies, whether the claim was handled in bad faith, or how a policy should be interpreted. Those questions belong to the insurer, the North Carolina Department of Insurance or the courts, and they call for a North Carolina attorney, not an appraiser. We are an appraisal firm, not a law firm or a public adjuster, so we keep our work on valuation and refer legal questions to counsel.
