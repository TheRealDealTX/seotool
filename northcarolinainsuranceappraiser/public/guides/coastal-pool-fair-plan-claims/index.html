Claims with the NC Coastal Pool and FAIR Plan: A Practical Guide

Many coastal and hard-to-insure properties carry policies from the state’s residual market associations. What that means when a claim turns into a dispute.

By the North Carolina Insurance Appraiser team · Published October 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Not every North Carolina property is insured by a traditional private carrier. Many homes along the coast, and some properties elsewhere that are hard to place in the regular market, are insured through one of two residual market associations: the North Carolina Insurance Underwriting Association, which operates the Coastal Property Insurance Pool, and the North Carolina Joint Underwriting Association, which operates the state’s FAIR Plan. Claims under these policies follow many of the same principles as other property claims, but the specific forms, procedures and deadlines deserve close attention.

What the two associations are

NCIUA and the Coastal Property Insurance Pool

The North Carolina Insurance Underwriting Association was created to make property coverage available in the beach and coastal areas of 18 eastern counties, where wind and hurricane exposure can make private coverage hard to find. Depending on the property and the policy, coverage may be written for wind and hail only, with other perils insured elsewhere, or on a broader basis. Many homeowners in coastal areas carry a private homeowners policy that excludes wind, paired with a wind policy from the association.

NCJUA and the FAIR Plan

The North Carolina Joint Underwriting Association administers the FAIR Plan, which offers basic property coverage to owners who cannot obtain it in the voluntary market. FAIR Plan policies are often narrower than a full homeowners policy, and the forms and endorsements available vary by property type.

These descriptions are general. Eligibility rules, territories, coverage options and procedures can change, so the association’s current information and your own policy documents are the authority, not this article.

Why two policies can mean two claims

If your coastal home has a private homeowners policy that excludes wind and a separate wind policy from the association, a hurricane can produce two claims: one under the wind policy and possibly another under the homeowners or flood policy for other damage. Each claim has its own deductible, adjuster, estimate and timelines. Damage has to be attributed carefully, much as it is between wind and flood. Our wind and flood damage splitter can help you organize line items by policy.

Deductibles on coastal policies

Coastal wind policies often carry named-storm or hurricane deductibles expressed as a percentage of the dwelling limit. That deductible can be several times larger than a flat all-other-perils deductible. Illustrative example: a 3 percent deductible on a $250,000 dwelling limit would be $7,500 before any payment. Check your declarations page for the exact percentage and the event that triggers it, and use our named-storm deductible calculator to see the effect.

Read your own policy form for dispute language

This is the most important point in this guide. Policies issued by the associations generally contain claim dispute provisions, and many include appraisal language, but the exact wording, conditions and deadlines depend on the specific form and edition you hold. Before relying on any timeline, check:

  • Whether the policy contains an appraisal clause and how it is triggered.
  • How many days each side has to name an appraiser.
  • How the umpire is chosen and what happens if the appraisers cannot agree.
  • How appraisal and umpire costs are divided.
  • Any proof-of-loss requirement and its due date.
  • The period the policy allows for filing suit, and when it starts.

If you do not have the full policy, request a complete copy, including endorsements, from the association or your agent. Do not assume the language matches your previous policy or a neighbor’s.

Practical steps after a coastal loss

  1. Report the claim promptly to every insurer that may be responsible.
  2. Photograph all elevations, the roof, interior damage and any waterlines before cleanup.
  3. Protect the property from further damage and keep every receipt.
  4. Keep a log of calls, inspections and correspondence with dates and names.
  5. Get a detailed, line-item estimate from a qualified contractor.
  6. Compare that estimate with the adjuster’s, line by line.

When the estimate seems too low

Coastal claims often involve exposure-specific costs that a generic estimate may underweight: wind-rated shingles and fasteners, elevated structures with difficult access, corrosion-resistant hardware, stairs and decks exposed to surge, and building code requirements in coastal jurisdictions. If the adjuster’s estimate leaves these out, start with a written supplement request supported by photos and contractor pricing.

If the gap remains and your policy provides for appraisal, the process generally works as it does for other property policies: each side names an appraiser, the appraisers select an umpire, and an award signed by two sets the amount of loss. Appraisal resolves amount of loss, not coverage. If the association has denied coverage for part of the claim, or questions arise about the policy terms, a North Carolina attorney is the right resource.

Complaints and help

The North Carolina Department of Insurance accepts consumer complaints and can be a useful resource if communication breaks down. A complaint is separate from appraisal and can proceed alongside it.
