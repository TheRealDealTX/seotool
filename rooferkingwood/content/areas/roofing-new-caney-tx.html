Three Kinds of New Caney Roofs

New construction

Communities such as Tavola were built mostly in the last decade or so. Their roofs are young, so problems are usually about workmanship: shingles nailed too high, pipe boots sealed only with caulk, and missing kick-out flashing where roof edges meet walls. Catching these early matters, particularly before a builder warranty runs out.

Older established homes

Neighborhoods like Roman Forest, a small incorporated city beside New Caney, have older homes under heavy tree cover. Many are on their second or third roof, and some have plank decking or multiple layers that need to come off. Shade and pine needles bring the same algae streaks and clogged valleys Kingwood homeowners know well.

Rural lots and manufactured homes

Off the main roads, larger lots often hold a mix of site-built homes, manufactured homes, barns and carports. Metal is common on these properties, and our metal roofing crew handles new installs and repairs to older exposed-fastener panels.

Storm and Tree Damage

East Montgomery County has a lot of tall timber and not much open ground to slow storm winds near the creeks. When hurricanes or strong thunderstorms push through, trees and limbs are the biggest threat to New Caney roofs. After Hurricane Beryl, many homes needed emergency tarps before repairs could start. Our tree damage roof repair service covers tarping, decking replacement and photo documentation.

After a tree strike: Stay out of rooms under the damaged area. Water-soaked drywall and insulation can come down without warning.

Planning a Roof Replacement in New Caney

Older New Caney homes, especially under heavy pines, often need replacement somewhere between 15 and 20 years, sooner if shade and debris have kept the shingles damp. A typical home has roughly 2,200 to 3,400 square feet of roof area, and architectural shingle replacement in the Houston area runs about $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot installed as a planning range. Your price depends on pitch, layers and decking condition. In Tavola and other deed-restricted communities, plan for HOA approval of the shingle color before the crew is scheduled; rural lots usually have more freedom, though some carry older deed restrictions.
