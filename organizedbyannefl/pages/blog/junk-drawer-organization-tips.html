Junk Drawer Organization Tips for a Drawer That Stays Tidy

Every home needs a catch-all drawer, but it should not be where things disappear. Here is our six-step process to empty, sort, zone and maintain a junk drawer that works.

Almost every home we walk into has one: the drawer near the kitchen or entry that collects rubber bands, takeout menus, dead batteries, mystery keys and a dozen pens that may or may not work. There is nothing wrong with having a catch-all drawer. In fact, we think every busy household needs one. The problem is when it becomes a place where things go to disappear.

Good junk drawer organization is not about making the drawer look like a showroom. It is about being able to open it, find the tape measure in two seconds and close it again without anything jamming. Below is the same process our team uses in Fort Lauderdale kitchens, from Victoria Park bungalows to Las Olas high-rises, adapted so you can do it yourself in under an hour.

Step 1: Empty the Drawer Completely

Resist the urge to tidy in place. Pull the drawer out (many slide free if you lift slightly at the end of the track) and empty everything onto a towel or a clear section of counter. Wipe out the crumbs, sticky residue and loose paper clips hiding in the corners. A clean, empty drawer lets you see the actual space you have to work with, and it makes the next step much faster.

Step 2: Sort Into Simple Piles

Sort quickly, using broad piles rather than precise categories. We like these five:

  • Keep here: items you reach for often and use near this drawer.
  • Belongs elsewhere: things that have a better home in another room, like a screwdriver that should live with the tools or a lip balm that belongs in a bag.
  • Trash: dried-out pens, expired coupons, broken hair ties and twist ties from long-gone bread bags.
  • Recycle or donate: extra chargers, duplicate scissors and takeout menus you can easily find online.
  • Mystery: keys, cords and small parts you cannot identify.

Put the mystery pile in a zip-top bag, label it with today's date and set it aside. If you have not needed anything from it in three months, let it go. This one habit keeps the drawer from filling back up with unidentified objects.

Pro tip

Test every pen and marker as you sort. Keep three or four good ones and toss the rest. Pens are one of the items we most often find overflowing in junk drawers, and almost nobody needs more than a handful in one spot.

Step 3: Decide What Belongs in a Junk Drawer

A junk drawer works best when it holds small, frequently used items that do not have an obvious home anywhere else. Here is what we typically keep in a well-edited drawer:

  • A few pens, a permanent marker and a small notepad
  • Scissors and a roll of clear tape
  • A small tape measure
  • A multi-bit screwdriver for quick fixes
  • Spare batteries in the sizes your household actually uses
  • Rubber bands and a few binder clips
  • A flashlight or small headlamp
  • Spare house keys, clearly tagged
  • A charging cable or two

What does not belong? Paperwork that needs action, medications, anything sharp without a cover and large items that crowd out everything else. Paper in particular tends to multiply. If bills and permission slips keep landing in the drawer, give them a dedicated inbox tray on the counter instead.

Step 4: Zone the Drawer by How You Use It

Once you know what stays, group like with like and assign zones. Think about the motion of opening the drawer: the front is prime real estate, so put your most-used items there. Less frequent things, like spare batteries or picture-hanging hardware, can live toward the back.

A typical zone layout might look like this:

  1. Front left: writing tools and notepad
  2. Front right: scissors, tape and tape measure
  3. Middle: chargers and cords, coiled and secured with a tie
  4. Back: batteries, spare keys and small hardware

Your zones will look different depending on where the drawer is. A drawer by the garage door might lean toward keys, sunglasses and gate remotes. A drawer in a home office might be mostly supplies. The point is to decide on purpose rather than letting the contents decide for you.

Step 5: Add Drawer Dividers That Fit

Now, and only now, it is time to think about containers. Measure the inside of the drawer: width, depth and height. Then choose dividers based on what you are actually keeping.

  • Modular trays in mixed sizes are our favorite for junk drawers because you can rearrange them as needs change. Look for trays with non-slip feet, or add small dots of museum putty so they stay put.
  • Expandable bamboo dividers work well in deeper drawers and add a warm, finished look.
  • Small lidded containers are ideal for tiny loose things like push pins, safety pins and spare screws.

Avoid buying a one-piece organizer before you measure. Many are slightly too long or too wide, which leaves wasted gaps or a drawer that will not close. With modular pieces, you can fill the space edge to edge.

Design-minded detail

Choose one material and color family for your trays, such as all white, all clear or all bamboo. A consistent look makes a small drawer feel calm instead of chaotic, and it is easier to add matching pieces later.

Step 6: Keep It Up With a Monthly 5-Minute Reset

Even a perfectly organized drawer drifts. Receipts sneak in, a stray toy part appears, the scissors end up in the wrong tray. The fix is a quick, predictable reset rather than another full overhaul.

Once a month, set a timer for five minutes and:

  1. Pull out anything that does not belong and return it to its home.
  2. Toss trash, dead pens and expired coupons.
  3. Put items back in their zones.
  4. Check the date on your mystery bag and let go of anything past its three-month window.
  5. Note anything you are running low on, like tape or batteries.

Tie the reset to something you already do monthly, like paying a bill or checking the HVAC filter, so it becomes automatic. If your household shares the drawer, a small label on the inside front edge listing the zones helps everyone put things back where they go.

When the drawer keeps overflowing

Sometimes the junk drawer is overflowing because the rest of the kitchen or home lacks clear homes for everyday things. If you find yourself constantly shoving items into the drawer because there is nowhere else for them, that is a sign the bigger system needs attention. Frequently asked questions

What should go in a junk drawer?

Small, frequently used items without another obvious home: a few pens, scissors, tape, a tape measure, a small screwdriver, spare batteries, rubber bands and tagged spare keys. Paperwork, medications and bulky items are better stored elsewhere.

How often should I clean out my junk drawer?

A quick five-minute reset once a month keeps most junk drawers under control. Return stray items, toss trash and put everything back in its zone.

Do I need special organizers for a junk drawer?

Not necessarily, but modular trays make a big difference. Measure the drawer first, then choose trays in mixed sizes that fill the space edge to edge so items cannot slide around.
