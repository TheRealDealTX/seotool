A good dog ear cleaning routine is less about scrubbing and more about rhythm: a quick check every week, a cleaning only as often as your dog actually needs, and a plan that bends for swimming season and allergy flare-ups. The right schedule for a Basset Hound is very different from the right schedule for a German Shepherd. This guide gives you ready-made routines by dog type, a weekly checklist, a monthly to-do list and a sample 4-week calendar you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • Every dog benefits from a weekly ear check. Not every dog needs a weekly cleaning.
  • Upright-eared dogs often need cleaning every few weeks to monthly. Floppy-eared dogs, swimmers and allergy dogs often need it weekly or every other week.
  • Over-cleaning irritates healthy ears. Clean based on what you see, not habit.
  • Drying ears after water is one of the most effective ear maintenance habits.
  • Pair ear care with treats or a lick mat so your dog learns to enjoy it.

How often should you clean your dog's ears?

There is no single right answer, which is why generic advice like "clean weekly" can lead people astray. Ear shape, coat type, swimming, allergies and age all change how quickly wax and moisture build up.

Treat the result as a starting point. If your dog's ears look clean and smell fine at cleaning time, you can stretch the interval. If they're waxy again within a few days, shorten it, and if they're red, smelly or painful, see a vet. Our guide to dog ear infection signs explains what to look for.

Why a dog ear cleaning routine works better than cleaning on demand

Many owners only clean ears when there's an obvious problem. By then, the wax and moisture have often been building for a while. A routine catches change early.

  • Earlier warnings. A weekly look means you notice redness or odor in days, not weeks.
  • Less buildup. Regular, gentle maintenance keeps debris from collecting in the bend of the L-shaped ear canal.
  • Calmer dogs. Dogs that have their ears handled often, in a positive way, are easier to treat when something does go wrong.
  • Fewer mistakes. A set schedule keeps you from both forgetting and overdoing it.

A routine won't prevent ear problems that start from allergies or other medical causes, but it helps you spot them sooner and keeps the ear environment as healthy as possible.

Upright-eared dogs

Dogs with erect ears get more airflow into the canal, so many need surprisingly little cleaning. The core of the routine is the weekly check. When the ears do look waxy, a standard fill-and-massage cleaning with a gentle, alcohol-free cleaner is plenty.

Floppy-eared dogs

Heavy ear flaps trap warmth and moisture and reduce airflow, which is why floppy-eared breeds are widely considered more prone to ear infections. A weekly or every-other-week cleaning with a drying cleaner is a common recommendation. Our floppy-ear breeds guide goes deeper.

Swimmers

Water left in the canal is a classic trigger for ear trouble. Make drying a non-negotiable part of the post-swim routine, and ask your vet whether a cleaner with drying agents after swims makes sense for your dog. Our after-bath care guide covers drying technique, and the same steps apply after the lake.

Allergy dogs

For dogs with environmental or food allergies, ear care is part of a bigger management plan. Inflamed, itchy ears can be sensitive, so a no-sting cleaner is often a better fit. Keep a simple log of itch, redness and odor so your vet can see patterns during rechecks.

Hairy-canal breeds

Hair in the canal can hold onto wax and moisture. Time your ear cleaning with grooming appointments, and talk to your vet or groomer before plucking. Many vets now advise against routine plucking unless the hair is causing problems, because it can irritate the canal.

Senior dogs

Older dogs may have drier skin, reduced hearing and less patience for handling. Keep sessions short and gentle. A weekly check is especially useful in seniors because a sudden head tilt or balance problem can have causes beyond the ear and needs a prompt vet visit.

Puppies

The goal with puppies is training, not cleaning. Touch the ears, lift the flaps and look inside several times a week, paired with treats, so ear care feels normal for life.

Your weekly ear check checklist

This takes about a minute and is the heart of any dog ear care routine.

  • Color: inner ear skin is pale pink, not red or dark.
  • Swelling: the canal opening looks open, not narrowed or puffy.
  • Wax: a small amount of light-brown wax is normal; heavy, dark, greasy or crumbly material is not.
  • Discharge: no yellow, green, black or bloody material.
  • Smell: faint or none; no yeasty, sour or rotten odor.
  • Comfort: your dog enjoys a gentle massage at the ear base without flinching.
  • Behavior: no extra scratching, head shaking, head rubbing or tilting.
  • Ear flap: no scabs, hair loss, lumps or puffy swelling.

If everything checks out and it's not a cleaning week, you're done. If something is off, use our ear symptom checker to decide whether it's a cleaning job or a vet job.

Monthly dog ear maintenance tasks

  • Trim hair around the ear opening and on the underside of the flap, or ask your groomer to.
  • Check your supplies. Make sure your cleaner hasn't expired and you have enough cotton rounds and treats.
  • Review your log. Are the ears getting dirty faster or slower? Adjust the interval.
  • Think about the season. Swimming season, grass-awn season and allergy season may call for more frequent checks.
  • Wash ear-adjacent gear like bedding, collars and any ear snoods or towels.

How to make your dog ear cleaning routine stress-free

A routine only works if your dog will put up with it. If ear cleaning is a wrestling match, it'll get skipped. These steps help.

Use counter-conditioning

Counter-conditioning means pairing something your dog finds mildly unpleasant with something they love, until the unpleasant thing predicts good stuff. For ears, that looks like this:

  1. Touch the ear for a second, then give a treat. Repeat a few times a day.
  2. When your dog looks happy about it, lift the flap and look inside, then treat.
  3. Next, touch the bottle to the ear without squeezing, then treat.
  4. Then add a small amount of cleaner and a brief massage, followed by a jackpot of treats.

Move to the next stage only when your dog is relaxed at the current one. For a dog that's had a painful ear in the past, this can take a couple of weeks. If your dog growls or snaps, stop and ask your vet or a qualified trainer for help.

Use a lick mat and great treats

A [[amazon-search:lick-mat|lick mat]] spread with dog-safe peanut butter or wet food keeps many dogs busy and still while you work. Stick it to a wall or tub at nose height, or have a helper hold it. Small, soft [[amazon-search:treats|training treats]] are easy to dole out quickly.

Small details that help

  • Warm the bottle in your hands or in a cup of warm water for a few minutes. Test it on your wrist.
  • Pick the same calm time each week, such as after the Sunday walk, so it becomes predictable.
  • Keep a [[amazon-search:dog-towel|microfiber towel]] ready for the shake.
  • End every session with something fun.

Adjusting your dog ear care routine through the seasons

Even a perfect schedule needs tweaking as the year changes. Plan for these shifts so your dog ear maintenance doesn't fall behind.

  • Spring: pollen season often brings allergy flare-ups. Increase ear checks for allergy-prone dogs and watch for itchy paws and faces too.
  • Summer: swimming, humidity and grass awns are the big three. Dry ears after every swim and check both ears after walks through tall grass. Sudden, violent head shaking in one ear needs a vet.
  • Fall: some outdoor allergens peak again, and leaf litter brings dirt and debris. Keep up weekly checks.
  • Winter: indoor heating can dry skin, and dust mites may bother allergic dogs. Many dogs can return to their baseline cleaning interval.

Life changes matter as well. A new grooming style, a move near water, a diet change or a new allergy diagnosis are all good reasons to revisit how often you clean your dog's ears and rerun the schedule calculator.

Keep a simple ear log

A note on your phone is enough. Record the date of each check and cleaning, plus a word or two on what you saw: "clean," "light wax," "bit red, left ear." Over a few months, the log shows whether your interval is right, and it gives your vet useful history if a problem appears.

Common dog ear cleaning routine mistakes

  • Cleaning daily "just in case." Healthy ears don't need it, and it can irritate them.
  • Skipping the check because it isn't a cleaning week. The check is the most valuable part.
  • Pushing cotton swabs into the canal. Wipe only what you can see.
  • Using alcohol, hydrogen peroxide or vinegar. They can sting and irritate. Use a dog ear cleaner instead.
  • Cleaning a red, painful or smelly ear instead of calling the vet. And don't clean right before an appointment, since your vet may need to sample the discharge.
  • Never adjusting. Seasons, age and health change. Your routine should too.

Building a dog ear cleaning routine that lasts

The best dog ear cleaning routine is the one you'll actually keep: a quick weekly check, cleanings matched to your dog's ears, drying after water, and plenty of treats.
