Dog ear infection signs are easy to miss at first: a little extra scratching, a bit more head shaking, a faint smell you notice when your dog curls up next to you. Caught early, most outer ear infections respond well to treatment. Left alone, they can become painful, chronic, and occasionally serious. This guide explains the symptoms to watch for, what causes them, how yeast, bacteria and ear mites differ, and exactly when to call your vet.

Key takeaways

  • The most common dog ear infection symptoms are scratching, head shaking, redness, odor, discharge and pain when the ear is touched.
  • Head tilt, loss of balance, circling, abnormal eye movements or a drooping face are emergency signs that need a vet the same day.
  • Smell and discharge give clues about yeast vs bacteria vs mites, but only ear cytology confirms the cause.
  • Don't clean your dog's ears right before a vet visit. It can wash away the evidence your vet needs.
  • Ear cleaners help prevent problems. They don't cure an established infection.

Common dog ear infection signs

Most dog ear infections start in the outer ear canal, a condition vets call otitis externa. These are the dog ear infection signs owners notice most often.

Scratching and rubbing

Itching is usually the first sign. Your dog may paw at one or both ears, rub the side of the face along the carpet or couch, or whine while scratching. Scratching can leave scabs, hair loss or raw skin around the ear.

Head shaking

Dogs shake their heads to clear water or debris, but frequent or violent shaking signals discomfort. Sudden, intense shaking of one ear after a walk can point to a grass awn (foxtail) in the canal rather than an infection.

Repeated hard shaking can also burst small blood vessels in the ear flap, causing an aural hematoma: a soft, puffy, fluid-filled swelling that needs veterinary care.

Redness and swelling

Healthy inner ear skin is pale pink. Inflamed skin looks red, feels warm and may swell enough to narrow the canal opening. Long-term inflammation can make the skin thick and darker.

Odor

A healthy ear smells faintly or not at all. A strong yeasty, musty, sour or rotten smell is one of the most reliable dog ear infection symptoms. Many owners smell the problem before they see it.

Discharge or excess wax

Brown, black, yellow, green or bloody material in the ear is not normal wax buildup. Discharge that comes back within a day or two of cleaning is a strong sign that something is driving the problem.

Pain

A dog with a sore ear may yelp, flinch, pull away or even snap when you touch the ear or head. Some dogs hold the painful ear lower, tilt the head slightly, or avoid chewing hard food. Of all the dog ear infection signs, pain is the one that should always send you to a vet.

Behavior changes

Ear pain can make dogs irritable, restless at night, less playful or reluctant to be petted on the head. These subtle dog ear infection signs are easy to blame on mood, so check the ears whenever behavior shifts.

How to check your dog for ear infection signs at home

A 30-second weekly check is the best way to catch dog ear infection signs before they turn into a painful problem. Do it in good light, ideally when your dog is relaxed.

  1. Look. Lift each ear flap and compare the two sides. Note the skin color, any swelling, and the color and amount of wax.
  2. Sniff. Get to know what your dog's healthy ears smell like, so a change stands out.
  3. Touch. Gently massage the base of each ear. A healthy dog usually enjoys this. Flinching, groaning or pulling away suggests pain.
  4. Watch. Over the next day, notice scratching, head shaking or head rubbing.

One ear or both ears?

The pattern is a useful clue for your vet. Symptoms in one ear only, especially if they start suddenly, raise suspicion of a foreign body such as a grass awn, a growth, or a local injury. Both ears affected, particularly when it keeps happening, more often points to an underlying cause like allergies or ear mites.

Recurring ear infections

If your dog gets ear infections several times a year, the ears are telling you something deeper is going on. Repeated inflammation can permanently thicken and narrow the ear canal, which traps more debris and makes infections harder to treat. In severe end-stage cases, surgery may become the only option. Ask your vet about an allergy workup and a long-term maintenance plan rather than treating each flare-up on its own.

Middle ear infections most often develop when an outer ear infection goes on for a long time or keeps coming back, damaging the eardrum. That's one reason prompt treatment of simple outer ear infections matters.

Emergency dog ear infection signs

Go to a vet the same day if your dog has: a head tilt; stumbling, falling or circling; rapid side-to-side or up-and-down eye movements (nystagmus); a drooping lip, ear or eyelid, or trouble blinking on one side; a small pupil or visible third eyelid on one side; sudden hearing loss; vomiting with balance problems; severe pain; or a swollen, fluid-filled ear flap.

Facial nerve signs and eye changes happen because nerves that control the face and eye run close to the middle ear. Balance problems point to the inner ear's vestibular system. Some of these signs can also come from conditions unrelated to the ear, such as idiopathic vestibular disease in older dogs, which is another reason for a prompt exam.

What causes ear infections in dogs?

Knowing the cause behind your dog's ear infection signs matters as much as treating them. Vets often think about ear disease in layers: a primary cause that starts the problem, secondary invaders like yeast and bacteria that take advantage, and factors such as ear shape and moisture that keep it going. Treating only the yeast or bacteria without addressing the primary cause is a common reason infections keep returning.

Allergies

Allergies are among the most common underlying causes of recurring ear infections. Both environmental allergies (atopic dermatitis) and food allergies inflame the ear canal skin, which changes the environment and lets yeast and bacteria overgrow. Red, itchy ears may be the first or only allergy sign some dogs show. Look for paw licking, face rubbing and itchy armpits or belly as well.

Moisture

Water trapped in the canal after swimming or bathing softens the skin and creates a warm, damp home for microbes. Our after-bath ear care guide explains how to dry ears properly.

Foreign bodies

Grass awns and foxtails are notorious for working their way down the ear canal during summer walks. Signs usually start suddenly and affect one ear: violent shaking, pawing and a tilted head. Don't try to remove one yourself. Your vet can retrieve it with an otoscope and special instruments.

Ear mites

Ear mites (Otodectes cynotis) are tiny parasites that are especially common in puppies, shelter dogs and homes with cats. They cause intense itching and spread between pets.

Ear anatomy and breed

All dogs have a long, L-shaped ear canal, but heavy, floppy ears, narrow canals and hairy canals all reduce airflow and trap debris. Spaniels, Basset Hounds, retrievers, Poodles and Shar-Peis are among the breeds known for ear trouble. See our guide to floppy-ear breeds for prevention tips.

Other causes

Hormonal conditions such as hypothyroidism, growths or polyps in the canal, and over-cleaning or harsh products can also trigger or worsen infections.

Yeast vs bacterial ear infection in dogs

Owners often search for the difference between a yeast and a bacterial ear infection in their dog, and between ear mites and an ear infection. Smell and discharge offer clues, but they overlap, and mixed infections are common.

The treatments differ: antifungals for yeast, antibiotics for bacteria and antiparasitic products for mites. Guessing wrong can delay healing, which is why your vet will want to look under the microscope.

How vets diagnose dog ear infections

When you bring in a dog showing ear infection signs, the visit usually follows a predictable pattern.

Otoscope exam

Your vet uses an otoscope, a lighted, magnifying cone, to look down the canal. They check for redness, swelling, discharge, foreign bodies, growths and whether the eardrum is intact. If the ear is too painful or packed with debris, sedation may be needed for a proper look.

Ear cytology

Cytology is the key test. The vet rolls a swab of ear material onto a slide, stains it, and examines it under the microscope to count yeast and bacteria and identify their shape. Mites and their eggs can be seen with a separate check of the debris. This guides which medication to use.

Culture and further testing

For chronic or severe infections, or when rod-shaped bacteria show up, your vet may send a sample for culture and sensitivity to find an antibiotic that works. Suspected middle ear disease may need imaging such as a CT scan. Recurrent infections often lead to an allergy workup.

Don't clean before the appointment. If you suspect an infection, skip cleaning for at least a day or two beforehand unless your vet says otherwise. Cleaning removes or dilutes the discharge your vet needs for cytology, which can make the results less reliable, and some cleaners leave residue on the slide. It also makes it harder for your vet to judge how bad the ear really is.

Typical treatment

Most outer ear infections are treated with prescription ear drops that combine an antifungal, an antibiotic and an anti-inflammatory steroid. Some vets use long-acting gels applied in the clinic. Painful or severe cases may also need oral medication. Follow the full course and go back for the recheck. Stopping early is a common reason infections return.

Dog ear cleaners and infections: prevention, not cure

A good dog ear cleaner removes wax and debris, helps keep the canal dry, and makes it harder for yeast and bacteria to build up. That makes it a useful prevention tool, especially for floppy-eared dogs, swimmers and allergy-prone dogs. Learn the safe technique in our guide on how to clean dog ears.

What a cleaner can't do is cure an established infection. When owners search for an ear cleaner for dogs with an infection, the honest answer is that the right product depends on your vet's diagnosis. Vets often prescribe or recommend a specific cleaner to use alongside medication, chosen for what's growing in the ear and whether the eardrum is intact.

How to prevent ear infections in dogs

  • Check ears weekly. Look, sniff and touch. Early dog ear infection signs are easier to treat.
  • Clean on a schedule that fits your dog, not too often and not too rarely. Our dog ear cleaning routines guide has templates by dog type.
  • Dry ears after swimming and baths. Pat the outer ear dry with a towel or [[amazon-search:cotton-rounds|soft cotton rounds]], and ask your vet whether a drying cleaner suits your dog.
  • Manage allergies with your vet if ear problems keep returning.
  • Keep hair around the ear opening trimmed and check ears after walks in tall grass.
  • Finish every prescribed treatment and attend rechecks.

The bottom line on dog ear infection signs

Scratching, head shaking, redness, odor, discharge and pain are the classic dog ear infection signs. Balance problems, head tilt and facial changes are emergencies. Smell and discharge can hint at yeast, bacteria or mites, but a vet's otoscope exam and cytology are what get your dog the right treatment.
